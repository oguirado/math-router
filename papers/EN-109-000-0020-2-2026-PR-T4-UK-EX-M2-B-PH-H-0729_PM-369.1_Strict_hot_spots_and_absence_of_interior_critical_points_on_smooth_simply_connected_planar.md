## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We prove the strict hot spots conjecture for smooth bounded simply connected planar domains. More precisely, every nonzero eigenfunction for the first positive Neumann eigenvalue has nonvanishing gradient in the interior, so all its global maxima and minima lie on the boundary. This holds even when the eigenvalue is multiple.

- **C1** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): For every $0\ne u\in\mathcal V$, $$\nabla u(x)\ne0\qquad(x\in\Omega).$$ In particular, $$\label{eq:hotspots} \min_{y\in\partial\Omega}u(y)<u(x)<\max_{y\in\partial\Omega}u(y) \qquad(x\in\Omega).$$
  - Explanation: Every nonzero first-positive Neumann eigenfunction has no interior critical point and has strict boundary-only extrema.
- **C2** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-gap)): One has $\mu<\lambda_D$.
  - Explanation: The strict spectral separation supplies coercivity for the Dirichlet problems used in the proof.
- **C3** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-conformal)): There is a conformal bijection $\Phi:\mathbb D\to\Omega$ extending to a smooth diffeomorphism of closures, with $\Phi'\ne0$ on $\overline\mathbb D$.
  - Explanation: The conformal parametrization transfers the smooth simply connected planar domain to the disk without boundary degeneration.
- **C4** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-boundary-weight)): There is a fixed smooth positive function $\beta$ on $\mathbb S^1$ such that every $v\in\mathcal V$ has boundary trace $h_v=v\circ\Phi$ satisfying $$\int_{\mathbb S^1}\beta h_v\,\mathrm ds=0.$$ If $v\ne0$, its boundary trace is nonzero and takes both signs.
  - Explanation: A positive boundary weight transfers the mean-zero condition and forces nonzero traces to take both signs.
- **C5** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-nodal)): For $0\ne v\in\mathcal V$, the sets $\{v>0\}$ and $\{v<0\}$ are connected. Its boundary trace cannot have four cyclically ordered points with strictly alternating signs.
  - Explanation: Connected sign sets prohibit strict alternating boundary signs by planar separation.
- **C6** (bound, [source](#stmt-prop-multiplicity)): The real eigenspace $\mathcal V$ has dimension at most two.
  - Explanation: The first-positive real Neumann eigenspace has dimension at most two.
- **C7** (bound, [source](#stmt-prop-vector)): For every such $X$, $$\label{eq:vector-inequality} \mathcal Q(X)\ge\mu\int_\Omega\|X\|^2,$$ with equality exactly when $X=\nabla v$ for some $v\in\mathcal V$.
  - Explanation: Tangent divergence–curl energy has an exact equality space consisting of first-positive eigenfunction gradients.
- **C8** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-curvature)): For tangent $X\in H^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^2)$, $$\label{eq:curvature} \mathcal Q(X)=\int_\Omega\|\nabla X\|^2 +\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\|X\|^2.$$
  - Explanation: The tangent vector energy separates into a bulk Dirichlet term and a boundary curvature term.
- **C9** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-green-operator)): The operator $$Tf(x)=\int_\mathbb DG(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm dq(y)$$ is a bounded self-adjoint operator on $L^2(q)$ with $\lVert T\rVert\le d<1$. The same statement holds with $q$ replaced by any measure $0\le\rho\le q$.
  - Explanation: The weighted Green operator is a self-adjoint contraction, including under smaller nonnegative measures.
- **C10** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-kernel-estimates)): The kernels above are finite at the stated points. For fixed $p$, $K(p,\cdot)$ is positive and bounded. The kernel $R$ is symmetric, nonnegative, and integrable on $\mathbb S^1\times\mathbb S^1$, with $$\label{eq:R-integral} \iint R(s,t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt\le\frac{q(\mathbb D)}{1-d}.$$ For Lipschitz boundary data, $$\begin{aligned} W_h(p)&=\int_{\mathbb S^1}K(p,s)h(s)\,\mathrm ds,\label{eq:K-representation}\\ \langle Ah,(I-T)^{-1}Ak\rangle_q &=\iint R(s,t)h(s)\cdot k(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt.\label{eq:R-representation} \end{aligned}$$
  - Explanation: The positive kernels admit finite evaluations and integral representations for Lipschitz boundary data.
- **C11** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-boundary-energy)): For tangent Lipschitz data $h,k$, $$\begin{aligned} E(h,k)={}&\frac12\iint N_0(s,t) (h(s)-h(t))\cdot(k(s)-k(t))\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt\notag\\ &-\iint R(s,t)h(s)\cdot k(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt +\int_{\mathbb S^1}\|\Phi'\|(\kappa\circ\Phi)h\cdot k\,\mathrm ds. \label{eq:E-boundary} \end{aligned}$$
  - Explanation: Boundary differences, potential interactions and curvature determine the tangent energy form.
- **C12** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-multiplier)): For every real Lipschitz function $b$ on $\mathbb S^1$, $$\label{eq:multiplier} E(bg)=\frac12\iint N(s,t)(b(s)-b(t))^2 g(s)\cdot g(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt.$$
  - Explanation: A scalar boundary multiplier produces a kernel-weighted squared-difference energy identity.
- **C13** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-negative-kernel)): Let $\,\mathrm dq=a\,\mathrm dx$ on $\mathbb D$, where $a\in L^\infty(\mathbb D)$ is nonnegative, and suppose there is a constant $0<d<1$ such that $$\int_\mathbb D\|\psi\|^2\,\mathrm dq\le d\int_\mathbb D\|\nabla\psi\|^2 \qquad(\psi\in H^1_0(\mathbb D)).$$ For the associated kernels $K,N$ and every $p\in\mathbb D$, the kernel $$\label{eq:D} D_p(s,t)=\frac{K(p,s)K(p,t)}{N(s,t)}\quad(s\ne t), \qquad D_p(s,s)=0,$$ is conditionally negative semidefinite on $\mathbb S^1$.
  - Explanation: The quotient of interior evaluation kernels by the boundary interaction kernel is conditionally negative semidefinite.
- **C14** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-regularization)): There are bounded continuous strictly positive kernels $C_\epsilon$ on $\mathbb D\times\mathbb D$, $0<\epsilon\le1$, such that $C_\epsilon\uparrow G$ as $\epsilon\downarrow0$, including on the diagonal, and every finite matrix $(1/C_\epsilon(x_i,x_j))_{i,j}$ has <span class="upright">(P)</span>.
  - Explanation: Bounded regularizations approach the singular Green kernel while preserving the reciprocal inertia condition.
- **C15** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-update)): Let $M$ be a real symmetric finite matrix with strictly positive entries such that its reciprocal has <span class="upright">(P)</span>. For any index $z$ and any $c\ge0$, the matrix $$\widetilde M_{ij}=M_{ij}+cM_{iz}M_{zj}$$ also has a reciprocal with <span class="upright">(P)</span>.
  - Explanation: An update along a current matrix column preserves the reciprocal inertia condition.
- **C16** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-interior-reciprocal)): For every finite set of interior points, the reciprocal matrix of $M^\rho$, with diagonal zero, has <span class="upright">(P)</span>.
  - Explanation: Positive quadratures and monotone limits propagate the reciprocal inertia condition to the interior resolvent.
- **C17** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-bordered)): For distinct $s_1,\ldots,s_m\in\mathbb S^1$ and $p\in\mathbb D$, the matrix $$\label{eq:bordered} \begin{pmatrix}B&\ell\\\ell^T&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B_{ij}=\begin{cases}1/N(s_i,s_j),&i\ne j,\\0,&i=j,\end{cases} \qquad \ell_i=1/K(p,s_i),$$ has <span class="upright">(P)</span>.
  - Explanation: The boundary reciprocal matrix bordered by an interior evaluation satisfies the reciprocal inertia condition.
- **C18** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-hilbert)): For every $p\in\mathbb D$ there is an injective Lipschitz map $b:\mathbb S^1\to\mathcal H$, where $\mathcal H$ is a separable real Hilbert space, such that $$\label{eq:hilbert-distance} \lVert b(s)-b(t)\rVert_\mathcal H^2=D_p(s,t).$$
  - Explanation: The distance kernel admits an injective Lipschitz realization in a separable real Hilbert space.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Divergence–curl energy** ([source](#sec:vector)): For a real vector field $X\in H^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^2)$ with tangent trace, meaning $\operatorname{tr}X\cdot\nu=0$, set $$\mathcal Q(X)=\int_\Omega\bigl(\|\operatorname{div}X\|^2+\|\operatorname{curl}X\|^2\bigr), \qquad \operatorname{curl}X=\partial_1X_2-\partial_2X_1.$$
- **Boundary curvature** ([source](#sec:vector)): Let $\tau$ be the counterclockwise unit tangent to $\partial\Omega$, and put $$\kappa=\det(\tau,\partial_\tau\tau).$$
- **Conformal potential measure** ([source](#eq:q)): The conformal map of [lem:conformal](#lem:conformal) defines a measure $$\label{eq:q} \,\mathrm dq(x)=\mu\|\Phi'(x)\|^2\,\mathrm dx.$$
- **Harmonic extension** ([source](#eq:extension)): For Lipschitz scalar or vector boundary data $h$, let $$Ah(x)=\int_{\mathbb S^1}P_s(x)h(s)\,\mathrm ds.$$
- **Positive boundary kernels** ([source](#eq:K)): For $p\in\mathbb D$ and distinct $s,t\in\mathbb S^1$, define $$\begin{aligned} K(p,s)&=P_s(p)+\sum_{n\ge1}(T^nP_s)(p),\label{eq:K}\\ R(s,t)&=\sum_{n\ge0}\int_\mathbb DP_s\,T^nP_t\,\mathrm dq,\label{eq:R}\\ N(s,t)&=N_0(s,t)+R(s,t),\qquad N_0(s,t)=\frac1{\pi\|s-t\|^2}.\label{eq:N} \end{aligned}$$
- **Conditional negative semidefiniteness** ([source](#sec:reciprocal)): A real symmetric kernel $D$ with zero diagonal is *conditionally negative semidefinite* if, for every finite family of points and real coefficients with $\sum_i a_i=0$, $$\sum_{i,j}a_i a_jD(s_i,s_j)\le0.$$
- **Reciprocal inertia property** ([source](#sec:reciprocal)): Say that a real symmetric matrix has property <span class="upright">(P)</span> if it has at most one positive eigenvalue, counted with multiplicity.
- **Interior resolvent kernel** ([source](#eq:M-rho)): For $0\le\rho\le q$ supported on a compact subset of $\mathbb D$, let $T_\rho$ be the Green operator on $L^2(\rho)$ and define $$\label{eq:M-rho} M^\rho(x,y)=G(x,y) +\langle G(x,\cdot),(I-T_\rho)^{-1}G(y,\cdot)\rangle_\rho.$$
- **Planar domain** ([source](#pm-h-2)): Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a nonempty bounded simply connected open set with $C^\infty$ boundary.
- **First-positive Neumann eigenvalue and eigenspace** ([source](#pm-h-2)): Set $$\label{eq:mu} \mu=\mu_1(\Omega) =\inf_{\substack{0\ne v\in H^1(\Omega)\\ \int_\Omega v=0}} \frac{\int_\Omega\|\nabla v\|^2}{\int_\Omega\|v\|^2},$$ and let $\mathcal V$ be the real Neumann eigenspace for $\mu$.
- **Disk and circle** ([source](#pm-h-4)): Write $\mathbb D=\{z\in\mathbb C:\|z\|<1\}$ and $\mathbb S^1=\partial\mathbb D$, and let $\Phi:\mathbb D\to\Omega$ be a smooth conformal parametrization.

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I-main**: Every nonzero first-positive Neumann eigenfunction has nonvanishing interior gradient.
- **I-update**: A nonnegative update along a column preserves the entrywise reciprocal condition of at most one positive eigenvalue.
- **I-kernel**: The boundary kernel is conditionally negative semidefinite.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **A-holes**: Apply the interior gradient conclusion on domains with holes. -> The stated topological hypothesis fails and counterexamples are reported.
- **A-mode**: Apply the conclusion to arbitrary Neumann eigenmodes. -> Membership in the first positive eigenspace is unproved.
- **A-inverse**: Use a matrix inverse instead of entrywise reciprocals. -> The inertia assertion concerns a different matrix.
- **A-certificate**: Treat finite update tests as a certificate of the analytic kernel or hot-spots theorem. -> Finite tests do not justify the quadrature, regularization, boundary and cutoff limits.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `constructive`. The finite column update preserves the reciprocal inertia condition. This implements only the finite matrix lemma, not the continuum kernel theorem or the hot-spots conclusion.

```python
# INPUTS: JSON list of rational matrix/update records M,z,c; at most 64 records.
# PRECONDITIONS: symmetric strictly positive M, reciprocal with at most one positive eigenvalue; valid z; c >= 0; order 1 to 3.
# OUTPUTS: updated rational matrices, or REJECT for an invalid record.
# TIME: O(B 2^n n^3) exact rational operations, n <= 3, B <= 64.
# SPACE: O(B n^2) rational entries.
# ARITHMETIC: exact python-flint fmpq and exact determinants.
# FAILURE: malformed JSON aborts; failed mathematical preconditions return REJECT.
import json,sys,itertools
from flint import fmpq,fmpq_mat
def apply(r):
    try:
        M=[[fmpq(str(x)) for x in row] for row in r['M']]
        n=len(M); z=r['z']; c=fmpq(str(r['c']))
        if not 1 <= n <= 3 or any(len(row)!=n for row in M): return 'REJECT'
        if type(z) is not int or not 0 <= z < n or c < 0: return 'REJECT'
        if any(M[i][j]<=0 or M[i][j]!=M[j][i] for i in range(n) for j in range(n)): return 'REJECT'
        a=M[z][z]
        U=[[a-M[i][z]*M[z][j]/M[i][j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
        for size in range(1,n+1):
            for S in itertools.combinations(range(n),size):
                if fmpq_mat([[U[i][j] for j in S] for i in S]).det()<0: return 'REJECT'
        A=[[M[i][j]+c*M[i][z]*M[z][j] for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
        return [[str(x) for x in row] for row in A]
    except (ValueError,TypeError,KeyError,ZeroDivisionError): return 'REJECT'
batch=json.load(sys.stdin)
if type(batch) is not list or len(batch)>64: raise ValueError('batch bound')
print(json.dumps([apply(r) for r in batch],separators=(',',':')))
```

## 6. Applicability limits and search

- **L1** (hypothesis): The hot-spots conclusion requires a nonempty bounded simply connected planar open set with smooth boundary. Blocked use: ['Applying the conclusion to unbounded domains, higher-dimensional domains, or nonsmooth boundaries.']
- **L2** (counterexample_scope): Simple connectivity cannot be removed on the basis of this result: the background describes counterexamples in planar domains with holes. Blocked use: ['Extending boundary-only extrema to arbitrary multiply connected planar domains.']
- **L3** (hypothesis): The conclusion concerns every nonzero member of the first positive Neumann eigenspace; it does not assert the same property for other spectral problems or higher modes. Blocked use: ['Applying the result to Dirichlet eigenfunctions, higher Neumann eigenmodes, or arbitrary functions.']
- **L4** (hypothesis): The independent disk-kernel theorem requires a bounded nonnegative potential and a strict subcritical Dirichlet inequality. Blocked use: ['Using the kernel conclusion for signed or unbounded potentials, or without a strict subcriticality proof.']
- **L5** (edge_case): The singular Green function has infinite diagonal values, so direct quadrature at integration nodes requires the stated regularization. Blocked use: ['Treating the singular diagonal as a finite kernel entry in direct quadrature.']
- **L6** (edge_case): The reciprocal operations in the matrix argument are entrywise. Blocked use: ['Replacing entrywise reciprocals by matrix inverses.']
- **L7** (hypothesis): The finite update is restricted to symmetric matrices with strictly positive entries whose entrywise reciprocal has the stated inertia property, and to updates along a current matrix column with nonnegative coefficient. Blocked use: ['Applying the update argument to arbitrary rank-one directions, negative coefficients, or matrices without the reciprocal inertia hypothesis.']
- **L8** (edge_case): An individual Poisson section need not belong to the weighted square-integrable space; the proof smooths it by Green integration before using the contraction estimate. Blocked use: ['Applying the weighted square-integrable operator directly to an unsmoothed Poisson section without establishing membership.']
- **L9** (approximation): The boundary limit is taken while the potential cutoff still has compact interior support. Blocked use: ['Removing the compact-support cutoff before the boundary limit on the strength of this proof.']
- **L10** (nonconstructive): The continuum kernel argument passes through quadrature, regularization, boundary, and cutoff limits. A finite matrix check alone does not establish those analytic passages or the hot-spots theorem. Blocked use: ['Presenting a finite resolvent-update certificate as a certificate of the full continuum theorem.']
- **L11** (unknown): Pointwise nonvanishing does not supply a uniform positive gradient margin or a discretization error bound. Such quantitative guarantees are ABSENT from this conclusion. Blocked use: ['Treating a sampled near-zero gradient as an exact critical point, or assuming an unstated uniform numerical margin.']
- **L12** (nonconstructive): The Hilbert-space realization is separable; a finite-dimensional realization or effective truncation bound is not asserted. Blocked use: ['Replacing the Hilbert realization by finitely many coordinates without a separate error argument.']

```lean
namespace StrictHotSpots

abbrev Plane := EuclideanSpace ℝ (Fin 2)

def SmoothBoundary (Ω : Set Plane) : Prop :=
  ∀ p ∈ frontier Ω, ∃ (U : Set Plane) (ρ : Plane → ℝ),
    IsOpen U ∧ p ∈ U ∧ ContDiffOn ℝ ∞ ρ U ∧
    fderiv ℝ ρ p ≠ 0 ∧ ρ p = 0 ∧
    Ω ∩ U = {x | ρ x < 0} ∩ U

def AdmissibleDomain (Ω : Set Plane) : Prop :=
  Ω.Nonempty ∧ IsOpen Ω ∧ Bornology.IsBounded Ω ∧
    IsSimplyConnected Ω ∧ SmoothBoundary Ω

def HasH1Gradient (Ω : Set Plane) (v : Plane → ℝ) (g : Plane → Plane) : Prop :=
  MemLp v 2 (volume.restrict Ω) ∧ MemLp g 2 (volume.restrict Ω) ∧
  ∀ φ : Plane → ℝ, ContDiff ℝ ∞ φ → HasCompactSupport φ → tsupport φ ⊆ Ω →
    ∀ e : Plane,
      (∫ x in Ω, v x * (fderiv ℝ φ x) e) =
        -(∫ x in Ω, (inner ℝ (g x) e) * φ x)

def rayleighValues (Ω : Set Plane) : Set ℝ :=
  {r | ∃ (v : Plane → ℝ) (g : Plane → Plane),

    0 < (∫ x in Ω, (v x) ^ 2) ∧
```

## 7. Complete mathematical paper


We prove the strict hot spots conjecture for smooth bounded simply connected planar domains. More precisely, every nonzero eigenfunction for the first positive Neumann eigenvalue has nonvanishing gradient in the interior, so all its global maxima and minima lie on the boundary. This holds even when the eigenvalue is multiple.

### Introduction

The hot-spots problem arose from Rauch’s discussion of the long-time behavior of heat flow with insulating boundary conditions (Rauch 1975) <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[Rauch1975](#ref-Rauch1975)]. Its spectral formulation asks whether the extrema of a first-positive Neumann eigenfunction occur on the boundary. Bañuelos and Burdzy distinguished strict and nonstrict formulations, and assertions about every eigenfunction from the existence of one favorable eigenfunction (Bañuelos and Burdzy 1999) <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[BanuelosBurdzy1999](#ref-BanuelosBurdzy1999)]. These distinctions matter when the eigenvalue is multiple.

Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a nonempty bounded simply connected open set with $C^\infty$ boundary. In particular, $\Omega$ is connected. Set <a id="eq:mu"></a>$$\label{eq:mu}
\mu=\mu_1(\Omega)
=\inf_{\substack{0\ne v\in H^1(\Omega)\\ \int_\Omega v=0}}
\frac{\int_\Omega|\nabla v|^2}{\int_\Omega|v|^2},$$ and let $\mathcal V$ be the real Neumann eigenspace for $\mu$. We count only positive eigenvalues in this notation; sources that count the constant eigenfunction first write $\mu_2$ for this same eigenvalue.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

<a id="eq:hotspots"></a>
**Theorem 1**. *For every $0\ne u\in\mathcal V$, $$\nabla u(x)\ne0\qquad(x\in\Omega).$$ In particular, $$\label{eq:hotspots}
\min_{y\in\partial\Omega}u(y)<u(x)<\max_{y\in\partial\Omega}u(y)
\qquad(x\in\Omega).$$*

</div>

The theorem imposes no convexity or symmetry condition. It applies to every member of the first positive eigenspace, including when the eigenvalue is multiple. The nonvanishing-gradient conclusion is stronger than <a href="#eq:hotspots" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:hotspots">[eq:hotspots]</a>, since it also rules out interior saddle points.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
###### Background and methods.

Topology matters in the hot-spots problem. Burdzy and Werner constructed a planar counterexample with two holes and a simple first-positive eigenvalue (Burdzy and Werner 1999) <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[BurdzyWerner1999](#ref-BurdzyWerner1999)]. Bass and Burdzy obtained examples with both extrema strictly in the interior (Bass and Burdzy 2000) <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[BassBurdzy2000](#ref-BassBurdzy2000)], and Burdzy subsequently obtained this behavior with just one hole (Burdzy 2005) <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[Burdzy2005](#ref-Burdzy2005)]. The simply connected planar conjecture is stated explicitly in (Burdzy 2005, Conjecture 1.2(ii)) <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[Burdzy2005](#ref-Burdzy2005)].

Important geometric special cases already establish strong gradient conclusions. Jerison and Nadirashvili proved a positive directional derivative for every first-positive eigenfunction on convex planar domains with two orthogonal reflection symmetries, allowing multiple eigenvalues (Jerison and Nadirashvili 2000, Theorem 1.4) <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[JerisonNadirashvili2000](#ref-JerisonNadirashvili2000)]. Probabilistic coupling methods developed by Bañuelos and Burdzy (Bañuelos and Burdzy 1999) <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[BanuelosBurdzy1999](#ref-BanuelosBurdzy1999)] led to further results. Pascu proved boundary-only extrema for antisymmetric eigenfunctions on $C^{1,\alpha}$ convex planar domains with one reflection symmetry, where $0<\alpha<1$ (Pascu 2002) <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[Pascu2002](#ref-Pascu2002)]. Atar and Burdzy treated every first-positive eigenfunction on lip domains, a class of Lipschitz domains bounded between graphs of functions with Lipschitz constants at most one (Atar and Burdzy 2004) <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[AtarBurdzy2004](#ref-AtarBurdzy2004)]. Miyamoto obtained interior critical-point exclusion under a spectral-diameter condition, giving nonsymmetric nearly circular convex examples (Miyamoto 2007, Lemma 1.2 and Theorem A) <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[Miyamoto2007](#ref-Miyamoto2007)].

The polygonal problem has a complementary development. Siudeja proved hot-spots results for a class of acute triangles (Siudeja 2015) <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[Siudeja2015](#ref-Siudeja2015)]. Judge and Mondal, with their subsequent erratum, excluded interior critical points for every first-positive Neumann eigenfunction on every Euclidean triangle (Judge and Mondal 2020, 2022) <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[JudgeMondal2020](#ref-JudgeMondal2020), [JudgeMondal2022](#ref-JudgeMondal2022)]. Chen, Gui and Yao established the finer classification of nonvertex critical points (Chen, Gui, and Yao 2026) <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[ChenGuiYao2026](#ref-ChenGuiYao2026)]. These results concern domains with corners; [thm:main](#thm:main) concerns the full smooth simply connected planar class.

Recent quantitative work measures how far the hot-spots conclusion can fail. For general bounded connected Lipschitz domains, de Dios Pont, Hsu and Taylor determined the sharp dimension-dependent upper bound for the ratio of a first-positive Neumann eigenfunction’s supremum over the domain to its maximum on the boundary (Dios Pont, Hsu, and Taylor 2025, Definition 1 and Theorem 4) <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[deDiosPontHsuTaylor2025](#ref-deDiosPontHsuTaylor2025)]. Deng, Jiang and Yang obtained quantitative bounds for convex domains in two-dimensional space forms (Deng, Jiang, and Yang 2026) <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[DengJiangYang2026](#ref-DengJiangYang2026)]. Such ratios concern extrema; excluding every interior critical point also requires excluding saddle points.

Our starting point is Rohleder’s variational principle for tangent vector fields (Rohleder 2021, 2024) <a id="pm-cite-17"></a>[[Rohleder2021](#ref-Rohleder2021), [Rohleder2024](#ref-Rohleder2024)]. In its lip-domain application, taking componentwise absolute values after a suitable rotation preserves the required tangent boundary condition and yields directional monotonicity. We use scalar boundary multipliers to keep tangency on an arbitrary smooth boundary, and construct those multipliers through a reciprocal Green-kernel theorem.

The difficulty is to control the gradient without a preferred direction in the domain. Each Cartesian derivative of an eigenfunction solves the same Helmholtz equation, but its boundary values need not have a fixed sign. The Neumann condition instead constrains the two derivatives together: their vector is tangent to the boundary. We use that constraint through a nonnegative quadratic form whose nullspace consists exactly of first-positive eigenfunction gradients. The remaining task is to construct enough scalar boundary multipliers that preserve this nullspace if an interior critical point exists.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
###### The mechanism.

Write $\mathbb D=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|<1\}$ and $\mathbb S^1=\partial\mathbb D$, and let $\Phi:\mathbb D\to\Omega$ be a smooth conformal parametrization. The proof begins with the variational principle for tangent vector fields associated with Neumann gradients. For a tangent vector field $X$, its divergence–curl energy is at least $\mu\lVert X\rVert_2^2$, with equality exactly for gradients of functions in $\mathcal V$. After this conformal change of variables, solving the scalar Helmholtz equation in each Cartesian component gives a nonnegative quadratic form $E$ on boundary vector data. If $g$ is the boundary gradient of an eigenfunction, then $E(g)=0$, and a scalar multiplier $b$ satisfies $$E(bg)=\frac12\iint_{\mathbb S^1\times\mathbb S^1}
N(s,t)(b(s)-b(t))^2g(s)\cdot g(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt.$$ Here $N$ is a positive boundary kernel, and the corresponding interior evaluation kernel is denoted by $K$.

The sign of $g(s)\cdot g(t)$ prevents a direct positivity argument in this identity. The main kernel result supplies a family of multipliers whose squared differences remove the factor $N$. For every interior point $p$, $$D_p(s,t)=\frac{K(p,s)K(p,t)}{N(s,t)}\quad(s\ne t),
\qquad D_p(s,s)=0,$$ is conditionally negative semidefinite. It is therefore the squared distance of an injective Lipschitz map from the circle to a real Hilbert space. Applying the preceding energy identity to its coordinates gives $$\sum_j E(b_jg)=\frac12|\nabla u(\Phi(p))|^2.$$ A critical point would thus produce too many eigenfunction gradients, contradicting the planar multiplicity bound or boundary uniqueness.

The construction of $D_p$ is the central analytic step. We first regularize the disk Green function so that the entrywise reciprocal of every finite kernel matrix has at most one positive eigenvalue. This property survives successive rank-one updates along columns of the current kernel matrix, which sum the Green resolvent over a finite set of integration nodes. Positive quadrature and monotone limits then pass from those matrices to the singular Green kernel and finally to its boundary kernels. Taking the boundary limit before removing a compact-support cutoff avoids treating a Poisson kernel as an $L^2$ function when it need not be one.

The kernel theorem, [thm:negative-kernel](#thm:negative-kernel), holds for every bounded nonnegative disk potential satisfying the subcritical Dirichlet inequality <a href="#eq:subcritical" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subcritical">[eq:subcritical]</a>, independently of the conformal origin of that potential. The reciprocal-matrix condition comes from the McCullough–Quiggin characterization of complete Nevanlinna–Pick kernels (McCullough 1992, 1994; P. Quiggin 1993; P. P. Quiggin 1994) <a id="pm-cite-18"></a>[[McCullough1992](#ref-McCullough1992), [McCullough1994](#ref-McCullough1994), [Quiggin1993](#ref-Quiggin1993), [Quiggin1994](#ref-Quiggin1994)]; see also (Agler and McCarthy 2000, Corollary 1.12) <a id="pm-cite-19"></a>[[AglerMcCarthy2000](#ref-AglerMcCarthy2000)]. Schoenberg’s squared-distance correspondence supplies the Hilbert-space realization (Schoenberg 1938) <a id="pm-cite-20"></a>[[Schoenberg1938](#ref-Schoenberg1938)]. The analytic work here concerns the singular Green kernel, its positive-potential resolvent, and its boundary limit; the resulting theorem can be used independently of the eigenfunction argument.

All specialized ingredients used in the proof are established below. The smooth-boundary regularity input is (Showalter 1994, chap. III, Theorems 6.1, 6.4 and 6.5) <a id="pm-cite-21"></a>[[Showalter1994](#ref-Showalter1994)]: $L^2$ Poisson data give $H^2$ solutions under homogeneous Dirichlet or Neumann conditions, and higher regularity follows with smoother data. Subtracting a smooth extension handles the inhomogeneous Dirichlet data used below. Bootstrap and Sobolev embedding make weak Neumann eigenfunctions smooth up to the boundary. Interior solutions of $(\Delta+\mu)v=0$ are analytic; alternatively, $e^{\sqrt\mu t}v(x)$ is harmonic in one additional variable.

### Spectral and planar preliminaries

Write $\lambda_D$ for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of $\Omega$. Both $\mu$ and $\lambda_D$ are positive by the respective Poincaré inequalities. Their Rayleigh infima are attained by compactness, and the eigenspaces are finite-dimensional.

The strict separation below is the first-mode case of the Friedlander–Filonov comparison (Friedlander 1991; Filonov 2005) <a id="pm-cite-22"></a>[[Friedlander1991](#ref-Friedlander1991), [Filonov2005](#ref-Filonov2005)]. We give the plane-wave argument of Filonov in the form needed here, including the strictness that identifies the equality space of the later vector variational principle.

<a id="stmt-lem-gap"></a>
<div id="lem:gap" class="lemma">

**Lemma 2**. *One has $\mu<\lambda_D$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-gap"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-gap</summary>

*Proof.* Choose a nonzero nonnegative Dirichlet minimizer $\phi$, using absolute values in the Rayleigh quotient. Then $\int_\Omega\phi>0$. Work temporarily over $\mathbb C$. For $k\in\mathbb R^2$ with $|k|^2=\lambda_D$, put $$e_k(x)=e^{ik\cdot x},\qquad
w_k=e_k-\frac{\int_\Omega e_k}{\int_\Omega\phi}\phi.$$ Each $w_k$ has mean zero. The form $\int(|\nabla v|^2-\lambda_D|v|^2)$ vanishes on $e_k$ and $\phi$; its cross term vanishes because $-\Delta e_k=\lambda_De_k$ and $\phi$ has zero trace. Thus every nonzero $w_k$ has Rayleigh quotient $\lambda_D$, proving $\mu\le\lambda_D$.

If equality held, each such $w_k$ would be a Neumann eigenfunction by variational equality. The Euler equation first holds against mean-zero tests and then against all tests, since $w_k$ has mean zero. But distinct plane waves are linearly independent: restrict any finite family to a line segment in an interior ball whose direction gives distinct frequencies. Subtracting multiples of one fixed function leaves their span infinite-dimensional. This contradicts finite eigenspace dimension. The real Neumann inequality and its equality case apply in the complexified space by separating real and imaginary parts. ◻

</details>

We also include the smooth conformal parametrization used throughout the proof; see (Pommerenke 1992) <a id="pm-cite-23"></a>[[Pommerenke1992](#ref-Pommerenke1992)] for the classical boundary-regularity theory of conformal maps.

<a id="stmt-lem-conformal"></a>
<div id="lem:conformal" class="lemma">

**Lemma 3**. *There is a conformal bijection $\Phi:\mathbb D\to\Omega$ extending to a smooth diffeomorphism of closures, with $\Phi'\ne0$ on $\overline\mathbb D$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-conformal"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-conformal</summary>

*Proof.* Identify the plane with $\mathbb C$ and fix $a\in\Omega$. Let $l$ solve the harmonic Dirichlet problem with boundary values $\log|z-a|$. It is smooth up to the boundary. Simple connectivity gives a harmonic conjugate $\widetilde l$; its gradient extends smoothly, so the conjugate also extends smoothly in boundary charts. The holomorphic function $$m(z)=(z-a)\exp(-l(z)-i\widetilde l(z))$$ has modulus one on the boundary, modulus less than one inside, and precisely one zero, of order one. Near the boundary, $\log|m|$ is harmonic, negative inside, and zero on the boundary. The boundary point lemma gives a nonzero normal derivative, so $m'$ does not vanish there.

For any $w\in\mathbb D$, take a smooth inner approximation of $\Omega$ so close to its boundary that $|m|>|w|$ throughout the omitted collar. Rouché’s theorem gives exactly one zero of $m-w$ in that approximation, counting multiplicity, because $m$ has exactly one. There are none in the collar. Thus $m$ is a biholomorphism in the interior. It maps the boundary onto the circle by compactness. Its local boundary inverses imply injectivity there: two boundary preimages would give two nearby interior preimages. The inverse map is the required $\Phi$. ◻

</details>

Throughout, $\mathbb S^1=\partial\mathbb D$ carries arclength $\,\mathrm ds$, of total mass $2\pi$.

To bound the dimension of $\mathcal V$, we transfer its mean-zero condition to the boundary and use connectedness of the positive and negative sets to control boundary signs.

<a id="stmt-lem-boundary-weight"></a>
<div id="lem:boundary-weight" class="lemma">

**Lemma 4**. *There is a fixed smooth positive function $\beta$ on $\mathbb S^1$ such that every $v\in\mathcal V$ has boundary trace $h_v=v\circ\Phi$ satisfying $$\int_{\mathbb S^1}\beta h_v\,\mathrm ds=0.$$ If $v\ne0$, its boundary trace is nonzero and takes both signs.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-boundary-weight"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-boundary-weight</summary>

*Proof.* By , the Dirichlet problem $$(-\Delta-\mu)L=1,\qquad L|_{\partial\Omega}=0$$ is coercive and has a smooth solution. Testing by its negative part gives $L\ge0$. Hence $-\Delta L=1+\mu L>0$, and the boundary point lemma gives $-\partial_\nu L>0$. Green’s identity and the Neumann equation yield $$0=\int_\Omega v=-\int_{\partial\Omega}v\,\partial_\nu L
=\int_{\mathbb S^1}\beta(s)h_v(s)\,\mathrm ds,
\quad
\beta=|\Phi'|\,(-\partial_\nu L)\circ\Phi.$$ If $h_v=0$, then $v\in H^1_0(\Omega)$, and the Dirichlet inequality and $\mu<\lambda_D$ force $v=0$. Positivity of $\beta$ gives the last assertion. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-lem-nodal"></a>
<div id="lem:nodal" class="lemma">

**Lemma 5**. *For $0\ne v\in\mathcal V$, the sets $\{v>0\}$ and $\{v<0\}$ are connected. Its boundary trace cannot have four cyclically ordered points with strictly alternating signs.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-nodal"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-nodal</summary>

*Proof.* For each component $U$ of a sign set, the function $v_U=v\mathbf 1_U$ belongs to $H^1(\Omega)$ and has weak gradient $\mathbf 1_U\nabla v$. Indeed it is locally Lipschitz in interior balls: any transition between $U$ and its complement crosses a zero of $v$, so the local Lipschitz bound for $v$ also bounds the zeroed function. This gives the local weak-gradient identity, and the global $L^2$ bounds give global $H^1$ membership. Testing the eigenfunction equation by $v_U$ shows $$\int_\Omega|\nabla v_U|^2=\mu\int_\Omega|v_U|^2.$$ If at least three sign components existed, a nonzero linear combination of two restrictions could be chosen mean zero. Disjoint supports show that it attains the quotient $\mu$. It is therefore an eigenfunction, but vanishes on the omitted open component, contradicting interior analyticity. Both sign sets are nonempty, so each is connected.

Four alternating strict signs on the boundary would give a simple arc joining the two positive points, otherwise in the positive interior set, and another joining the negative points in the negative interior set. Such arcs exist by connectedness of open planar sign sets, using short interior end segments and then polygonal paths with loops removed. After applying $\Phi^{-1}$, their endpoints alternate on a circle. Planar separation forces the two arcs to intersect, contrary to their signs; see [fig:boundary-signs](#fig:boundary-signs). ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-prop-multiplicity"></a>
<div id="prop:multiplicity" class="proposition">

**Proposition 6**. *The real eigenspace $\mathcal V$ has dimension at most two.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-multiplicity"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-multiplicity</summary>

*Proof.* Suppose its dimension is at least three. Since the trace map is injective, choose $0\ne v\in\mathcal V$ whose trace $h=h_v$ satisfies $$\int_{\mathbb S^1}\beta h\cos\theta\,\mathrm ds
=\int_{\mathbb S^1}\beta h\sin\theta\,\mathrm ds=0,
\qquad s=e^{i\theta}.$$ It is already orthogonal to $1$ by [lem:boundary-weight](#lem:boundary-weight). Cut the circle at a strictly negative point and write its angles as $(\theta_0,\theta_0+2\pi)$. If $a$ and $b$ are the infimum and supremum of the positive angles, then $$\theta_0<a<b<\theta_0+2\pi.$$ There are no positive values outside $[a,b]$. Nor is there a negative value between $a$ and $b$: positive values on either side of it, together with the cut point, would violate [lem:nodal](#lem:nodal). The function $$F(\theta)=\cos\!\left(\theta-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)
-\cos\!\left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right)$$ is strictly positive on $(a,b)$ and negative outside $[a,b]$ in the cut interval. Consequently $Fh\ge0$ everywhere and $Fh>0$ on a nonempty open set. Thus $\int\beta Fh>0$, contradicting the three orthogonalities. ◻

</details>

### A variational principle for tangent vector fields

For a real vector field $X\in H^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^2)$ with tangent trace, meaning $\operatorname{tr}X\cdot\nu=0$, set $$\mathcal Q(X)=\int_\Omega\bigl(|\operatorname{div}X|^2+|\operatorname{curl}X|^2\bigr),
\qquad \operatorname{curl}X=\partial_1X_2-\partial_2X_1.$$ The following is Rohleder’s vector variational principle (Rohleder 2021, Theorem 1.1) <a id="pm-cite-26"></a>[[Rohleder2021](#ref-Rohleder2021)]; see also (Rohleder 2024) <a id="pm-cite-27"></a>[[Rohleder2024](#ref-Rohleder2024)]. We include its derivation, in particular its equality case. Our curvature convention below is the opposite of his, so the corresponding boundary term has the opposite sign.

<a id="stmt-prop-vector"></a>
<div id="prop:vector" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:vector-inequality"></a>
**Proposition 7**. *For every such $X$, $$\label{eq:vector-inequality}
\mathcal Q(X)\ge\mu\int_\Omega|X|^2,$$ with equality exactly when $X=\nabla v$ for some $v\in\mathcal V$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-vector"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-vector</summary>

*Proof.* Solve $$\Delta v=\operatorname{div}X,\quad \partial_\nu v=0,\quad\int_\Omega v=0,
\qquad
\Delta j=\operatorname{curl}X,\quad j|_{\partial\Omega}=0.$$ The Neumann compatibility follows from the tangent trace. The solutions are in $H^2(\Omega)$ by smooth-boundary regularity (Showalter 1994, chap. III, Theorems 6.1 and 6.4) <a id="pm-cite-28"></a>[[Showalter1994](#ref-Showalter1994)]. The rotated gradient $\nabla^\perp j=(-\partial_2j,\partial_1j)$ is tangent. Thus $$Z=X-\nabla v-\nabla^\perp j$$ is tangent, curl-free, and divergence-free. Its components are harmonic in the interior, so $Z$ is smooth there. Simple connectivity gives an interior potential $r$ with $Z=\nabla r$.

Here $r\in H^1(\Omega)$, as can be seen without assuming its global integrability in advance. For $M>0$, its bounded truncation $r_M=\max(-M,\min(r,M))$ has local weak gradient of norm at most $|Z|$, hence belongs globally to $H^1(\Omega)$. Fix an interior ball $B$. Its averages $(r_M)_B$ are bounded by $\sup_B|r|$. The Poincaré inequality, with this fixed-ball average as anchor, gives $$\lVert r_M\rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}
\le C\lVert \nabla r_M\rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}+C|(r_M)_B|
\le C\lVert Z\rVert_2+C\sup_B|r|.$$ Fatou gives $r\in L^2$, and its weak gradient is $Z$. Testing $\operatorname{div}Z=0$ against $r$, with its zero normal trace, yields $\int|\nabla r|^2=0$. Thus $$X=\nabla v+\nabla^\perp j.$$ The summands are orthogonal in $L^2$, since the second is divergence-free and tangent. Moreover, $$\mathcal Q(X)=\lVert \Delta v\rVert_2^2+\lVert \Delta j\rVert_2^2.$$ Integration by parts and the two Poincaré inequalities show $$\lVert \nabla v\rVert_2^2
\le\lVert v\rVert_2\lVert \Delta v\rVert_2
\le\mu^{-1/2}\lVert \nabla v\rVert_2\lVert \Delta v\rVert_2,$$ and likewise $\lVert \Delta j\rVert_2^2\ge\lambda_D\lVert \nabla j\rVert_2^2$. Hence $$\mathcal Q(X)\ge\mu\lVert \nabla v\rVert_2^2+\lambda_D\lVert \nabla j\rVert_2^2
=\mu\lVert X\rVert_2^2+(\lambda_D-\mu)\lVert \nabla j\rVert_2^2.$$ By [lem:gap](#lem:gap), this proves <a href="#eq:vector-inequality" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:vector-inequality">[eq:vector-inequality]</a>. Equality forces $j=0$ and equality in the Rayleigh inequality for $v$, so $v\in\mathcal V$. Conversely, gradients of functions in $\mathcal V$ give equality directly. ◻

</details>

For a differential-form interpretation of this divergence–curl energy, see (Fries, Goffeng, and Miranda 2026, sec. 5.1) <a id="pm-cite-29"></a>[[FriesGoffengMiranda2026](#ref-FriesGoffengMiranda2026)]. We will use the following boundary identity in the fixed Cartesian coordinates of the plane.

Let $\tau$ be the counterclockwise unit tangent to $\partial\Omega$, and put $$\kappa=\det(\tau,\partial_\tau\tau).$$ Thus $\kappa$ is positive on a convex circle.

<a id="stmt-lem-curvature"></a>
<div id="lem:curvature" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:curvature"></a>
**Lemma 8**. *For tangent $X\in H^1(\Omega;\mathbb R^2)$, $$\label{eq:curvature}
\mathcal Q(X)=\int_\Omega|\nabla X|^2
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa|X|^2.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-curvature"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-curvature</summary>

*Proof.* For smooth fields, the difference of the bulk integrands is $2\det\nabla X$. Its integral is the boundary pairing $$\int_{\partial\Omega}
(X_1\partial_\tau X_2-X_2\partial_\tau X_1).$$ This identity extends to $H^1$ fields by smooth approximation, since traces are in $H^{1/2}$ and their tangential derivatives are in $H^{-1/2}$. If the trace is $a\tau$, the pairing is $\int\kappa a^2$. This follows first for smooth $a$ and then by approximation in $H^{1/2}$. It gives <a href="#eq:curvature" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:curvature">[eq:curvature]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:boundary"></a>
### Boundary kernels and the multiplier identity

Our objective is a boundary formula for the nonnegative vector energy from [prop:vector](#prop:vector). We first construct scalar solution and interaction kernels for a general nonnegative subcritical potential on the disk. We then return to the conformal potential and derive the multiplier identity that will connect these kernels to an eigenfunction gradient.

<a id="pm-h-9"></a>
#### A subcritical disk potential

<a id="eq:q"></a>
<a id="eq:subcritical"></a>
The conformal map of [lem:conformal](#lem:conformal) defines a measure $$\label{eq:q}
\,\mathrm dq(x)=\mu|\Phi'(x)|^2\,\mathrm dx.$$ It has a bounded positive density. A subscript $q$ on an inner product or norm denotes $L^2(q)$. Conformal invariance of the Dirichlet integral and the Dirichlet inequality on $\Omega$ give $$\label{eq:subcritical}
\lVert \psi\rVert_{L^2(q)}^2\le d\int_\mathbb D|\nabla\psi|^2,
\qquad \psi\in H^1_0(\mathbb D),\qquad d=\frac\mu{\lambda_D}<1.$$ Until the vector-form application in [subsec:energy](#subsec:energy), only boundedness and nonnegativity of the density and <a href="#eq:subcritical" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subcritical">[eq:subcritical]</a> are needed. In particular, the kernel constructions apply to any measure $q$ with those properties. For such a general measure we fix a constant $0<d<1$ satisfying the same inequality; the zero measure also admits this choice.

<a id="eq:green"></a>
The Dirichlet Green function and Poisson kernel of the disk are <a id="eq:poisson"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
G(x,y)&=\frac1{2\pi}\log\left|\frac{1-x\overline y}{x-y}\right|
=\frac1{4\pi}\log\left(1+\frac{(1-|x|^2)(1-|y|^2)}{|x-y|^2}\right),\label{eq:green}\\
P_s(x)&=\frac{1-|x|^2}{2\pi|s-x|^2},\qquad s\in\mathbb S^1.\label{eq:poisson}
\end{aligned}$$ The Green function is positive and is assigned value $+\infty$ on the diagonal. Its logarithmic pole, harmonic correction, and zero boundary data give the Green identity. Also $\int_{\mathbb S^1}P_s(x)\,\mathrm ds=1$.

<a id="stmt-lem-green-operator"></a>
<div id="lem:green-operator" class="lemma">

**Lemma 9**. *The operator $$Tf(x)=\int_\mathbb DG(x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm dq(y)$$ is a bounded self-adjoint operator on $L^2(q)$ with $\lVert T\rVert\le d<1$. The same statement holds with $q$ replaced by any measure $0\le\rho\le q$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-green-operator"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-green-operator</summary>

*Proof.* Write $\,\mathrm dq=a\,\mathrm dx$, with $a$ bounded. For $f\in L^2(q)$, the source $af$ lies in $L^2(\,\mathrm dx)$, since $\int|af|^2\le\lVert a\rVert_\infty\int|f|^2\,\mathrm dq$. Its Dirichlet solution $\psi=Tf$ satisfies $$\int_\mathbb D|\nabla\psi|^2=\langle \psi,f\rangle_q.$$ Combining this with <a href="#eq:subcritical" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subcritical">[eq:subcritical]</a> gives $$\lVert \psi\rVert_q^2\le d\lVert \psi\rVert_q\lVert f\rVert_q,$$ hence the norm bound. Symmetry follows from the Green kernel, or by testing the two Poisson equations against each other. The Green formula can first be proved for smooth compactly supported sources and then passed to $L^2(\,\mathrm dx)$ by approximation; $G(x,\cdot)\in L^2(\,\mathrm dx)$ gives pointwise evaluation. These sections vary continuously in $L^2$ on compact interior sets, so this is the continuous interior representative. For $\rho\le q$, its density remains bounded and its weighted Dirichlet inequality follows from <a href="#eq:subcritical" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subcritical">[eq:subcritical]</a>, proving the same result. ◻

</details>

<a id="eq:extension"></a>
For Lipschitz scalar or vector boundary data $h$, let $$Ah(x)=\int_{\mathbb S^1}P_s(x)h(s)\,\mathrm ds.$$ This harmonic extension belongs to $H^1(\mathbb D)$. The unique weak solution with trace $h$ of $$-\Delta W_h\,\mathrm dx=W_h\,\mathrm dq$$ is $$\label{eq:extension}
W_h=Ah+T(I-T)^{-1}Ah.$$ Indeed the correction has zero trace and solves the required Poisson equation. Uniqueness follows from <a href="#eq:subcritical" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subcritical">[eq:subcritical]</a>. The construction is componentwise for vectors.

<a id="pm-h-10"></a>
#### Positive kernels and their estimates

<a id="eq:K"></a>
<a id="eq:R"></a>
<a id="eq:N"></a>
For $p\in\mathbb D$ and distinct $s,t\in\mathbb S^1$, define $$\begin{aligned}
K(p,s)&=P_s(p)+\sum_{n\ge1}(T^nP_s)(p),\label{eq:K}\\
R(s,t)&=\sum_{n\ge0}\int_\mathbb DP_s\,T^nP_t\,\mathrm dq,\label{eq:R}\\
N(s,t)&=N_0(s,t)+R(s,t),\qquad N_0(s,t)=\frac1{\pi|s-t|^2}.\label{eq:N}
\end{aligned}$$ Powers applied to $P_s$ mean iterated nonnegative kernel integrals, not an a priori application of an $L^2$ operator to $P_s$.

<a id="stmt-lem-kernel-estimates"></a>
<div id="lem:kernel-estimates" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:R-integral"></a>
<a id="eq:K-representation"></a>
<a id="eq:R-representation"></a>
**Lemma 10**. *The kernels above are finite at the stated points. For fixed $p$, $K(p,\cdot)$ is positive and bounded. The kernel $R$ is symmetric, nonnegative, and integrable on $\mathbb S^1\times\mathbb S^1$, with $$\label{eq:R-integral}
\iint R(s,t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt\le\frac{q(\mathbb D)}{1-d}.$$ For Lipschitz boundary data, $$\begin{aligned}
W_h(p)&=\int_{\mathbb S^1}K(p,s)h(s)\,\mathrm ds,\label{eq:K-representation}\\
\langle Ah,(I-T)^{-1}Ak\rangle_q
&=\iint R(s,t)h(s)\cdot k(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt.\label{eq:R-representation}
\end{aligned}$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-kernel-estimates"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-kernel-estimates</summary>

<a id="eq:iterate-bound"></a>
*Proof.* The elementary estimates $$P_s(y)\le\frac C{|y-s|},\qquad
0\le G(x,y)\le C\log\frac2{|x-y|}$$ give uniform bounds for $P_s$ in $L^{3/2}(\,\mathrm dx)$ and for $G(x,\cdot)$ in $L^3(\,\mathrm dx)$. Hölder and the bounded density imply $$\sup_{s\in\mathbb S^1}\lVert TP_s\rVert_{L^\infty(\mathbb D)}<\infty.$$ The section $P_s$ need not belong to $L^2(q)$, but one Green integration gives $F_s=TP_s\in L^\infty(\mathbb D)\subset L^2(q)$. We can now apply the $L^2(q)$ contraction estimate to the remaining iterates. For $n\ge2$, $$\label{eq:iterate-bound}
|(T^nP_s)(p)|
=|\langle G(p,\cdot),T^{n-2}F_s\rangle_q|
\le\lVert G(p,\cdot)\rVert_q\,d^{n-2}\lVert F_s\rVert_q
\le C d^{n-2}.$$ The constants can be chosen uniformly in $p$ and $s$. This proves the assertion about $K$.

For distinct $s,t$, the product $P_sP_t$ is integrable: near either boundary pole, the other factor is bounded, and $|y-s|^{-1}$ is locally integrable in two dimensions. The terms with $n\ge1$ in <a href="#eq:R" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:R">[eq:R]</a> are summable by the first smoothing estimate and <a href="#eq:iterate-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:iterate-bound">[eq:iterate-bound]</a>, since $\int P_s\,\mathrm dq$ is uniformly bounded. Reversing the nonnegative chains proves symmetry. Tonelli’s theorem and $\int P_s\,\mathrm ds=1$ give $$\iint R(s,t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt
=\sum_{n\ge0}\langle \mathbf 1,T^n\mathbf 1\rangle_q
\le\sum_{n\ge0}d^n\lVert \mathbf 1\rVert_q^2,$$ which is <a href="#eq:R-integral" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:R-integral">[eq:R-integral]</a>. Diagonal values of $R$ play no role in these boundary integrals. Expanding the Neumann series in <a href="#eq:extension" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:extension">[eq:extension]</a> and using the positive kernels gives <a href="#eq:K-representation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:K-representation">[eq:K-representation]</a> and <a href="#eq:R-representation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:R-representation">[eq:R-representation]</a>; the same bounds justify the exchanges for signed data by absolute domination. ◻

</details>

<a id="subsec:energy"></a>
#### The boundary energy

<a id="eq:E-nullspace"></a>
Return now to $q$ in <a href="#eq:q" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:q">[eq:q]</a>. A vector boundary function $h$ is called tangent if $h(s)$ is tangent to $\partial\Omega$ at $\Phi(s)$. Extend its fixed Cartesian components by <a href="#eq:extension" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:extension">[eq:extension]</a>, and set $$X_h=W_h\circ\Phi^{-1},\qquad
E(h)=\mathcal Q(X_h)-\mu\lVert X_h\rVert_{L^2(\Omega)}^2.$$ We write $E(h,k)$ for its bilinear form. [prop:vector](#prop:vector) gives $E\ge0$ on tangent Lipschitz boundary data, and $$\label{eq:E-nullspace}
E(h)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X_h=\nabla v\text{ for some }v\in\mathcal V.$$

<a id="stmt-lem-boundary-energy"></a>
<div id="lem:boundary-energy" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:E-boundary"></a>
**Lemma 11**. *For tangent Lipschitz data $h,k$, $$\begin{aligned}
E(h,k)={}&\frac12\iint N_0(s,t)
(h(s)-h(t))\cdot(k(s)-k(t))\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt\notag\\
&-\iint R(s,t)h(s)\cdot k(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt
+\int_{\mathbb S^1}|\Phi'|(\kappa\circ\Phi)h\cdot k\,\mathrm ds.
\label{eq:E-boundary}
\end{aligned}$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-boundary-energy"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-boundary-energy</summary>

*Proof.* By and conformal invariance of the scalar Dirichlet integral applied to each Cartesian component, the bulk terms of $E$ are $$\int_\mathbb D\nabla W_h:\nabla W_k-\langle W_h,W_k\rangle_q.$$ The equation for $W_k$ permits replacing $W_h$ here by $Ah$, since their difference has zero trace. Harmonicity of $Ah$ then replaces $W_k$ by $Ak$ in the gradient term. Since $W_k=(I-T)^{-1}Ak$ in $L^2(q)$, this gives $$\int_\mathbb D\nabla Ah:\nabla Ak-\langle Ah,(I-T)^{-1}Ak\rangle_q.$$ The boundary curvature term changes by the factor $|\Phi'|$, and <a href="#eq:R-representation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:R-representation">[eq:R-representation]</a> identifies the second term.

For the harmonic energy, <a id="eq:harmonic-energy"></a>$$\label{eq:harmonic-energy}
\int_\mathbb D\nabla Ah:\nabla Ak
=\frac12\iint N_0(s,t)(h(s)-h(t))\cdot(k(s)-k(t))\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt.$$ Indeed both sides diagonalize in Fourier modes. The Hermitian energy of $s^m$ is $2\pi|m|$, while $$\iint\frac{|s^m-t^m|^2}{|s-t|^2}\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt=(2\pi)^2|m|$$ by a geometric sum, verifying the normalization. Smooth convolution approximation, with uniformly bounded Lipschitz constants and convergence in $H^{1/2}$, extends the identity to the stated data. This proves <a href="#eq:E-boundary" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:E-boundary">[eq:E-boundary]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="eq:g"></a>
Fix $0\ne u\in\mathcal V$ and let $$\label{eq:g}
g(s)=(\nabla u)(\Phi(s)),\qquad s\in\mathbb S^1.$$ It is tangent by the Neumann condition. Differentiation of the Euclidean Helmholtz equation commutes with its Cartesian derivatives, so uniqueness gives <a id="eq:gradient-extension"></a>$$\label{eq:gradient-extension}
W_g=(\nabla u)\circ\Phi,
\qquad E(g)=0.$$

<a id="stmt-prop-multiplier"></a>
<div id="prop:multiplier" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:multiplier"></a>
**Proposition 12** (Multiplier identity). *For every real Lipschitz function $b$ on $\mathbb S^1$, $$\label{eq:multiplier}
E(bg)=\frac12\iint N(s,t)(b(s)-b(t))^2
g(s)\cdot g(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-multiplier"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-multiplier</summary>

*Proof.* Since $E$ is positive semidefinite and $E(g)=0$, its bilinear form annihilates $g$: this follows by considering $E(g+th)$ for real $t$. In particular, $E(g,b^2g)=0$. Subtract <a href="#eq:E-boundary" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:E-boundary">[eq:E-boundary]</a> for this pair from the formula for $E(bg,bg)$. The curvature terms cancel. The harmonic term gives $$\frac12N_0(s,t)(b(s)-b(t))^2g(s)\cdot g(t)$$ in the double integral. Symmetry of $R$ gives the same expression with $R$ in place of $N_0$. Their sum is <a href="#eq:multiplier" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:multiplier">[eq:multiplier]</a>. All terms are integrable: the squared Lipschitz difference cancels the singularity of $N_0$, and $R$ is integrable. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:reciprocal"></a>
### Reciprocal Green functions and a squared-distance kernel

In this section we prove the reciprocal-kernel theorem for a general disk potential. The kernels $K,N$ are those of <a href="#eq:K" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:K">[eq:K]</a>–<a href="#eq:N" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:N">[eq:N]</a>; their construction and estimates require only the measure hypotheses restated in the theorem below.

A real symmetric kernel $D$ with zero diagonal is *conditionally negative semidefinite* if, for every finite family of points and real coefficients with $\sum_i a_i=0$, $$\sum_{i,j}a_i a_jD(s_i,s_j)\le0.$$

<a id="stmt-thm-negative-kernel"></a>
<div id="thm:negative-kernel" class="theorem">

**Theorem 13**. *Let $\,\mathrm dq=a\,\mathrm dx$ on $\mathbb D$, where $a\in L^\infty(\mathbb D)$ is nonnegative, and suppose there is a constant $0<d<1$ such that $$\int_\mathbb D|\psi|^2\,\mathrm dq\le d\int_\mathbb D|\nabla\psi|^2
\qquad(\psi\in H^1_0(\mathbb D)).$$ For the associated kernels $K,N$ and every $p\in\mathbb D$, the kernel <a id="eq:D"></a>$$\label{eq:D}
D_p(s,t)=\frac{K(p,s)K(p,t)}{N(s,t)}\quad(s\ne t),
\qquad D_p(s,s)=0,$$ is conditionally negative semidefinite on $\mathbb S^1$.*

</div>

<a id="stmt-rem-zero-potential"></a>
<div id="rem:zero-potential" class="remark">

*Remark 14*. For $q=0$, this kernel is a rescaled squared chordal distance. Indeed, with the disk automorphism $\varphi_p(z)=(z-p)/(1-\overline pz)$, $$D_p(s,t)=\pi P_s(p)P_t(p)|s-t|^2
=\frac1{4\pi}|\varphi_p(s)-\varphi_p(t)|^2.$$ [thm:negative-kernel](#thm:negative-kernel) supplies a Hilbert-space squared-distance interpretation after a nonnegative subcritical potential is added.

</div>

We prove the theorem through reciprocal matrices. The reciprocal inertia condition comes from the McCullough–Quiggin theory (McCullough 1994; P. P. Quiggin 1994) <a id="pm-cite-30"></a>[[McCullough1994](#ref-McCullough1994), [Quiggin1994](#ref-Quiggin1994)]. For finite-valued positive-definite kernels whose entries never vanish, it characterizes the complete Nevanlinna–Pick property (Agler and McCarthy 2000, Corollary 1.12) <a id="pm-cite-31"></a>[[AglerMcCarthy2000](#ref-AglerMcCarthy2000)]. Quiggin established the reciprocal condition for a class of weighted one-dimensional Sobolev kernels, represented by Green functions of regular Sturm–Liouville problems (P. P. Quiggin 1994, sec. 2.2, Corollary 2.2.2 and Theorem 2.2.3) <a id="pm-cite-32"></a>[[Quiggin1994](#ref-Quiggin1994)]. Here this condition serves as an algebraic model; all the matrix and analytic arguments needed for the singular Green kernel will be given below.

Say that a real symmetric matrix has property <span class="upright">(P)</span> if it has at most one positive eigenvalue, counted with multiplicity. This property is preserved by positive diagonal congruence, subtraction of a positive semidefinite matrix, passage to principal submatrices, and limits of matrices of fixed size. Reciprocals below are always *entrywise*, never matrix inverses.

The fixed interior point $p$ connects <span class="upright">(P)</span> to the desired conditional negativity. Adjoining it to boundary points will give a reciprocal matrix with zero diagonal, boundary block $B_{ij}=1/N(s_i,s_j)$ for $i\ne j$, and border $\ell_i=1/K(p,s_i)$. Property <span class="upright">(P)</span> for this bordered matrix forces $x^TBx\le0$ on $\ell^Tx=0$; the substitution $x_i=K(p,s_i)a_i$ turns this into the required inequality for $\sum_i a_i=0$. We prove this last algebraic step at the end of the section.

We first construct bounded regularizations of $G$ whose reciprocals have <span class="upright">(P)</span>, and prove that updates along a column of the current kernel matrix preserve this condition. The resolvent construction then uses four limits, in order: refine a positive quadrature at fixed $\epsilon>0$ and compactly supported $0\le\rho\le q$; let $\epsilon\downarrow0$; send the moving evaluation points radially to the circle while fixing $p$; and finally increase $\rho$ to $q$. Compact support keeps the Poisson sections bounded during the boundary step.

<a id="pm-h-13"></a>
#### A bounded regularization of the Green kernel

Direct quadrature of $G$ would encounter the infinite value $G(z,z)$ at every integration node. We therefore need a bounded replacement with finite positive diagonal that retains the reciprocal condition <span class="upright">(P)</span>.

The scalar integral used below is an equivalent form of the logarithmic-mean representations in (Qi, Zhang, and Li 2014) <a id="pm-cite-33"></a>[[QiZhangLi2014](#ref-QiZhangLi2014)]. We derive it and then establish the positive-semidefinite disk-kernel property that we need.

<a id="stmt-lem-regularization"></a>
<div id="lem:regularization" class="lemma">

**Lemma 15**. *There are bounded continuous strictly positive kernels $C_\epsilon$ on $\mathbb D\times\mathbb D$, $0<\epsilon\le1$, such that $C_\epsilon\uparrow G$ as $\epsilon\downarrow0$, including on the diagonal, and every finite matrix $(1/C_\epsilon(x_i,x_j))_{i,j}$ has <span class="upright">(P)</span>.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-regularization"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-regularization</summary>

<a id="eq:lorentz"></a>
*Proof.* Put $$h(x,y)=\frac{|x-y|^2}{(1-|x|^2)(1-|y|^2)},\qquad
L(h)=\frac1{\log(1+1/h)}\ (h>0),\quad L(0)=0,$$ and $f(h)=h+\tfrac12-L(h)$. After multiplying the $x$- and $y$-entries by $1-|x|^2$ and $1-|y|^2$, respectively, the kernel $h+\tfrac12$ becomes $$\label{eq:lorentz}
\tfrac12(1+|x|^2)(1+|y|^2)-2x\cdot y.$$ This is a positive rank-one kernel minus a positive semidefinite kernel, so $h+\tfrac12$ has <span class="upright">(P)</span> on finite sets.

<a id="eq:f-mixture"></a>
Since $1/G=4\pi L(h)$, the identity $L=(h+\tfrac12)-f$ will be useful once we prove that $f(h(x,y))$ is positive semidefinite. For $h>0$, <a id="eq:log-mean"></a>$$\label{eq:log-mean}
L(h)=\int_0^1h^{1-\alpha}(h+1)^\alpha\,\mathrm d\alpha.$$ For $0<\alpha<1$, let $c_\alpha$ normalize $v^{\alpha-1}(1+v)^{-1}\,\mathrm dv$ to a probability measure on $(0,\infty)$, and set $r=(1+v)^{-1}$. Integration by parts gives $$\mathbb E_\alpha(1-r)
=c_\alpha\int_0^\infty\frac{v^\alpha}{(1+v)^2}\,\mathrm dv
=\alpha.$$ For $h>0$, scaling $v$ in the normalized integral yields <a id="eq:mixture-algebra"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
h^{1-\alpha}(h+1)^\alpha
&=c_\alpha\int_0^\infty
\frac{h(h+1)}{(h+1)+vh}v^{\alpha-1}\,\mathrm dv\notag\\
&=\mathbb E_\alpha\frac{h(h+1)}{h+r}
=\mathbb E_\alpha\left[h+(1-r)-\frac{r(1-r)}{h+r}\right].\label{eq:mixture-algebra}
\end{aligned}$$ The last equality remains true at $h=0$. Integrating in $\alpha$ therefore gives $$\label{eq:f-mixture}
f(h)=\int_0^1\mathbb E_\alpha\frac{r(1-r)}{h+r}\,\mathrm d\alpha,
\qquad 0\le f(h)\le f(0)=\tfrac12.$$

Now $$k(x,y)=\frac1{1+h(x,y)}
=\frac{(1-|x|^2)(1-|y|^2)}{|1-x\overline y|^2}$$ is positive semidefinite. Indeed $(1-x\overline y)^{-1}$ is a Gram kernel by its power series; multiplying it by its complex conjugate and the positive diagonal factors preserves positive semidefiniteness. For fixed $0<r<1$, <a id="eq:schur-geometric"></a>$$\label{eq:schur-geometric}
\frac1{h+r}=\sum_{n\ge0}(1-r)^n k^{n+1},$$ where powers are entrywise. This series converges even when $h=0$. Each power is positive semidefinite, as follows by taking tensor products of Gram vectors. Thus $1/(h+r)$ is positive semidefinite, and the positive mixture <a href="#eq:f-mixture" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:f-mixture">[eq:f-mixture]</a> proves the claim. The mixture has finite entries by the displayed bound, including on the diagonal.

Define <a id="eq:C-epsilon"></a>$$\label{eq:C-epsilon}
\frac1{C_\epsilon(x,y)}
=4\pi\bigl[h+\tfrac12-(1-\epsilon)f(h)\bigr]
=4\pi\bigl[L(h)+\epsilon f(h)\bigr].$$ Its reciprocal matrix has <span class="upright">(P)</span> by <a href="#eq:lorentz" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:lorentz">[eq:lorentz]</a> and the positive semidefiniteness just proved. Its denominator is at least $2\pi\epsilon$, because $$L+\epsilon f=\epsilon(h+\tfrac12)+(1-\epsilon)L\ge\epsilon/2.$$ It is positive and continuous, so $C_\epsilon$ is bounded, continuous, and strictly positive. Since $f\ge0$, these kernels increase to $G$ by <a href="#eq:green" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:green">[eq:green]</a>, and $0<C_\epsilon\le G$. On the diagonal $C_\epsilon(x,x)=1/(2\pi\epsilon)$, as required. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-14"></a>
#### A finite resolvent update

<a id="stmt-lem-update"></a>
<div id="lem:update" class="lemma">

**Lemma 16**. *Let $M$ be a real symmetric finite matrix with strictly positive entries such that its reciprocal has <span class="upright">(P)</span>. For any index $z$ and any $c\ge0$, the matrix $$\widetilde M_{ij}=M_{ij}+cM_{iz}M_{zj}$$ also has a reciprocal with <span class="upright">(P)</span>.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-update"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-update</summary>

*Proof.* Positive diagonal congruence transforms the reciprocal of $M$ into $$H_{ij}=\frac{M_{iz}M_{zj}}{M_{ij}}.$$ Writing $a=M_{zz}>0$, its $z$-row and column are identically $a$. Put $z$ last, and let $H_0$ denote the remaining principal block. Subtracting the last row from every other row, and doing the same to the columns, gives the congruence $$H\ \sim\
\begin{pmatrix}H_0-a\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T&0\\0&a\end{pmatrix}.$$ Because $a>0$ and $H$ has <span class="upright">(P)</span>, the first block is nonpositive. Hence $$U=a\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T-H\succeq 0.$$ Since $H_{ii}>0$, one has $0\le U_{ii}<a$, and positive-semidefinite Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|U_{ij}|<a$.

Using the same diagonal normalization, the new reciprocal has entries $H_{ij}/(1+cH_{ij})$. The difference from the constant rank-one kernel is <a id="eq:update-series"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
\frac a{1+ca}-\frac{H_{ij}}{1+cH_{ij}}
&=\frac{U_{ij}}{(1+ca)(1+ca-cU_{ij})}\notag\\
&=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{c^nU_{ij}^{n+1}}{(1+ca)^{n+2}}.\label{eq:update-series}
\end{aligned}$$ The series converges absolutely because $|cU_{ij}|<1+ca$. Its coefficients are nonnegative and every entrywise power of $U$ is positive semidefinite, even if $U$ has negative entries. Thus the normalized reciprocal of $\widetilde M$ is a positive rank-one kernel minus a positive semidefinite matrix, proving <span class="upright">(P)</span>. For $c=0$ the identity reduces directly to $U$. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-15"></a>
#### The interior resolvent kernel

<a id="eq:M-rho"></a>
For $0\le\rho\le q$ supported on a compact subset of $\mathbb D$, let $T_\rho$ be the Green operator on $L^2(\rho)$ and define $$\label{eq:M-rho}
M^\rho(x,y)=G(x,y)
+\langle G(x,\cdot),(I-T_\rho)^{-1}G(y,\cdot)\rangle_\rho.$$ It is finite for distinct interior points, since its Green sections belong to $L^2(\rho)$, and has infinite diagonal.

<a id="stmt-prop-interior-reciprocal"></a>
<div id="prop:interior-reciprocal" class="proposition">

**Proposition 17**. *For every finite set of interior points, the reciprocal matrix of $M^\rho$, with diagonal zero, has <span class="upright">(P)</span>.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-interior-reciprocal"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-interior-reciprocal</summary>

*Proof.* First fix $\epsilon>0$ and replace $G$ throughout <a href="#eq:M-rho" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:M-rho">[eq:M-rho]</a> by $C=C_\epsilon$. Its integral operator $T_C$ on $L^2(\rho)$ satisfies $$|T_Cf|\le T_\rho|f|,\qquad \lVert T_C\rVert\le d<1.$$ The absolute pointwise domination proves the norm bound without requiring a separate positivity assertion about its quadratic form.

<a id="eq:quadrature-resolvent"></a>
Partition a compact integration set into finitely many measurable cells with uniformly vanishing diameters. For each cell of positive $\rho$-mass, choose a representative, and let $\pi$ send that cell to the representative. Use its mass as the quadrature weight. Uniform continuity gives uniform convergence of $$C(\pi(\cdot),\pi(\cdot))\longrightarrow C$$ on the integration set up to null sets. The associated operators $T_\pi$ on $L^2(\rho)$ converge to $T_C$ in operator norm; for example their norm difference is at most $\rho(\mathbb D)$ times the uniform kernel error. Likewise $C(x,\pi(\cdot))\to C(x,\cdot)$ in $L^2(\rho)$ for each fixed evaluation point $x$. In particular, sufficiently fine quadratures have $\lVert T_\pi\rVert<1$, and $$\label{eq:quadrature-resolvent}
C(x,y)+\langle C(x,\pi(\cdot)),(I-T_\pi)^{-1}C(y,\pi(\cdot))\rangle_\rho$$ converges to the regularized continuum resolvent evaluation. This follows from convergence of the source vectors and of the inverses in operator norm.

For clarity, if the nodes and weights are $z_\alpha,w_\alpha$, set $$\mathsf T_{\alpha\beta}
=\sqrt{w_\alpha}\,C(z_\alpha,z_\beta)\sqrt{w_\beta},
\qquad
(\xi_x)_\alpha=\sqrt{w_\alpha}\,C(x,z_\alpha).$$ The normalized cell indicators form an orthonormal basis for the step functions. On this subspace $T_\pi$ is represented by $\mathsf T$, and it vanishes on the orthogonal complement. Thus $\lVert \mathsf T\rVert<1$, and the correction in <a href="#eq:quadrature-resolvent" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:quadrature-resolvent">[eq:quadrature-resolvent]</a> is $\xi_x^T(I-\mathsf T)^{-1}\xi_y$. Evaluation points enter only these finite vectors, including when they coincide with nodes.

We next prove <span class="upright">(P)</span> for each sufficiently fine quadrature. On the union of the evaluation points and quadrature nodes, start with $M_{ij}=C(x_i,x_j)$. By [lem:regularization](#lem:regularization), its reciprocal has <span class="upright">(P)</span>. Expanding the finite resolvent sums all chains, multiplying kernel entries along each chain and the quadrature weight at every intermediate occurrence. These nonnegative sums are finite by the preceding matrix formula. Restricting the allowed intermediate nodes gives a subseries and hence remains finite.

Suppose some nodes have been allowed and their chain sum is $M$. Admit one more node $z$ of weight $w>0$. Chains with exactly $k\ge1$ internal occurrences of $z$ have total weight $$w^kM_{iz}M_{zz}^{k-1}M_{zj}.$$ All factors are positive. Finiteness of the full series forces $wM_{zz}<1$, and summing over $k$ updates the matrix to <a id="eq:elimination"></a>$$\label{eq:elimination}
M_{ij}+\frac{w}{1-wM_{zz}}M_{iz}M_{zj}.$$ [lem:update](#lem:update) therefore propagates <span class="upright">(P)</span> one node at a time. This counting remains valid when an endpoint is a quadrature node, since only internal occurrences receive weights. Coincident node locations may be merged by adding their weights. Thus the discrete kernel <a href="#eq:quadrature-resolvent" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:quadrature-resolvent">[eq:quadrature-resolvent]</a> has the required reciprocal property. The quadrature limit gives it for the regularized continuum kernel. If $\rho=0$, this conclusion follows directly from [lem:regularization](#lem:regularization).

Finally let $\epsilon\downarrow0$. Every chain integral increases to its counterpart with $G$ by monotone convergence, as does the sum of these nonnegative integrals. Off the diagonal, its limit is <a href="#eq:M-rho" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:M-rho">[eq:M-rho]</a>, and the correction is finite because $$\big|\langle G(x,\cdot),(I-T_\rho)^{-1}G(y,\cdot)\rangle_\rho\big|
\le\frac{\lVert G(x,\cdot)\rVert_\rho\lVert G(y,\cdot)\rVert_\rho}{1-d}.$$ The direct diagonal term tends to infinity, so its reciprocal tends to zero. Finite entrywise limits preserve <span class="upright">(P)</span>, completing the proof. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-16"></a>
#### The boundary limit

For compactly supported $\rho$, write $K^\rho,R^\rho,N^\rho$ for the kernels in <a href="#eq:K" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:K">[eq:K]</a>–<a href="#eq:N" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:N">[eq:N]</a> with $q$ replaced by $\rho$. The boundary limit will be taken with this compact support fixed, before increasing $\rho$ to $q$.

<a id="stmt-lem-bordered"></a>
<div id="lem:bordered" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:bordered"></a>
**Lemma 18**. *For distinct $s_1,\ldots,s_m\in\mathbb S^1$ and $p\in\mathbb D$, the matrix $$\label{eq:bordered}
\begin{pmatrix}B&\ell\\\ell^T&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B_{ij}=\begin{cases}1/N(s_i,s_j),&i\ne j,\\0,&i=j,\end{cases}
\qquad \ell_i=1/K(p,s_i),$$ has <span class="upright">(P)</span>.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-bordered"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-bordered</summary>

*Proof.* Fix compactly supported $0\le\rho\le q$. Apply [prop:interior-reciprocal](#prop:interior-reciprocal) to the points $r s_1,\ldots,r s_m,p$, with $r\uparrow1$ and $r>|p|$. The explicit Green formula gives <a id="eq:radial-one"></a><a id="eq:radial-two"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{G(rs,x)}{1-r}&\longrightarrow P_s(x),\label{eq:radial-one}\\
\frac{G(rs,rt)}{(1-r)^2}&\longrightarrow N_0(s,t)\qquad(s\ne t).\label{eq:radial-two}
\end{aligned}$$ The first convergence is uniform on compact interior sets. For the second, one may use $$G(rs,rt)=\frac1{4\pi}\log\left(1+\frac{(1-r^2)^2}{r^2|s-t|^2}\right).$$ The bounded resolvent in <a href="#eq:M-rho" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:M-rho">[eq:M-rho]</a> and the uniform convergence on $\operatorname{supp}\rho$ therefore imply $$\frac{M^\rho(rs_i,rs_j)}{(1-r)^2}\longrightarrow N^\rho(s_i,s_j)
\quad(i\ne j),\qquad
\frac{M^\rho(p,rs_i)}{1-r}\longrightarrow K^\rho(p,s_i).$$ Here the first resolvent correction tends to $\langle P_{s_i},(I-T_\rho)^{-1}P_{s_j}\rangle_\rho=R^\rho(s_i,s_j)$. The second tends to $P_{s_i}(p)+\langle G(p,\cdot),(I-T_\rho)^{-1}P_{s_i}\rangle_\rho=K^\rho(p,s_i)$. These pairings are legitimate on the compact support, where the Poisson kernels are bounded.

Scale the reciprocal matrix by positive diagonal congruence with factors $1-r$ at the moving points and factor $1$ at $p$. Its limit is <a href="#eq:bordered" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:bordered">[eq:bordered]</a> with $K^\rho,N^\rho$, so it has <span class="upright">(P)</span>. The zero diagonal stays zero throughout.

Now choose increasing disk cutoffs $\,\mathrm d\rho_n=\mathbf 1_{\{|x|\le r_n\}}\,\mathrm dq$, with $r_n\uparrow1$. Every term of $K^{\rho_n}$ and $R^{\rho_n}$ is a nonnegative chain integral. Monotone convergence in each finite product integral and then in the series gives $$K^{\rho_n}(p,s)\uparrow K(p,s),\qquad
N^{\rho_n}(s,t)\uparrow N(s,t)\quad(s\ne t).$$ These limits are finite by [lem:kernel-estimates](#lem:kernel-estimates). Taking the reciprocals and the finite matrix limit proves <a href="#eq:bordered" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:bordered">[eq:bordered]</a>. No $L^2(q)$ boundary limit of an individual Poisson kernel has been used. ◻

</details>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-bordered-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-bordered</summary>

*Proof of [thm:negative-kernel](#thm:negative-kernel).* Use the matrix of [lem:bordered](#lem:bordered). We first show that $x^TBx\le0$ whenever $\ell^Tx=0$. If instead $x^TBx>0$, choose $y$ with $\ell^Ty\ne0$ and replace it by $$y-\frac{x^TBy}{x^TBx}x.$$ Then $x^TBy=0$ and still $\ell^Ty\ne0$. In the bordered form, $(x,0)$ and $(y,c)$ are orthogonal, and $c$ can be chosen so that $$(y,c)^T\begin{pmatrix}B&\ell\\\ell^T&0\end{pmatrix}(y,c)
=y^TBy+2c\ell^Ty>0.$$ They would span a two-dimensional positive subspace, contradicting <span class="upright">(P)</span>.

Finally, for coefficients with $\sum_i a_i=0$, put $x_i=K(p,s_i)a_i$. Then $\ell^Tx=0$ and $$\sum_{i,j}a_i a_jD_p(s_i,s_j)=x^TBx\le0.$$ This proves the assertion on distinct points; repeated points are handled by combining their coefficients. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:conclusion"></a>
### Hilbert multipliers and the absence of critical points

We now use the conditional negativity proved in [thm:negative-kernel](#thm:negative-kernel) to construct all the scalar multipliers needed in [prop:multiplier](#prop:multiplier) at once. The Hilbert-space construction is the classical squared-distance correspondence of Schoenberg (Schoenberg 1938) <a id="pm-cite-34"></a>[[Schoenberg1938](#ref-Schoenberg1938)]; we give the short Gram-kernel proof.

<a id="stmt-lem-hilbert"></a>
<div id="lem:hilbert" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:hilbert-distance"></a>
**Lemma 19**. *For every $p\in\mathbb D$ there is an injective Lipschitz map $b:\mathbb S^1\to\mathcal H$, where $\mathcal H$ is a separable real Hilbert space, such that $$\label{eq:hilbert-distance}
\lVert b(s)-b(t)\rVert_\mathcal H^2=D_p(s,t).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-hilbert"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-hilbert</summary>

*Proof.* Fix $s_0\in\mathbb S^1$. Conditional negativity makes the anchored kernel $$\Gamma(s,t)=\tfrac12\bigl(D_p(s,s_0)+D_p(t,s_0)-D_p(s,t)\bigr)$$ positive semidefinite: append to any finite family of coefficients the coefficient at $s_0$ that makes their sum zero. Give formal finite linear combinations of symbols $b(s)$ the bilinear form with Gram kernel $\Gamma$, quotient by its nullspace, and complete. Since $D_p(s,s)=0$, this realizes <a href="#eq:hilbert-distance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:hilbert-distance">[eq:hilbert-distance]</a> and $b(s_0)=0$.

Positivity of $K$ and finiteness and positivity of $N$ off the diagonal give $D_p(s,t)>0$ for $s\ne t$, so $b$ is injective. Also $N\ge N_0$ gives $$\lVert b(s)-b(t)\rVert_\mathcal H^2
\le\pi\lVert K(p,\cdot)\rVert_\infty^2|s-t|^2.$$ Thus $b$ is Lipschitz. Replace $\mathcal H$ by the closed span of $b(\mathbb S^1)$; it is separable by continuity and separability of the circle. ◻

</details>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-hilbert-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-hilbert</summary>

*Proof of [thm:main](#thm:main).* Take an arbitrary $0\ne u\in\mathcal V$, with $g$ defined by <a href="#eq:g" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:g">[eq:g]</a>. Suppose $\Phi(p)$ is a critical point. Choose $b$ from [lem:hilbert](#lem:hilbert), and let $b_j$ be its coordinates in a finite or countable orthonormal basis. Each $b_j$ is real Lipschitz, so [prop:multiplier](#prop:multiplier) applies. For partial sums, the absolute integrand is bounded by $$\begin{aligned}
N(s,t)\sum_{j\le m}|b_j(s)-b_j(t)|^2|g(s)||g(t)|
&\le N(s,t)D_p(s,t)|g(s)||g(t)|\\
&=K(p,s)K(p,t)|g(s)||g(t)|.
\end{aligned}$$ The right side is bounded and integrable. Dominated convergence therefore gives <a id="eq:sum-energy"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
\sum_j E(b_jg)
&=\frac12\iint K(p,s)K(p,t)g(s)\cdot g(t)\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt\notag\\
&=\frac12\left|\int_{\mathbb S^1}K(p,s)g(s)\,\mathrm ds\right|^2
=\frac12|W_g(p)|^2=0.\label{eq:sum-energy}
\end{aligned}$$ Every summand is nonnegative. By <a href="#eq:E-nullspace" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:E-nullspace">[eq:E-nullspace]</a>, for every $j$ there is $v_j\in\mathcal V$ whose boundary gradient, pulled back by $\Phi$, is $b_jg$. The trace equality holds pointwise because both sides are continuous. The boundary gradient of $u$ is $g$.

Set $S_g=\{s\in\mathbb S^1:g(s)\ne0\}$. This is a nonempty open set. If it were empty, each Cartesian derivative of $u$ would solve the Helmholtz equation with zero Dirichlet trace, contradicting $\mu<\lambda_D$ unless both vanished. Then $u$ would be a constant eigenfunction for a positive eigenvalue, hence zero.

The two possibilities for $S_g$ lead respectively to multiplicity and boundary-uniqueness contradictions.

*Case 1: $S_g$ is dense.* The set $b(S_g)$ cannot lie in an affine line. Otherwise continuity would place $b(\mathbb S^1)$ in that closed line, but a continuous injective circle cannot map into a line. Indeed its compact connected image would be an interval; deleting an interior point disconnects an interval but not a circle with one point removed.

Choose three points of $S_g$ whose images are affinely independent. Their two difference vectors are linearly independent in $\mathcal H$, so some two coordinates $j,k$ have a nonzero $2\times2$ minor. To see this, if all such minors vanished, a nonzero coordinate of the first difference vector would force every coordinate of the second to be proportional to it. Thus the functions $1,b_j,b_k$ are linearly independent on those three points. Since $g\ne0$ there, the vector traces $g,b_jg,b_kg$ are linearly independent. They are gradient traces of three functions in $\mathcal V$, contradicting [prop:multiplicity](#prop:multiplicity).

*Case 2: $S_g$ is not dense.* There is a nonempty open arc $I$ on which $g=0$. Since $S_g$ is nonempty and open and $b$ is injective, some coordinate $b_j$ is nonconstant on $S_g$. Consequently $g$ and $b_jg$ are linearly independent, and so are their eigenfunctions $u$ and $v_j$. Their gradients vanish on the boundary arc $\Phi(I)$, so each has a constant trace there. Choose a nonzero linear combination $w$ of them whose constant trace on that arc is zero. It also has zero normal derivative there.

Take a small neighborhood of an interior point of the arc that meets no other boundary portion, and extend $w$ by zero to the exterior of $\Omega$ in that neighborhood. This is a local version of the zero-extension argument in (Filonov 2005, Lemma, p. 414) <a id="pm-cite-35"></a>[[Filonov2005](#ref-Filonov2005)]. Integration by parts against compactly supported tests shows that this extension solves $(\Delta+\mu)w=0$ distributionally: both its Dirichlet and normal traces on the intervening boundary vanish. Interior elliptic regularity and analyticity make the extension analytic. Since it is zero on an exterior open set, it is zero throughout a smaller neighborhood. Analytic continuation inside the connected domain gives $w=0$ on $\Omega$, contradicting independence of $u$ and $v_j$. No analyticity of the boundary is used.

Both cases contradict the assumed critical point. Thus $\nabla u$ is nowhere zero in $\Omega$. The continuous function $u$ attains both extrema on the compact closure. Any interior extremum would be critical, so both are attained on the boundary. An interior value equal to either boundary extremum would itself be a global extremum. This proves both strict inequalities in <a href="#eq:hotspots" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:hotspots">[eq:hotspots]</a>. ◻

</details>
