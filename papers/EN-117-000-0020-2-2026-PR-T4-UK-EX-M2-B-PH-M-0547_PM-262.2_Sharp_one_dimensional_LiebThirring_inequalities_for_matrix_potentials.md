## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We prove the sharp one-dimensional Lieb–Thirring inequality for every finite matrix size and every exponent $`1/2\lt \gamma\lt 3/2`$. The optimal constant is the scalar one-bound-state constant, independently of the matrix size. The inequality bounds the full sum of negative eigenvalue moments for every measurable Hermitian positive semidefinite potential <i>W</i> satisfying $`\int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits (W^{\gamma+1/2})\lt \infty`$. The matrices may have arbitrary rank and need not commute at different points.

- **claim-sharp-matrix-lt** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): For every $1/2<\gamma<3/2$, every finite $m\ge1$, and every $W$ satisfying <a href="#eq:potential-class" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:potential-class">[eq:potential-class]</a>, $$\label{eq:main-bound} \mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(H_W)_-^\gamma\le C_\gamma\int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W(x)^{\gamma+1/2})\,dx, \qquad C_\gamma= \left(\frac{\gamma-1/2}{\gamma+1/2}\right)^{\gamma-1/2} \frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\gamma+3/2)}.$$ The constant is optimal in every matrix dimension. With $r=(\gamma-1/2)^{-1}$, it is attained by $$\label{eq:extremal-potential} W(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\big((r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx),0,\ldots,0\big).$$
  - Explanation: The sum of all negative eigenvalue moments of the matrix Schrödinger operator is bounded by the one-bound-state constant times the integral of the trace power of the potential. The constant does not depend on the matrix size and is attained by a potential supported in one channel.
- **claim-action-inequality** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-action)): For every $0<\sigma<1$ and all the data just specified, $$\label{eq:action-bound} \sup_{C\in\mathcal L}\mathcal E(CU) \ge\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds.$$
  - Explanation: For orthonormal rows on a bounded interval, the supremum of the action over lower triangular coefficient matrices is bounded below by an explicit sum of integrals. This is the variational input for the spectral moment bound.
- **claim-integrated-trace-comparison** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-trace)): Let $n\geq1$ and let $B,V\in\mathbb C^{n\times n}$ be Hermitian. Suppose that for some $b>0$, $$\label{eq:trace-symbol} Y_s=(sI-B)^2,\qquad X_s=Y_s+V\geq bI \qquad\text{for every }s\in\mathbb R.$$ For $t>0$, set $$\label{eq:trace-resolvents} R(t)=\frac1\pi\int_\mathbb R(X_s+tI)^{-1}\,ds, \qquad P(t)=t^{-1/2}I-R(t),$$ and define $$\label{eq:trace-N} N=c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta P(t)\,dt.$$ These integrals converge absolutely in matrix norm. If $N\geq0$, then $$\label{eq:trace-comparison} \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(NV)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q).$$
  - Explanation: For noncommuting Hermitian matrices whose resolvent symbol is uniformly positive, the trace of the integrated resolvent-difference matrix against the perturbation dominates the trace of its conjugate power, provided that matrix is positive semidefinite.
- **lemma-half-line-riccati** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-trace-half-lines)): For every $v\in\mathbb C^n$, equation <a href="#eq:trace-ode" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-ode">[eq:trace-ode]</a> has a unique $H^1$ solution on each half-line with boundary value $v$ at zero. There are Hermitian matrices $S=S(t)$ and $T=T(t)$ such that these solutions satisfy $$\label{eq:trace-boundary-maps} \nabla\psi(0+)=-Sv,\qquad \nabla\psi(0-)=Tv,$$ respectively. They obey $$\label{eq:trace-riccati} V+tI=S^2+i[B,S]=T^2-i[B,T], \qquad \frac{S+T}{2}=R(t)^{-1}.$$ Every eigenvalue of $B+iS$ has strictly positive imaginary part.
  - Explanation: The covariant equation has unique decaying solutions on both half-lines. Their boundary derivative maps are Hermitian Riccati matrices whose average is the inverse of the resolvent average, and the shifted boundary operator has spectrum in the upper half-plane.
- **lemma-field-regularity** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-field-regularity)): The limit in <a href="#eq:field-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-M">[eq:field-M]</a> exists locally uniformly on $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$, and $M_\delta:\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n\to\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ is locally Lipschitz. There is a $C^1$ function $J_\delta:\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n\to\mathbb R$, which we normalize by $J_\delta(0)=0$, such that $$\label{eq:field-primitive} dJ_\delta(B)[H]=-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M_\delta(B)H) \qquad (B,H\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n).$$
  - Explanation: The symmetric improper integral defining the regularized matrix field converges locally uniformly, the field is locally Lipschitz, and it has a scalar primitive whose differential is minus the trace pairing with the field.
- **lemma-field-properties** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-field-properties)): For every $B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ and every real unit vector $e$, $$\label{eq:field-jensen} e^{\mathsf T}M_\delta(B)e \leq\mu_\delta\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e -(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\bigr).$$ For a standard basis vector $e_i$ and $y\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:field-coordinate} Be_i=ye_i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M_\delta(B)e_i=\mu_\delta(k_i^2-y^2)e_i.$$ If $S$ is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries in $\{1,-1\}$, then $$\label{eq:field-equivariance} M_\delta(SBS)=S M_\delta(B)S.$$ Finally, $$\label{eq:field-endpoints} J_\delta(-K)-J_\delta(K) =\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{-k_i}^{k_i} \mu_\delta(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds.$$
  - Explanation: The field satisfies a directional upper bound along unit vectors, a coordinate identity on eigenvectors of diagonal type, equivariance under diagonal sign conjugation, and an explicit endpoint difference for its primitive.
- **prop-field-trace-comparison** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-field-comparison)): For every $B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ such that $M_\delta(B)\geq0$, $$\label{eq:field-error} \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(M_\delta(B)(K^2-B^2)\bigr) \geq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(M_\delta(B)^q\bigr)-E_\delta, \qquad E_\delta=n\bigl(\delta\kappa^{2\sigma} +\delta^\sigma(\kappa^2+\delta)\bigr).$$ In particular, $E_\delta\to0$ as $\delta\downarrow0$ for fixed $K$.
  - Explanation: Where the regularized field is positive semidefinite, the trace pairing with the indefinite matrix bounds the trace of the conjugate power from below, up to an explicit error that vanishes with the regularization parameter.
- **prop-connecting-paths** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-paths)): Let $n,d\geq1$, let $\Omega=(x_-,x_+)$ be a bounded interval, and let $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$ with $k_i>0$. Suppose $U\in H^1_0(\Omega;\mathbb R^{n\times d})$ satisfies $$\int_\Omega UU^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_n.$$ For every $\delta>0$ and $\varepsilon>0$, there are $C_\varepsilon\in\mathcal L$ and $B_\varepsilon\in C^1(\overline\Omega;\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n)$ such that, with $A_\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon U$, $$\label{eq:path-ode} \varepsilon B_\varepsilon' =M_\delta(B_\varepsilon)-A_\varepsilon A_\varepsilon^{\mathsf T}, \qquad B_\varepsilon(x_-)=K, \qquad B_\varepsilon(x_+)=-K.$$ These choices satisfy $$\label{eq:path-bounds} \sup_{x\in\overline\Omega}\\|B_\varepsilon(x)\\|_{\mathrm{op}} \leq\kappa, \qquad \sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq1}\\|C_\varepsilon\\|_{\mathrm{HS}}<\infty,$$ where the second bound is for fixed $\delta$, $K$, and $\Omega$.
  - Explanation: For every regularization and penalty parameter there is a lower triangular coefficient matrix and a continuously differentiable symmetric path joining the two diagonal endpoints, solving the penalized equation, with the path norm and coefficient size controlled.
- **lemma-form-compactness** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-form-compactness)): Let $m\geq1$, let $p>1$, and let $W:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C^{m\times m}$ be a measurable Hermitian positive semidefinite function satisfying $\int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)<\infty$. The form $$h_W[\psi]=\int_\mathbb R\\|\psi'\\|^2 -\int_\mathbb R\langle\psi,W\psi\rangle, \qquad \psi\in H^1(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m),$$ is closed and bounded below. Multiplication by $W^{1/2}$ is compact from $H^1(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m)$ to $L^2(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m)$. The associated self-adjoint operator has only isolated eigenvalues of finite multiplicity below zero, with possible accumulation only at zero.
  - Explanation: For potentials in the trace-power class the quadratic form is closed and bounded below, multiplication by the square root of the potential is compact, and the negative spectrum is discrete with finite multiplicities accumulating only at zero.

- **inv-matrix-bound-independent-of-size**: For every exponent in the open interval and every finite matrix size, the full sum of negative eigenvalue moments of the matrix Schrödinger operator is at most the scalar one-bound-state constant times the integral of the trace power of the potential.
- **inv-constant-optimal-every-dimension**: The constant cannot be lowered in any matrix dimension; an explicit potential with a single nonzero diagonal channel attains equality.
- **inv-action-lower-bound**: On a bounded interval, for orthonormal rows and lower triangular coefficient matrices, the supremum of the action is bounded below by the sum of the scalar beta-type integrals determined by the diagonal entries.
- **inv-integrated-trace-comparison**: For Hermitian matrices whose full symbol is uniformly positive, whenever the integrated resolvent-difference matrix is positive semidefinite, the trace of its product with the perturbation dominates the trace of its conjugate power, even without commutation.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **anti-non-hermitian-potentials**: Apply the moment bound to effective potentials with gain or loss, or to sign-indefinite matrix potentials. -> Matrix powers of the potential, the lower-bounded quadratic form and the self-adjoint spectral setting are no longer available, so the inequality has no meaning.
- **anti-endpoint-exponents**: Use the sharp constant at an endpoint exponent of the interval, or outside it. -> The theorem is only asserted on the open interval; the paper treats the endpoints by citing separate results.
- **anti-higher-dimension**: Transfer the one-bound-state constant to multi-dimensional Schrödinger problems. -> The result and its variational proof concern a single spatial variable only.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `not_applicable`. The results are inequalities and existence statements for unbounded operators on the real line, proved by compactness, continuation with boundary parity and Sard's theorem; the source gives no finite procedure whose output could be checked against an independent oracle. The constant is a closed formula, but the theorem itself is not a finite computation.

The matrices may have arbitrary rank and need not commute at different points.

<a id="sec:introduction"></a>
### Introduction

Lieb–Thirring inequalities bound moments of the negative eigenvalues of a Schrödinger operator by an integral of its potential. Their sharp constants measure how much binding is possible for a prescribed potential cost. When the wave function has several components, the potential becomes a matrix, and its eigenspaces may change with position. The question addressed here is whether this additional freedom increases the optimal constant in one dimension.

<a id="eq:potential-class"></a>
Let $m\ge1$, let $1/2<\gamma<3/2$, and put $p=\gamma+1/2$. For a measurable Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix function $W:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C^{m\times m}$ satisfying $$\label{eq:potential-class}
  \int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W(x)^p)\,dx<\infty,$$ consider the quadratic form <a id="eq:schrodinger-form"></a>$$\label{eq:schrodinger-form}
 h_W[\psi]=\int_\mathbb R\|\psi'(x)\|^2\,dx
       -\int_\mathbb R\langle\psi(x),W(x)\psi(x)\rangle\,dx,
 \qquad \psi\in H^1(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m).$$ This form is closed and bounded below. Its self-adjoint operator is denoted by $H_W=-d^2/dx^2-W$. Its negative spectrum consists of eigenvalues, with finite multiplicities and possible accumulation only at zero; these facts are proved under exactly <a href="#eq:potential-class" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:potential-class">[eq:potential-class]</a> in Section <a href="#sec:spectral" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:spectral">7</a>. We write $$\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(H_W)_-^\gamma=\sum_{\lambda_j(H_W)<0}|\lambda_j(H_W)|^\gamma,$$ initially allowing the sum to be infinite. Matrix powers are defined by spectral functional calculus, and $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}$ is the ordinary, unnormalized matrix trace.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1** (Sharp matrix inequality). *For every $1/2<\gamma<3/2$, every finite $m\ge1$, and every $W$ satisfying <a href="#eq:potential-class" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:potential-class">[eq:potential-class]</a>, <a id="eq:main-bound"></a>$$\label{eq:main-bound}
 \mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(H_W)_-^\gamma\le C_\gamma\int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W(x)^{\gamma+1/2})\,dx,
 \qquad
 C_\gamma=
 \left(\frac{\gamma-1/2}{\gamma+1/2}\right)^{\gamma-1/2}
 \frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\gamma+3/2)}.$$ The constant is optimal in every matrix dimension. With $r=(\gamma-1/2)^{-1}$, it is attained by <a id="eq:extremal-potential"></a>$$\label{eq:extremal-potential}
 W(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\big((r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx),0,\ldots,0\big).$$*

</div>

The matrices $W(x)$ may be noncommuting and may have arbitrary rank. In particular, the conclusion concerns changing internal channels as well as independent scalar channels.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
#### Historical context and relationship to prior work

Lieb and Thirring introduced their eigenvalue inequalities in the study of fermionic kinetic energy and the stability of matter <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[LiebThirring1975](#ref-LiebThirring1975), [LiebThirring1976](#ref-LiebThirring1976)]. The one-state variational problem goes back to Keller <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[Keller1961](#ref-Keller1961)]. In one dimension its optimal constant exceeds the semiclassical constant for $1/2<\gamma<3/2$. The scalar conjecture in this interval asks whether summing all negative eigenvalues changes that one-state constant. The companion paper <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Theorem 1.1] proves that it does not. The present paper establishes the same constant for arbitrary finite matrix size, allowing the eigenspaces of the potential to vary with position.

Sharp matrix inequalities at the two endpoint exponents have an established history. Weidl proved finiteness at the scalar lower endpoint <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[Weidl1996](#ref-Weidl1996)], and Hundertmark, Lieb and Thomas obtained the sharp $\gamma=1/2$ inequality with constant $1/2$ <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[HLT1998](#ref-HLT1998), Theorem 1]; Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl proved the operator-valued version <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[HLW2000](#ref-HLW2000), Theorem 3.1]. At $\gamma=3/2$, Laptev and Weidl established the sharp matrix estimate by trace identities and extended it to operator-valued potentials <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[LaptevWeidl2000](#ref-LaptevWeidl2000), Theorems 2.1 and 2.2]. Aizenman–Lieb integration in the spectral parameter gives the larger exponents <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[AizenmanLieb1978](#ref-AizenmanLieb1978)]. Laptev and Weidl’s dimension-lifting argument then yields the semiclassical constant in every spatial dimension for $\gamma\ge3/2$ <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[LaptevWeidl2000](#ref-LaptevWeidl2000), Theorem 3.1]. Benguria and Loss gave a proof of the matrix upper-endpoint estimate by Riccati factorization and commutation <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[BenguriaLoss2000](#ref-BenguriaLoss2000)].

In the open interval, Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl obtained the operator-valued upper bound with twice the semiclassical constant <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[HLW2000](#ref-HLW2000), Theorem 4.1]. Dolbeault, Laptev and Loss developed the method of orthonormal vector-valued functions at exponent one <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[DLL2008](#ref-DLL2008), Theorems 1 and 3]. More recently, Read and Schulz proved the sharp value $4/(3\sqrt3\pi)$ for $\gamma=1$, including operator-valued potentials <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[ReadSchulz2026](#ref-ReadSchulz2026), Proposition 4 and Equation (2.1)]. Thus the exponent-one case of our theorem is already contained in their result. Our additional range is $1/2<\gamma<3/2$, $\gamma\ne1$.

The proof adapts the finite-interval action and continuation framework of <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Theorem 2.1, Proposition 5.1 and Section 6]. The companion’s comparison applies when the pointwise density has rank at most one <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Proposition 4.1]. The new analytic step removes that restriction, making the action method applicable to vector-valued wave functions.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
#### The matrix comparison and the proof strategy

The proof uses the finite-interval action method of the scalar companion <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion)]. To explain the extension, choose finitely many orthonormal real vector-valued trial functions $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ and place them in the rows of a matrix function $U(x)$. A lower triangular coefficient matrix $C$ produces $A(x)=CU(x)$, so that the $i$th row of $A$ remains in the span of $u_1,\ldots,u_i$. For trial functions ordered by their negative energies, this preserves the spectral inequalities needed to bound the action from above. The action subtracts a power of the matrix density $\rho(x)=A(x)A(x)^{\mathsf T}$, and a complementary lower bound recovers the sum of the chosen eigenvalue moments. A fixed realification of the potential will subsequently give the complex Hermitian case.

The lower bound is obtained by connecting two prescribed diagonal matrices $K$ and $-K$, where the diagonal entries of $K$ are the positive square roots of the absolute values of the negative trial energies. A regularized matrix field $M$ drives an equation with the penalized constraint $M(B)=\rho$. In the scalar-potential argument, $A$ has one column and this constraint has rank at most one. For matrix potentials, $\rho$ can have any rank. The essential new estimate controls $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho^q)$ in this setting, where $q$ is conjugate to $p$.

We prove that estimate as an independent result in Section <a href="#sec:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:trace">3</a>. For Hermitian $B,V$ such that $(sI-B)^2+V$ is uniformly positive for real $s$, we form a weighted integral $N$ of resolvent differences. When $N\ge0$, the result is $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(NV)\ge\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q)$. The hypothesis permits indefinite $V$, which is necessary when $V$ is later obtained from $K^2-B^2$. Half-line solutions of a constant-coefficient differential equation give two boundary matrices. Their sum identifies the integrated resolvent, and their difference accounts for the noncommutative error. A logarithmic determinant identity and a nonnegative matrix square then prove the trace comparison with the required constant.

Section <a href="#sec:action-framework" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action-framework">2</a> states the action inequality and isolates its relation to spectral estimates. Sections <a href="#sec:field" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:field">4</a>–<a href="#sec:action-limit" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action-limit">6</a> construct the field, connect the endpoints, and remove the penalty. The continuation argument works modulo independent signs of the rows of $C$. After smoothing and perturbation, parity of the boundary of the quotient zero set forces an endpoint solution. Finally, Section <a href="#sec:spectral" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:spectral">7</a> applies the action inequality to smooth trial spaces and passes to general potentials and the full spectral moment. All of these steps are proved here; the companion is cited for the method and its scalar specialization.

<a id="pm-h-5"></a>
#### Conventions

For the rest of the paper we use <a id="eq:exponents"></a>$$\label{eq:exponents}
 \sigma=\gamma-\tfrac12\in(0,1),\qquad
 p=1+\sigma,\qquad q=1+\sigma^{-1},\qquad
 \beta=\sigma-\tfrac12.$$ Thus $p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1$. The identity matrix has the size specified by its context. On matrices we use the Hilbert–Schmidt norm $\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(A^*A)$ and the operator norm $\|A\|_{\mathop{\mathrm{op}}}$. The space $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ of real symmetric matrices carries the inner product $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(BH)$. Complex inner products are linear in the second variable.

<a id="sec:action-framework"></a>
### The finite-interval action inequality

We first formulate the lower bound on the variational action that will lead to an upper bound on spectral moments. Let $\Omega=(x_-,x_+)$ be a bounded interval, let $n,d\ge1$, and suppose <a id="eq:row-orthonormality"></a>$$\label{eq:row-orthonormality}
 U\in H^1_0(\Omega;\mathbb R^{n\times d}),\qquad
 \int_\Omega U(x)U(x)^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_n.$$ Thus the rows of $U$ are orthonormal as $\mathbb R^d$-valued functions. Fix $K=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$ with each $k_i>0$. For $A\in H^1_0(\Omega;\mathbb R^{n\times d})$ define <a id="eq:action"></a>$$\label{eq:action}
 \mathcal E(A)=\int_\Omega
 \left(\|A'\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(K^2AA^{\mathsf T})
                  -\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}((AA^{\mathsf T})^q)\right)\,dx.$$ This integral is finite, since one-dimensional $H^1$ functions on a bounded interval are continuous. Write $\mathcal L$ for the real lower triangular $n\times n$ matrices.

<a id="stmt-thm-action"></a>
<div id="thm:action" class="theorem">

**Theorem 2** (Action inequality). *For every $0<\sigma<1$ and all the data just specified, <a id="eq:action-bound"></a>$$\label{eq:action-bound}
 \sup_{C\in\mathcal L}\mathcal E(CU)
 \ge\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds.$$*

</div>

The order of the $k_i$ is immaterial for this theorem. For its spectral application we order them decreasingly: lower triangularity then preserves the nested trial spaces on which the form is at most $-k_i^2$ times the squared norm. Matrix Young duality bounds the action from above by a constant times $\int\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)$, while $$\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds
 =\mathrm B(\tfrac12,1+\sigma)k_i^{2\sigma+1}$$ has exactly the desired spectral exponent. Section <a href="#sec:spectral" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:spectral">7</a> gives this deduction, including its approximation steps.

The scalar companion proves the version with $d=1$ <a id="pm-cite-17"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Theorem 2.1]. Its field and endpoint construction also suggest the present proof. The necessary new ingredient is an inequality for positive matrix densities of unrestricted rank. We prove that comparison next, before constructing the regularized field to which it applies. The proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> is completed in Section <a href="#sec:action-limit" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action-limit">6</a>.

<a id="sec:trace"></a>
### An integrated matrix trace inequality

The matrix step in the proof compares an integrated resolvent difference with the Hermitian perturbation that produces it. We prove the comparison for matrices of arbitrary size. The perturbation may be indefinite; positivity is imposed on the entire symbol.

<a id="eq:trace-normalization"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-scaling"></a>
For the fixed exponent $0<\sigma<1$, define $c_\sigma>0$ by $$\label{eq:trace-normalization}
 c_\sigma^{-1}
 =\int_0^\infty t^\beta
       \bigl(t^{-1/2}-(t+1)^{-1/2}\bigr)\,dt.$$ Recall that $\beta=\sigma-\tfrac12\in(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$. The integrand in <a href="#eq:trace-normalization" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-normalization">[eq:trace-normalization]</a> is $O(t^{\beta-1/2})$ at zero and $O(t^{\beta-3/2})$ at infinity. The integral is therefore finite, and scaling gives $$\label{eq:trace-scaling}
 c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta
       \bigl(t^{-1/2}-(t+y)^{-1/2}\bigr)\,dt=y^\sigma,
 \qquad y\geq0.$$

<a id="stmt-thm-trace"></a>
<div id="thm:trace" class="theorem">

<a id="eq:trace-symbol"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-resolvents"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-N"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-comparison"></a>
**Theorem 3** (Integrated trace comparison). *Let $n\geq1$ and let $B,V\in\mathbb C^{n\times n}$ be Hermitian. Suppose that for some $b>0$, $$\label{eq:trace-symbol}
 Y_s=(sI-B)^2,\qquad X_s=Y_s+V\geq bI
 \qquad\text{for every }s\in\mathbb R.$$ For $t>0$, set $$\label{eq:trace-resolvents}
 R(t)=\frac1\pi\int_\mathbb R(X_s+tI)^{-1}\,ds,
 \qquad P(t)=t^{-1/2}I-R(t),$$ and define $$\label{eq:trace-N}
 N=c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta P(t)\,dt.$$ These integrals converge absolutely in matrix norm. If $N\geq0$, then $$\label{eq:trace-comparison}
                         \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(NV)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q).$$*

</div>

For a scalar $V=v>0$, shifting the variable $s$ gives $R(t)=(t+v)^{-1/2}$ and $N=v^\sigma$. Thus the two sides of <a href="#eq:trace-comparison" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-comparison">[eq:trace-comparison]</a> agree in the scalar case. To retain this constant for noncommuting matrices, we will express the difference in <a href="#eq:trace-comparison" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-comparison">[eq:trace-comparison]</a> as an integral of nonnegative terms. The first step computes the resolvent through solutions of a constant-coefficient equation on the two half-lines.

<a id="pm-h-8"></a>
#### Resolvent estimates and half-line solutions

<a id="eq:trace-P-bounds"></a>
We first verify convergence. The matrices $X_s$ and $Y_s$ grow quadratically as $|s|\to\infty$. Combined with the lower bound in <a href="#eq:trace-symbol" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-symbol">[eq:trace-symbol]</a>, this shows that $X_s^{-1}$ has an integrable matrix-norm bound on $\mathbb R$. Consequently $R(t)>0$ and $R(t)=O(1)$ as $t\downarrow0$. Diagonalizing $B$ gives $$\frac1\pi\int_\mathbb R(Y_s+tI)^{-1}\,ds=t^{-1/2}I.$$ For $t\geq1$, both $X_s+tI$ and $Y_s+tI$ are bounded below by $c(s^2+t)I$ for some $c>0$ independent of $s,t$. The resolvent identity, with the fixed matrix difference $V$, therefore gives $$\label{eq:trace-P-bounds}
 \|P(t)\|=
 \begin{cases}
  O(t^{-1/2}),&t\downarrow0,\\
  O(t^{-3/2}),&t\to\infty.
 \end{cases}$$ For the second estimate, the norm of the difference of the two resolvents is at most $C(s^2+t)^{-2}$, whose $s$-integral is $O(t^{-3/2})$. The range of $\beta$ now proves the absolute convergence in <a href="#eq:trace-N" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-N">[eq:trace-N]</a>.

<a id="eq:trace-ode"></a>
Matrix Riccati equations also underlie the commutation approach to matrix Schrödinger inequalities <a id="pm-cite-18"></a>[[BenguriaLoss2000](#ref-BenguriaLoss2000)]. Here the boundary matrices will compute both a resolvent integral and a logarithmic determinant integral. Fix $t>0$, write $\nabla=\partial_x-iB$, and consider $$\label{eq:trace-ode}
                    (-\nabla^2+V+tI)\psi=0.$$ The following construction relates its decaying solutions to $R(t)$.

<a id="stmt-lem-trace-half-lines"></a>
<div id="lem:trace-half-lines" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:trace-boundary-maps"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-riccati"></a>
**Lemma 4**. *For every $v\in\mathbb C^n$, equation <a href="#eq:trace-ode" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-ode">[eq:trace-ode]</a> has a unique $H^1$ solution on each half-line with boundary value $v$ at zero. There are Hermitian matrices $S=S(t)$ and $T=T(t)$ such that these solutions satisfy $$\label{eq:trace-boundary-maps}
 \nabla\psi(0+)=-Sv,\qquad \nabla\psi(0-)=Tv,$$ respectively. They obey $$\label{eq:trace-riccati}
 V+tI=S^2+i[B,S]=T^2-i[B,T],
 \qquad \frac{S+T}{2}=R(t)^{-1}.$$ Every eigenvalue of $B+iS$ has strictly positive imaginary part.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-trace-half-lines"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-trace-half-lines</summary>

*Proof.* With inner products linear in the second argument, let $$a_t(\phi,\psi)=\int
    \bigl(\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla\psi\rangle
       +\langle\phi,(V+tI)\psi\rangle\bigr)\,dx.$$ On the full line its Fourier symbol is $X_s+tI$, and <a href="#eq:trace-symbol" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-symbol">[eq:trace-symbol]</a> and quadratic growth give $X_s+tI\geq c_t(1+s^2)I$ for some $c_t>0$. Thus $a_t$ is coercive on $H^1(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^n)$. Zero extension proves the same assertion on the subspace of $H^1$ functions on either half-line whose boundary trace is zero.

Choose any $H^1$ extension $\eta$ of the prescribed boundary value. On the trace-zero space the form $a_t$ is an equivalent Hilbert space inner product. The Riesz representation theorem gives a unique correction $u$ in that space with $a_t(\phi,u)=-a_t(\phi,\eta)$ for every trace-zero $\phi$. Then $\psi=\eta+u$ solves <a href="#eq:trace-ode" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-ode">[eq:trace-ode]</a> weakly. Conversely, an $H^1$ solution satisfies this weak identity by density of interior test functions in the trace-zero space, so coercivity proves uniqueness.

The equation implies $\psi''\in L^2$. In particular the solutions belong to $H^2$, their first derivatives have boundary traces, and both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ tend to zero at the infinite end of the half-line. Define $S$ and $T$ by <a href="#eq:trace-boundary-maps" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-boundary-maps">[eq:trace-boundary-maps]</a>. For the right solutions with data $v,w$, integration by parts gives $a_t(\psi_v,\psi_w)=\langle v,Sw\rangle$; on the left it gives $a_t(\psi_v,\psi_w)=\langle v,Tw\rangle$. Since $a_t$ is Hermitian, both boundary matrices are Hermitian.

Translation and uniqueness imply the same derivative relations at every interior point: $$\nabla\psi=-S\psi\quad\text{on }(0,\infty),\qquad
 \nabla\psi=T\psi\quad\text{on }(-\infty,0).$$ For example, $\nabla(-S\psi)=-S\nabla\psi+i[B,S]\psi$. Substitution into <a href="#eq:trace-ode" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-ode">[eq:trace-ode]</a>, followed by evaluation at zero for arbitrary boundary data, proves the two Riccati identities in <a href="#eq:trace-riccati" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-riccati">[eq:trace-riccati]</a>.

Glue the right and left solutions with the same value $v$ at zero. Figure <a href="#fig:half-line-gluing" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:half-line-gluing">1</a> records the boundary signs and the normalization in this gluing identity. The resulting function is continuous, and its derivative has jump $-(S+T)v$. On the full line it therefore satisfies $$(-\nabla^2+V+tI)\psi=\delta_0(S+T)v.$$ Using the Fourier transform with forward kernel $e^{-isx}$ and inverting at zero gives $$v=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\mathbb R(X_s+tI)^{-1}\,ds\,(S+T)v
   =\frac12R(t)(S+T)v.$$ This inversion may first be taken in tempered distributions; the transformed function is integrable because the inverse symbol is $O(s^{-2})$, so evaluation at zero is legitimate. Since $R(t)>0$, the second identity in <a href="#eq:trace-riccati" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-riccati">[eq:trace-riccati]</a> follows.

Finally the right solutions satisfy $\psi'=i(B+iS)\psi$. If $(B+iS)v=zv$ with $v\ne0$, uniqueness for this first-order initial-value problem gives $\psi(x)=e^{izx}v$. It belongs to $L^2(0,\infty;\mathbb C^n)$, hence $\operatorname{Im}z>0$. ◻

<a id="eq:trace-LZ-identities"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-RV"></a>
Define the Hermitian matrices <a id="eq:trace-LZ-definition"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-LZ-definition}
 L=\frac{S+T}{2}=R(t)^{-1}>0,
 \qquad Z=\frac{S-T}{2}.$$ Adding and subtracting the Riccati identities gives $$\label{eq:trace-LZ-identities}
 V+tI=L^2+Z^2+i[B,Z],\qquad
 LZ+ZL+i[B,L]=0.$$ In an eigenbasis of $L$, the second identity has diagonal entries $2L_{jj}Z_{jj}=0$. Thus $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}Z=0$. Multiplying that identity on both sides by $R=L^{-1}$ gives $i[R,B]=-(ZR+RZ)$, and cyclicity of the trace yields $$i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(R[B,Z])=i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}([R,B]Z)=-2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(RZ^2).$$ Consequently $$\label{eq:trace-RV}
                 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(R(V+tI)\bigr)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}L-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(RZ^2).$$ The correction $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(RZ^2)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(ZRZ)$ is nonnegative.

We have expressed the integrated resolvent through $L$ and identified a nonnegative correction involving $Z$. To compare the weighted integral $N$ with $V$, we next need a scalar quantity whose $t$-derivative contains $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}R(t)$. The logarithm of the determinant provides that quantity, and the same half-line matrices evaluate it.

<a id="pm-h-9"></a>
#### The logarithmic integral and its weighted identity

<a id="eq:trace-F-definition"></a>
Define $$\label{eq:trace-F-definition}
 F(t)=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}V}{\sqrt t}
       -\frac1\pi\int_\mathbb R
          \log\frac{\det(X_s+tI)}{\det(Y_s+tI)}\,ds.$$ The determinants are positive. Factoring with $(Y_s+tI)^{1/2}$ shows that the log ratio is $O(|s|^{-2})$ at infinity, so its integral converges absolutely. Its $t$-derivative is the trace of the difference of the two resolvents. This has an integrable bound locally uniform for $t>0$, by the same resolvent identity used above. In particular $F$ is continuously differentiable on $(0,\infty)$.

<a id="eq:trace-factorization"></a>
The first Riccati identity gives the factorization $$\label{eq:trace-factorization}
             X_s+tI=(sI-B+iS)(sI-B-iS).$$ Let $u_j+iv_j$, $1\leq j\leq n$, be the eigenvalues of $B+iS$, with algebraic multiplicity. Lemma <a href="#lem:trace-half-lines" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:trace-half-lines">4</a> gives $v_j>0$. Since $B-iS=(B+iS)^*$, taking determinants in <a href="#eq:trace-factorization" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-factorization">[eq:trace-factorization]</a> gives $$\det(X_s+tI)=\prod_{j=1}^n\bigl((s-u_j)^2+v_j^2\bigr).$$ The denominator in <a href="#eq:trace-F-definition" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F-definition">[eq:trace-F-definition]</a> has the same form, with centers the eigenvalues of $B$ and widths all equal to $\sqrt t$.

<a id="eq:trace-F-evaluation"></a>
These centers can be removed when integrating with symmetric cutoffs. Indeed, for $h(s)=\log(s^2+v^2)$, the function $h$ is even and $h'(s)\to0$ at both ends. For a fixed translation $a$, $$\frac{d}{da}\int_{-r}^r h(s-a)\,ds=h(r+a)-h(r-a)\longrightarrow0
 \quad(r\to\infty),$$ uniformly for $a$ in bounded intervals. Integrating in $a$ shows that the translation changes the symmetric-cutoff integral by a quantity tending to zero. After centering the factors, the scalar identity $$\int_\mathbb R\log\frac{s^2+v^2}{s^2+w^2}\,ds=2\pi(v-w),
 \qquad v,w>0,$$ follows by differentiation and integration in $v$. Since $\sum_jv_j=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}S=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}L$, we obtain $$\label{eq:trace-F-evaluation}
                  F(t)=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}V}{\sqrt t}
                         -2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(L-\sqrt t I).$$

<a id="eq:trace-G-definition"></a>
<a id="eq:trace-FG-identity"></a>
Introduce a second scalar function $$\label{eq:trace-G-definition}
                 G(t)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(L-2\sqrt t I+tR(t)\bigr).$$ For each eigenvalue $r>0$ of $R(t)$, $r^{-1}-2\sqrt t+t\,r=(1-\sqrt t\,r)^2/r\geq0$, so $G(t)\geq0$. Taking traces in <a href="#eq:trace-LZ-identities" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-LZ-identities">[eq:trace-LZ-identities]</a> and using <a href="#eq:trace-F-evaluation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F-evaluation">[eq:trace-F-evaluation]</a> also gives <a id="eq:trace-F-positive"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-F-positive}
 F(t)=\frac1{\sqrt t}
        \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((L-\sqrt t I)^2+Z^2\bigr)\geq0.$$ Equations <a href="#eq:trace-RV" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-RV">[eq:trace-RV]</a> and <a href="#eq:trace-F-evaluation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F-evaluation">[eq:trace-F-evaluation]</a> now imply the exact identity $$\label{eq:trace-FG-identity}
                   \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(P(t)V)=F(t)+G(t)+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(R(t)Z^2).$$

<a id="eq:trace-F-boundary"></a>
This identity supplies all bounds needed at the improper endpoints. In fact $0\leq F(t),G(t)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(P(t)V)$, even though neither $V$ nor $P(t)$ is assumed positive. By <a href="#eq:trace-P-bounds" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-P-bounds">[eq:trace-P-bounds]</a>, both $F$ and $G$ are $O(t^{-1/2})$ at zero and $O(t^{-3/2})$ at infinity. They are therefore integrable against $t^\beta\,dt$, and $$\label{eq:trace-F-boundary}
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}t^{\beta+1}F(t)
   =\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\beta+1}F(t)=0.$$ The same identity proves weighted integrability of $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(RZ^2)$.

<a id="eq:trace-weighted-identity"></a>
Differentiating <a href="#eq:trace-F-definition" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F-definition">[eq:trace-F-definition]</a> gives $$F'(t)=-\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}V}{2t^{3/2}}
          -\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(R(t)-t^{-1/2}I\bigr).$$ Substitution from <a href="#eq:trace-F-evaluation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F-evaluation">[eq:trace-F-evaluation]</a> and <a href="#eq:trace-G-definition" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-G-definition">[eq:trace-G-definition]</a> yields $tF'(t)=-F(t)/2-G(t)$. Integrate the derivative of $t^{\beta+1}F(t)$ on a compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$, then use <a href="#eq:trace-F-boundary" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F-boundary">[eq:trace-F-boundary]</a> to pass to the endpoints. Since $\beta+\tfrac12=\sigma$, the result is <a id="eq:trace-weighted-balance"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-weighted-balance}
 \int_0^\infty t^\beta G(t)\,dt
       =\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta F(t)\,dt.$$ In particular, integrating <a href="#eq:trace-FG-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-FG-identity">[eq:trace-FG-identity]</a> gives $$\label{eq:trace-weighted-identity}
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(NV)
   =q c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta G(t)\,dt
      +c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(R(t)Z^2)\,dt.$$

The half-line calculation has thus reduced the theorem to a bound on the weighted integral of $G$. The last step compares $G(t)$ with a scalar square-root expression evaluated on an arbitrary positive semidefinite matrix. Choosing that matrix in terms of $N$ will produce the exponent $q$ with no loss in the constant.

<a id="pm-h-10"></a>
#### Completion of the trace comparison

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-trace-half-lines-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-trace-half-lines</summary>

<a id="eq:trace-F0-integral"></a>
*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:trace">3</a>.* The convergence assertions were proved above. Let $D\geq0$ be an $n\times n$ matrix and define $$F_0(t,D)=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}D}{\sqrt t}
             -2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((tI+D)^{1/2}-\sqrt t I\bigr).$$ For a scalar eigenvalue $d\geq0$, its contribution to $F_0$ is $$\frac d{\sqrt t}-2\bigl(\sqrt{t+d}-\sqrt t\bigr)
      =\int_0^d\bigl(t^{-1/2}-(t+y)^{-1/2}\bigr)\,dy.$$ The integrand is nonnegative. Tonelli’s theorem and <a href="#eq:trace-scaling" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-scaling">[eq:trace-scaling]</a>, applied to each eigenvalue of $D$, give $$\label{eq:trace-F0-integral}
 c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta F_0(t,D)\,dt
                    =\frac1p\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(D^p).$$

<a id="eq:trace-duality"></a>
For $R=R(t)>0$, the Hilbert–Schmidt square <a id="eq:trace-square"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal D_D(t)
  &:=\bigl\|R^{-1/2}-R^{1/2}(tI+D)^{1/2}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
       \notag\\
  &=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(R^{-1}+R(tI+D)-2(tI+D)^{1/2}\bigr)\geq0
       \label{eq:trace-square}
\end{aligned}$$ is valid without a commutation assumption. Indeed the cross term is $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(tI+D)^{1/2}$ because the adjacent factors $R^{-1/2}R^{1/2}$ cancel, while cyclicity puts the remaining quadratic term in the form $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(R(tI+D))$. Using the definitions of $P,G,F_0$, the same square reads $$\mathcal D_D(t)=G(t)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(P(t)D)+F_0(t,D).$$ All three terms on the right are integrable against $t^\beta\,dt$, by <a href="#eq:trace-P-bounds" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-P-bounds">[eq:trace-P-bounds]</a>, <a href="#eq:trace-FG-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-FG-identity">[eq:trace-FG-identity]</a>, and <a href="#eq:trace-F0-integral" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-F0-integral">[eq:trace-F0-integral]</a>. Hence $$\label{eq:trace-duality}
 c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta G(t)\,dt
  =\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(ND)-\frac1p\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(D^p)
      +c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta\mathcal D_D(t)\,dt.$$

<a id="eq:trace-deficit"></a>
Now assume $N\geq0$ and choose $D=N^{1/\sigma}$. Since $p=1+\sigma$ and $q=p/\sigma$, we have $$\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(ND)-\frac1p\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(D^p)=\frac1q\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q).$$ Combining <a href="#eq:trace-duality" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-duality">[eq:trace-duality]</a> with <a href="#eq:trace-weighted-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-weighted-identity">[eq:trace-weighted-identity]</a> gives the exact deficit formula $$\label{eq:trace-deficit}
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(NV)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q)
   =c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta
       \bigl(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(R(t)Z(t)^2)
                 +q\,\mathcal D_{N^{1/\sigma}}(t)\bigr)\,dt.$$ Both integrands are nonnegative, proving <a href="#eq:trace-comparison" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-comparison">[eq:trace-comparison]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-rem-trace-commuting"></a>
<div id="rem:trace-commuting" class="remark">

*Remark 5*. If $B$ and $V$ commute, condition <a href="#eq:trace-symbol" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-symbol">[eq:trace-symbol]</a> implies $V\geq bI$, and simultaneous diagonalization gives $R(t)=(tI+V)^{-1/2}$ and $N=V^\sigma$. Then $Z=0$ and $\mathcal D_{N^{1/\sigma}}=0$, so <a href="#eq:trace-deficit" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-deficit">[eq:trace-deficit]</a> vanishes. In general the two nonnegative terms in that formula retain the comparison despite the absence of a common eigenbasis.

</div>

<a id="sec:field"></a>
### A regularized matrix field

Fix a positive diagonal matrix $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$ from the finite-interval action problem, and put $\kappa=\max_i k_i$. We construct a field $M_\delta$ on $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$, the real vector space of symmetric $n\times n$ matrices, and a scalar primitive $J_\delta$ for its negative. The difference $J_\delta(-K)-J_\delta(K)$ will give the boundary contribution in the action argument. The other required property is a lower bound for $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M_\delta(B)(K^2-B^2))$ whenever $M_\delta(B)$ is positive semidefinite. The trace comparison of Theorem <a href="#thm:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:trace">3</a> will supply this bound.

The construction of the field and its primitive, together with the scalar directional estimates, follows <a id="pm-cite-19"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Section 3]. We use a resolvent normalization that identifies the field directly with the integrated matrix in Theorem <a href="#thm:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:trace">3</a>; this gives the comparison for positive values of arbitrary rank in Proposition <a href="#prop:field-comparison" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:field-comparison">8</a>.

Throughout this section $\delta>0$ is fixed. We use the parameters $0<\sigma<1$, $p=1+\sigma$, $q=1+1/\sigma$, and $\beta=\sigma-1/2$, and the normalization $c_\sigma$ from <a href="#eq:trace-normalization" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-normalization">[eq:trace-normalization]</a>.

<a id="pm-h-12"></a>
#### Construction and regularity

<a id="eq:field-f"></a>
<a id="eq:field-f-derivative"></a>
For $y\geq0$, define $$\label{eq:field-f}
 f_\delta(y)=\frac{c_\sigma}{\pi}\int_0^\infty t^\beta
 \left(\frac1{t+\delta}-\frac1{t+\delta+y}\right)\,dt.$$ For $y<0$, set $f_\delta(y)=f_\delta'(0)y$, the tangent-line extension at zero. Differentiation under the integral and the change of variable $t=(y+\delta)u$ give $$\label{eq:field-f-derivative}
 f_\delta'(y)=a_\sigma(y+\delta)^{\beta-1},\qquad
 a_\sigma=\frac{c_\sigma}{\pi}
       \int_0^\infty\frac{u^\beta}{(1+u)^2}\,du>0
       \quad (y\geq0).$$ The integrals converge because $-1/2<\beta<1/2$. Thus $f_\delta$ is $C^1$, strictly increasing, and concave on $\mathbb R$. Its extension permits us to apply it to every real symmetric matrix, including matrices with negative eigenvalues.

<a id="eq:field-M"></a>
For $s\in\mathbb R$ and $B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$, let <a id="eq:field-Q"></a>$$\label{eq:field-Q}
 Q_s(B)=s^2I-2sB+K^2.$$ We define the field by a symmetric improper integral: $$\label{eq:field-M}
 M_\delta(B)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{-r}^r
       \bigl(f_\delta(Q_s(B))-f_\delta(s^2)I\bigr)\,ds.$$ The symmetric cutoff cancels the leading term that is odd in $s$. The next lemma makes this cancellation quantitative and provides the primitive used in the action identity.

<a id="stmt-lem-field-regularity"></a>
<div id="lem:field-regularity" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:field-primitive"></a>
**Lemma 6**. *The limit in <a href="#eq:field-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-M">[eq:field-M]</a> exists locally uniformly on $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$, and $M_\delta:\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n\to\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ is locally Lipschitz. There is a $C^1$ function $J_\delta:\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n\to\mathbb R$, which we normalize by $J_\delta(0)=0$, such that $$\label{eq:field-primitive}
 dJ_\delta(B)[H]=-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M_\delta(B)H)
       \qquad (B,H\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-field-regularity"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-field-regularity</summary>

<a id="eq:field-HS-calculus"></a>
*Proof.* We first recall a useful estimate for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm. If $h$ is scalar Lipschitz on an interval containing the spectra of Hermitian matrices $X$ and $Y$, then $$\label{eq:field-HS-calculus}
 \|h(X)-h(Y)\|_\mathrm{HS}\leq\operatorname{Lip}(h)\|X-Y\|_\mathrm{HS}.$$ Indeed, in eigenbases $(e_i)$ of $X$ and $(v_j)$ of $Y$, the $(i,j)$ entries of these two differences are respectively $(h(\lambda_i)-h(\nu_j))\langle e_i,v_j\rangle$ and $(\lambda_i-\nu_j)\langle e_i,v_j\rangle$. The scalar Lipschitz bound, followed by summation of their squared absolute values, proves <a href="#eq:field-HS-calculus" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-HS-calculus">[eq:field-HS-calculus]</a>.

<a id="eq:field-tail"></a>
Restrict $B$ to a bounded set. For sufficiently large $|s|$, the spectrum of $E_s(B)=-2sB+K^2$ lies in $[-a|s|,a|s|]$, with a constant $a$ independent of $B$. On this interval put $$h_s(y)=f_\delta(s^2+y)-f_\delta(s^2)-f_\delta'(s^2)y.$$ By <a href="#eq:field-f-derivative" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-f-derivative">[eq:field-f-derivative]</a>, $$\sup_{|y|\leq a|s|}|h_s'(y)|=O(|s|^{2\beta-3}).$$ Here the arguments $s^2+y$ are positive for large $|s|$, so the derivative formula applies. Since $h_s(0)=0$ and $\|E_s(B)\|_\mathrm{HS}=O(|s|)$, <a href="#eq:field-HS-calculus" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-HS-calculus">[eq:field-HS-calculus]</a> gives $$\label{eq:field-tail}
 f_\delta(Q_s(B))-f_\delta(s^2)I
 =-2s f_\delta'(s^2)B+T_s(B),$$ where, uniformly on the chosen bounded set, $$\|T_s(B)\|_\mathrm{HS}=O(|s|^{2\beta-2}),\qquad
 \|T_s(B_1)-T_s(B_2)\|_\mathrm{HS}
 \leq C|s|^{2\beta-2}\|B_1-B_2\|_\mathrm{HS}.$$ For the first bound, include the term $f_\delta'(s^2)K^2$ in $T_s$; for the second, use $E_s(B_1)-E_s(B_2)=-2s(B_1-B_2)$. The first term in <a href="#eq:field-tail" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-tail">[eq:field-tail]</a> integrates to zero on $[-r,r]$, while $2\beta-2=2\sigma-3<-1$ makes both remainder bounds integrable at infinity. On bounded $s$-intervals, <a href="#eq:field-HS-calculus" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-HS-calculus">[eq:field-HS-calculus]</a> and the scalar Lipschitz bound on $f_\delta$ give local Lipschitz continuity directly. This proves the convergence and regularity assertions.

To construct the primitive, choose $g_\delta$ with $g_\delta'=f_\delta$. The trace differentiation formula is $$d\bigl(\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}g_\delta(X)\bigr)[H]
       =\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(f_\delta(X)H).$$ It follows first for polynomials by cyclicity of the trace and then for $g_\delta$ by polynomial approximation in $C^1$ on compact intervals containing the relevant spectra. For $s\ne0$, the function $$B\longmapsto
 \frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(g_\delta(Q_s(B))-g_\delta(K^2)\bigr)}{2s}
      +f_\delta(s^2)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}B$$ therefore has differential $-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}((f_\delta(Q_s(B))-f_\delta(s^2)I)H)$. At $s=0$ the field is constant and has a linear primitive. It follows that the integral of the negative field around every piecewise smooth closed curve is zero at each finite cutoff in <a href="#eq:field-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-M">[eq:field-M]</a>. Local uniform convergence passes this identity to $M_\delta$. Path integration on the vector space $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ now defines $J_\delta$, with <a href="#eq:field-primitive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-primitive">[eq:field-primitive]</a>; continuity of $M_\delta$ gives $J_\delta\in C^1$. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-13"></a>
#### Scalar shifts, matrix directions, and endpoints

<a id="eq:field-mu"></a>
<a id="eq:field-mu-positive"></a>
The scalar function associated with the field is $$\label{eq:field-mu}
 \mu_\delta(z)=\int_\mathbb R
       \bigl(f_\delta(s^2+z)-f_\delta(s^2)\bigr)\,ds,
       \qquad z\in\mathbb R.$$ This integral is absolutely convergent: on bounded sets of $z$, its integrand and its Lipschitz constant in $z$ are $O(|s|^{2\beta-2})$ at infinity. Consequently $\mu_\delta$ is locally Lipschitz. Strict increase of $f_\delta$ implies that $\mu_\delta$ is strictly increasing, and $\mu_\delta(0)=0$. For $z\geq0$, Tonelli’s theorem and $\pi^{-1}\int_\mathbb R(s^2+a)^{-1}\,ds=a^{-1/2}$ give $$\begin{aligned}
 \mu_\delta(z)
 &=c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta
       \bigl((t+\delta)^{-1/2}-(t+\delta+z)^{-1/2}\bigr)\,dt
       \notag\\
 &=(z+\delta)^\sigma-\delta^\sigma.
       \label{eq:field-mu-positive}
\end{aligned}$$ The last equality is the difference of <a href="#eq:trace-scaling" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-scaling">[eq:trace-scaling]</a> at $y=\delta+z$ and $y=\delta$.

<a id="eq:field-shift"></a>
We will also translate $s$ inside the first term of <a href="#eq:field-mu" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-mu">[eq:field-mu]</a>. To justify this when the individual terms are not integrable, fix $z\in\mathbb R$ and set $F_z(s)=f_\delta(s^2+z)$. This function is even, and $$F_z'(s)=O(|s|^{2\beta-1})\longrightarrow0
             \quad\text{as }|s|\longrightarrow\infty.$$ For a fixed $a\in\mathbb R$ and $r>|a|$, evenness gives the bound $$\left|\int_{-r}^r\bigl(F_z(s-a)-F_z(s)\bigr)\,ds\right|
 \leq |a|^2
       \sup_{r-|a|\leq u\leq r+|a|}|F_z'(u)|.$$ For example, when $a\geq0$, the difference consists of the integral over $[r,r+a]$ minus the integral over $[r-a,r]$; pairing these intervals proves the estimate. Its right-hand side tends to zero. Thus $$\label{eq:field-shift}
 \lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{-r}^r
  \bigl(f_\delta((s-a)^2+z)-f_\delta(s^2)\bigr)\,ds
       =\mu_\delta(z).$$

These scalar identities control the field in each matrix direction. They also identify its primitive on the diagonal matrices, where the two endpoints $K$ and $-K$ lie.

<a id="stmt-lem-field-properties"></a>
<div id="lem:field-properties" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:field-jensen"></a>
<a id="eq:field-coordinate"></a>
<a id="eq:field-equivariance"></a>
<a id="eq:field-endpoints"></a>
**Lemma 7**. *For every $B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ and every real unit vector $e$, $$\label{eq:field-jensen}
 e^{\mathsf T}M_\delta(B)e
 \leq\mu_\delta\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e
                         -(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\bigr).$$ For a standard basis vector $e_i$ and $y\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:field-coordinate}
 Be_i=ye_i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 M_\delta(B)e_i=\mu_\delta(k_i^2-y^2)e_i.$$ If $S$ is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries in $\{1,-1\}$, then $$\label{eq:field-equivariance}
 M_\delta(SBS)=S M_\delta(B)S.$$ Finally, $$\label{eq:field-endpoints}
 J_\delta(-K)-J_\delta(K)
   =\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}
                    \mu_\delta(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-field-properties"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-field-properties</summary>

*Proof.* Scalar concavity, applied in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $Q_s(B)$, gives $$e^{\mathsf T}f_\delta(Q_s(B))e
      \leq f_\delta(e^{\mathsf T}Q_s(B)e).$$ Set $a=e^{\mathsf T}Be$ and $z=e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-a^2$. Then $e^{\mathsf T}Q_s(B)e=(s-a)^2+z$. Subtract $f_\delta(s^2)$, integrate over $[-r,r]$, and use <a href="#eq:field-shift" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-shift">[eq:field-shift]</a> to obtain <a href="#eq:field-jensen" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-jensen">[eq:field-jensen]</a>.

If $Be_i=ye_i$, the vector $e_i$ is also an eigenvector of $Q_s(B)$, with eigenvalue $(s-y)^2+k_i^2-y^2$. Functional calculus and <a href="#eq:field-shift" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-shift">[eq:field-shift]</a> prove <a href="#eq:field-coordinate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-coordinate">[eq:field-coordinate]</a>. For <a href="#eq:field-equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-equivariance">[eq:field-equivariance]</a>, use $SK^2S=K^2$ to obtain $Q_s(SBS)=SQ_s(B)S$, and then conjugate inside <a href="#eq:field-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-M">[eq:field-M]</a>.

On the diagonal subspace, <a href="#eq:field-coordinate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-coordinate">[eq:field-coordinate]</a> and <a href="#eq:field-primitive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-primitive">[eq:field-primitive]</a> give $$dJ_\delta(\operatorname{diag}(b_1,\ldots,b_n))
       =-\sum_{i=1}^n\mu_\delta(k_i^2-b_i^2)\,db_i.$$ Integrating from $(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$ to $(-k_1,\ldots,-k_n)$ within this subspace proves <a href="#eq:field-endpoints" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-endpoints">[eq:field-endpoints]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-14"></a>
#### The trace estimate at positive values of the field

We have constructed a locally Lipschitz field and computed the boundary difference of its primitive. It remains to connect this field with Theorem <a href="#thm:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:trace">3</a>. Positivity of $M_\delta(B)$ will imply positivity of every $Q_s(B)$, making the resolvent representation available even though $K^2-B^2$ can be indefinite.

<a id="stmt-prop-field-comparison"></a>
<div id="prop:field-comparison" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:field-error"></a>
**Proposition 8**. *For every $B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ such that $M_\delta(B)\geq0$, $$\label{eq:field-error}
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(M_\delta(B)(K^2-B^2)\bigr)
       \geq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl(M_\delta(B)^q\bigr)-E_\delta,
 \qquad
 E_\delta=n\bigl(\delta\kappa^{2\sigma}
                 +\delta^\sigma(\kappa^2+\delta)\bigr).$$ In particular, $E_\delta\to0$ as $\delta\downarrow0$ for fixed $K$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-field-comparison"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-field-comparison</summary>

<a id="eq:field-M-bound"></a>
*Proof.* Write $M=M_\delta(B)$. By <a href="#eq:field-jensen" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-jensen">[eq:field-jensen]</a>, its positivity and the strict increase of $\mu_\delta$ imply $$e^{\mathsf T}K^2e\geq(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2
                 \qquad\text{for every real unit vector }e.$$ Consequently $$e^{\mathsf T}Q_s(B)e
  =(s-e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2
       +e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\geq0,$$ and hence $Q_s(B)\geq0$ for all $s\in\mathbb R$. This is positivity also on $\mathbb C^n$: for $z=u+iv$ with real $u,v$, symmetry gives $z^*Q_s(B)z=u^{\mathsf T}Q_s(B)u+v^{\mathsf T}Q_s(B)v$. The same directional estimate, together with <a href="#eq:field-mu-positive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-mu-positive">[eq:field-mu-positive]</a>, gives $$\label{eq:field-M-bound}
 0\leq M\leq\mu_\delta(\kappa^2)I
          \leq\kappa^{2\sigma}I.$$ The last inequality uses $(a+b)^\sigma\leq a^\sigma+b^\sigma$ for $a,b\geq0$ and $0<\sigma<1$.

<a id="eq:field-N"></a>
Put $$D=K^2-B^2,\qquad V=D+\delta I,\qquad Y_s=(sI-B)^2.$$ Then $X_s=Y_s+V=Q_s(B)+\delta I\geq\delta I$ satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem <a href="#thm:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:trace">3</a>. Let $R(t)$ and $N$ be its resolvent average and integrated matrix, as defined in <a href="#eq:trace-resolvents" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-resolvents">[eq:trace-resolvents]</a> and <a href="#eq:trace-N" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-N">[eq:trace-N]</a>. We claim that $$\label{eq:field-N}
 N=M+\delta^\sigma I.$$

Diagonalize $B$ and apply <a href="#eq:field-shift" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-shift">[eq:field-shift]</a> with $z=0$ to each diagonal entry. This allows $f_\delta(s^2)I$ in <a href="#eq:field-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-M">[eq:field-M]</a> to be replaced by $f_\delta(Y_s)$. Both $Q_s(B)$ and $Y_s$ are positive semidefinite, so <a href="#eq:field-f" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-f">[eq:field-f]</a> expresses the resulting difference as $$\frac{c_\sigma}{\pi}\int_0^\infty t^\beta
  \left((Y_s+(t+\delta)I)^{-1}
       -(Q_s(B)+(t+\delta)I)^{-1}\right)\,dt.$$ For fixed $B$ and $\delta>0$, positivity and quadratic growth in $s$ give a common lower bound $c(1+t+s^2)I$ for the two matrices being inverted. Their difference before inversion is the constant matrix $D$. The resolvent identity therefore bounds the norm of the weighted difference by $$C\|D\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,t^\beta(1+t+s^2)^{-2}.$$ This is integrable on $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$: integration in $s$ leaves a constant times $t^\beta(1+t)^{-3/2}$, integrable because $-1<\beta<1/2$. Fubini’s theorem now yields $$M=c_\sigma\int_0^\infty t^\beta
          \bigl((t+\delta)^{-1/2}I-R(t)\bigr)\,dt.$$ Subtract this identity from the definition of $N$ and apply <a href="#eq:trace-scaling" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace-scaling">[eq:trace-scaling]</a> with $y=\delta$. This proves <a href="#eq:field-N" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-N">[eq:field-N]</a>.

In particular $N\geq0$, so Theorem <a href="#thm:trace" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:trace">3</a> applies and gives $$\begin{aligned}
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(MD)
 &=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(NV)-\delta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}M-\delta^\sigma\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}V\\
 &\geq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q)-\delta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}M-\delta^\sigma\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}V.
\end{aligned}$$ Because $N=M+\delta^\sigma I$, the matrices $N$ and $M$ have a common eigenbasis and $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(N^q)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M^q)$. Equation <a href="#eq:field-M-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-M-bound">[eq:field-M-bound]</a> bounds $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}M$ by $n\kappa^{2\sigma}$, while $V=K^2-B^2+\delta I\leq(\kappa^2+\delta)I$ bounds $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}V$ by $n(\kappa^2+\delta)$. Substitution proves <a href="#eq:field-error" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-error">[eq:field-error]</a>. ◻

</details>

In particular, if a matrix $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ satisfies $M_\delta(B)=AA^{\mathsf T}$, then Proposition <a href="#prop:field-comparison" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:field-comparison">8</a> controls the nonlinear term $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}((AA^{\mathsf T})^q)$ in the action. The next section constructs the connecting paths. In Section <a href="#sec:action-limit" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action-limit">6</a>, their action identities penalize the squared residual $\|M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T}\|_\mathrm{HS}^2$ by a factor tending to infinity. Compactness of pairs of matrices then reduces the estimate to the exact constraint.

<a id="sec:paths"></a>
### A matrix path with prescribed endpoints

The construction in this section adapts the connecting-path argument of <a id="pm-cite-20"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Proposition 5.1] to a rectangular matrix $A$. The action estimate uses a path from $K$ to $-K$ and penalizes failure of $M_\delta(B)=AA^{\mathsf T}$, with $A=CU$ and $C$ lower triangular. For a penalty parameter $\varepsilon>0$, we prescribe the difference $M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T}$ as $\varepsilon B'$ through an ordinary differential equation and choose $C$ to obtain the second endpoint. The choice is made by continuation and boundary parity. At the initial parameter there is exactly one solution after identifying matrices that differ by row signs. If no terminal solution existed, smoothing and a small perturbation respecting these signs would produce a quotient zero set that is a finite disjoint union of circles and intervals, with exactly one boundary point. Circles have no boundary and intervals contribute two boundary points, giving a contradiction.

As in Section <a href="#sec:action-framework" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action-framework">2</a>, $\mathcal L$ is the vector space of real lower triangular $n\times n$ matrices. Set $\kappa=\max_i k_i$.

<a id="stmt-prop-paths"></a>
<div id="prop:paths" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:path-ode"></a>
<a id="eq:path-bounds"></a>
**Proposition 9** (Connecting paths). *Let $n,d\geq1$, let $\Omega=(x_-,x_+)$ be a bounded interval, and let $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$ with $k_i>0$. Suppose $U\in H^1_0(\Omega;\mathbb R^{n\times d})$ satisfies $$\int_\Omega UU^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_n.$$ For every $\delta>0$ and $\varepsilon>0$, there are $C_\varepsilon\in\mathcal L$ and $B_\varepsilon\in C^1(\overline\Omega;\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n)$ such that, with $A_\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon U$, $$\label{eq:path-ode}
    \varepsilon B_\varepsilon'
       =M_\delta(B_\varepsilon)-A_\varepsilon A_\varepsilon^{\mathsf T},
    \qquad
    B_\varepsilon(x_-)=K,
    \qquad B_\varepsilon(x_+)=-K.$$ These choices satisfy $$\label{eq:path-bounds}
    \sup_{x\in\overline\Omega}\|B_\varepsilon(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}
       \leq\kappa,
    \qquad
    \sup_{0<\varepsilon\leq1}\|C_\varepsilon\|_{\mathrm{HS}}<\infty,$$ where the second bound is for fixed $\delta$, $K$, and $\Omega$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-paths"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-paths</summary>

*Proof.* Fix $\delta>0$. We use the continuous representative of $U$, available because the interval is bounded and $U\in H^1$. Choose a smooth nonnegative function $\chi$ on $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$, of compact support, which is one on $\{B:\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa\}$ and is invariant under conjugation by diagonal sign matrices. Such a cutoff can be obtained from any cutoff of a neighborhood of this compact set by averaging over the finitely many sign matrices. By Lemma <a href="#lem:field-regularity" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:field-regularity">6</a>, the field $$\widehat M_\delta(B)=\chi(B)M_\delta(B)$$ is bounded and globally Lipschitz.

<a id="eq:path-homotopy"></a>
*The endpoint map.* For fixed $\varepsilon>0$, $C\in\mathcal L$, and $0\leq\theta\leq1$, solve the initial-value problem $$\label{eq:path-homotopy}
    \varepsilon B'
       =\theta\widehat M_\delta(B)-(CU)(CU)^{\mathsf T},
    \qquad B(x_-)=K,$$ and define $$\Phi(C,\theta)=B(x_+)+K\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n.$$ The bounded Lipschitz field and the continuous forcing give a unique $C^1$ solution on the whole interval. Continuous dependence on the parameters makes $\Phi$ continuous. A zero of $\Phi(\cdot,1)$ gives the required endpoints; we will then show that its path remains where $\chi=1$.

Let $\mathcal G$ be the group of diagonal matrices whose entries are $\pm1$. It acts on $\mathcal L$ by $C\mapsto SC$ and on $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ by $B\mapsto SBS$. The field is equivariant under these actions by <a href="#eq:field-equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-equivariance">[eq:field-equivariance]</a>, and $SKS=K$. Uniqueness in <a href="#eq:path-homotopy" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-homotopy">[eq:path-homotopy]</a> therefore gives <a id="eq:path-equivariance"></a>$$\label{eq:path-equivariance}
    \Phi(SC,\theta)=S\Phi(C,\theta)S
    \qquad(S\in\mathcal G).$$

<a id="eq:path-C-bound"></a>
All zeros of this endpoint map lie in a bounded set. Indeed, integrating <a href="#eq:path-homotopy" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-homotopy">[eq:path-homotopy]</a> at a zero and using the orthonormality of the rows of $U$ yields <a id="eq:path-integrated"></a>$$\label{eq:path-integrated}
    CC^{\mathsf T}
       =2\varepsilon K
          +\theta\int_\Omega\widehat M_\delta(B(x))\,dx.$$ Consequently, for $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, $$\label{eq:path-C-bound}
    \|C\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
       \leq 2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}K
          +n|\Omega|\sup_{B\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n}
                    \|\widehat M_\delta(B)\|_{\mathrm{op}}.$$ For each fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the same calculation gives a finite bound. Thus the joint zero set $\{(C,\theta):\Phi(C,\theta)=0\}$ is compact.

The sign action is *free* at every such zero: only the identity sign matrix fixes $C$. To prove this, it suffices to show that no row of $C$ vanishes. If row $i$ vanished, the reflection $S_i$ that changes only its sign would fix $C$. Equivariance and uniqueness would then force $S_iB(x)S_i=B(x)$, so that $B(x)e_i=y(x)e_i$ for a scalar function $y$. The coordinate identity <a href="#eq:field-coordinate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-coordinate">[eq:field-coordinate]</a> would give $$\varepsilon y'
       =\theta\chi(B(x))\mu_\delta(k_i^2-y^2),
    \qquad y(x_-)=k_i.$$ Here $\chi(B(x))$ is a fixed continuous coefficient. Since $\mu_\delta$ is locally Lipschitz and $\mu_\delta(0)=0$, uniqueness for this scalar equation gives $y\equiv k_i$. This contradicts the terminal condition $y(x_+)=-k_i$.

<a id="eq:path-initial"></a>
At $\theta=0$ the endpoint map is explicit: $$\label{eq:path-initial}
    \Phi(C,0)=2K-\varepsilon^{-1}CC^{\mathsf T}.$$ Its zeros in $\mathcal L$ are precisely $$C=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n),
    \qquad d_i=\pm\sqrt{2\varepsilon k_i}.$$ For example, the first row of $CC^{\mathsf T}=2\varepsilon K$ determines $d_1$ and forces the remaining entries in the first column of $C$ to vanish; induction gives the claim. These $2^n$ zeros form one orbit under $\mathcal G$. Each is a regular zero, meaning that the derivative with respect to $C$ is onto $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$. In fact, for a lower triangular variation $H$, this derivative has diagonal entries $-2\varepsilon^{-1}d_iH_{ii}$ and entries $-\varepsilon^{-1}d_jH_{ij}$ when $i>j$. It is therefore an isomorphism between vector spaces of dimension $n(n+1)/2$.

*Continuation after identifying row signs.* We have a compact zero set, lying entirely where the sign action is free, and a single regular zero at the initial parameter after taking the quotient by that action. We now show that these properties force a zero at $\theta=1$. The details below replace the continuous endpoint map by a smooth one, so no differentiability of the ODE flow with respect to $C$ is required.

<a id="eq:path-gap-set"></a>
Suppose, to the contrary, that $\Phi(C,1)$ never vanishes. Compactness allows us to choose a bounded invariant open set $O\subset\mathcal L$ containing the $C$-projection of every zero, with $\overline O$ contained in the open set where all rows are nonzero. There is then a positive lower bound for $\|\Phi\|_{\mathrm{HS}}$ on $$\label{eq:path-gap-set}
    (\partial O\times[0,1])\ \cup\ (\overline O\times\{1\}).$$ Reparametrize $\theta$ by a smooth map $\rho:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ that equals zero near $0$ and has $\rho(1)=1$. The resulting map $F(C,\theta)=\Phi(C,\rho(\theta))$ agrees with the smooth map <a href="#eq:path-initial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-initial">[eq:path-initial]</a> on an initial collar, that is, for all $0\leq\theta\leq a$ with some $a>0$. It retains a positive gap on <a href="#eq:path-gap-set" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-gap-set">[eq:path-gap-set]</a>.

Approximate $F$ uniformly on $\overline O\times[0,1]$ by a smooth equivariant map $\Psi$ that still equals <a href="#eq:path-initial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-initial">[eq:path-initial]</a> on a smaller initial collar. Here is a direct construction. Extend $F$ constantly in $\theta$ outside $[0,1]$, smooth by convolution in the finite-dimensional variables, and use a smooth function of $\theta$ to splice the approximation to $F(C,0)$ inside its initial collar. Averaging the result in the form $$\frac1{|\mathcal G|}\sum_{S\in\mathcal G}
       S\Psi_0(SC,\theta)S$$ makes it equivariant. Uniform approximation can be as close as needed, so the positive gap persists.

We next make zero a regular value in the interior by a small equivariant perturbation. The freeness of the action on $\overline O$ is exactly what permits this step. Around any $C_0\in\overline O$, choose a ball whose translates by distinct sign matrices are disjoint, and a smooth bump function $b$ supported in that ball with $b(C_0)=1$. For $H\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$, the map $$V_H(C)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal G} b(SC)SHS$$ is smooth and equivariant, and $V_H(C_0)=H$. Taking $H$ in a basis of $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ gives maps whose values span the target near $C_0$. A finite covering of $\overline O$ therefore supplies smooth equivariant maps $V_1,\ldots,V_N$ whose values span $\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$ at every point of $\overline O$. The action on the target need not be free: the disjoint supports in the domain allow an arbitrary target value at a representative of each orbit.

Choose a smooth function $h:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)$ that vanishes near $0$ and is positive thereafter, with its zero set contained in the collar where $\Psi(C,\theta)=\Phi(C,0)$. For a parameter $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_N)\in\mathbb R^N$, put $$\Psi_\alpha(C,\theta)
       =\Psi(C,\theta)+h(\theta)\sum_{j=1}^N\alpha_jV_j(C).$$ Restrict $\alpha$ to a sufficiently small open ball about zero so that the gap on <a href="#eq:path-gap-set" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-gap-set">[eq:path-gap-set]</a> remains. At an interior zero of the map of all three variables $(C,\theta,\alpha)$, its differential is onto: if $h(\theta)>0$, the parameter directions span the target; if $h(\theta)=0$, the derivative in $C$ is the isomorphism already computed for <a href="#eq:path-initial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-initial">[eq:path-initial]</a>.

The interior zero set of this parameter family is thus a smooth manifold. Apply Sard’s theorem <a id="pm-cite-21"></a>[[Sard1942](#ref-Sard1942)] to its projection onto the parameter ball. For a regular value $\alpha$ of this projection, zero is a regular value of $(C,\theta)\mapsto\Psi_\alpha(C,\theta)$. To see the implication, write the differential of the parameter family as $(L_1,L_2)$ in the $(C,\theta)$ and $\alpha$ directions. Surjectivity of the differential of the projection means that, for every $w$, there is a $v$ with $L_1v+L_2w=0$. Thus $\operatorname{ran}L_2\subset\operatorname{ran}L_1$; since $(L_1,L_2)$ is onto, $L_1$ is onto as well. Sard’s theorem supplies such parameters arbitrarily close to zero.

For this choice of $\alpha$, the zero set of $\Psi_\alpha$ in $O\times[0,1]$ is a compact smooth one-dimensional manifold with boundary. Its dimension follows from $\dim\mathcal L=\dim\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$. There are no zeros on <a href="#eq:path-gap-set" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-gap-set">[eq:path-gap-set]</a>, and near $\theta=0$ the map is the initial map, independent of $\theta$, with invertible derivative in $C$. The boundary therefore consists precisely of the $2^n$ initial zeros.

The sign group acts freely on this manifold. Its quotient is again a compact one-dimensional manifold with boundary: around each point, choose a neighborhood disjoint from its nontrivial translates, so that the quotient has the same local interval or half-interval coordinate. All initial zeros belong to one orbit, so the quotient has exactly one boundary point. This is impossible. Every compact one-dimensional manifold is a finite disjoint union of circles and closed intervals, and hence has an even number of boundary points. The contradiction proves that the original map $\Phi(\cdot,1)$ has a zero. This is the usual regular-value proof of invariance of parity, applied after quotienting by row signs; see also <a id="pm-cite-22"></a>[[Milnor1965](#ref-Milnor1965), Sections 2–4].

*Confinement of the path.* Let $C$ and $B$ be given by such a zero. For any fixed real unit vector $e$, set $y(x)=e^{\mathsf T}B(x)e$. By <a href="#eq:field-jensen" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-jensen">[eq:field-jensen]</a>, whenever $|y(x)|>\kappa$, $$e^{\mathsf T}M_\delta(B(x))e
       \leq\mu_\delta\!\left(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-y(x)^2\right)<0.$$ Since $\chi\geq0$ and $(CU)(CU)^{\mathsf T}\geq0$, the differential equation gives $y'(x)\leq0$ on this set. Both endpoint values of $y$ lie in $[-\kappa,\kappa]$. A connected interval on which $y>\kappa$ cannot begin at the level $\kappa$ while $y$ is nonincreasing. Likewise, an interval on which $y<-\kappa$ cannot end at the level $-\kappa$ while $y$ is nonincreasing. The first endpoint excludes the former excursion, and the second endpoint excludes the latter. Thus $|e^{\mathsf T}B(x)e|\leq\kappa$ for every unit vector $e$ and every $x$.

It follows that $\|B(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa$ throughout the interval, so $\chi(B(x))=1$ and <a href="#eq:path-homotopy" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-homotopy">[eq:path-homotopy]</a> becomes <a href="#eq:path-ode" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-ode">[eq:path-ode]</a>. Finally, <a href="#eq:path-C-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-C-bound">[eq:path-C-bound]</a> gives the uniform bound on $C_\varepsilon$ in <a href="#eq:path-bounds" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-bounds">[eq:path-bounds]</a> for fixed $\delta$. This completes the construction. ◻

<a id="sec:action-limit"></a>
### The action bound

We now prove Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> by combining the paths of Proposition <a href="#prop:paths" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:paths">9</a> with the field comparison. This follows the action identity and penalty argument in <a id="pm-cite-23"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Section 6]. The path equation produces a negative square in the action identity. As $\varepsilon$ tends to zero, this term forces any matrix values that could violate the desired bound toward the constraint $M_\delta(B)=AA^{\mathsf T}$. At that constraint the field is positive semidefinite, so Proposition <a href="#prop:field-comparison" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:field-comparison">8</a> applies.

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-paths-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-paths</summary>

<a id="eq:action-penalty"></a>
*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a>.* Fix $\delta>0$ and, for each $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, choose $C_\varepsilon$, $A_\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon U$, and $B_\varepsilon$ as in Proposition <a href="#prop:paths" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:paths">9</a>. Temporarily suppress the subscript $\varepsilon$. Since $A\in H^1_0$ and $B\in C^1$, the function $$x\longmapsto J_\delta(B(x))+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(A(x)^{\mathsf T}B(x)A(x))$$ is absolutely continuous. The primitive identity <a href="#eq:field-primitive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-primitive">[eq:field-primitive]</a> and the path equation give, almost everywhere, $$\begin{aligned}
    \bigl(J_\delta(B)+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(A^{\mathsf T}BA)\bigr)'
       &=2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}((A')^{\mathsf T}BA)
          -\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T})B'\bigr)\\
       &=2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}((A')^{\mathsf T}BA)
          -\varepsilon^{-1}
              \|M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\\
       &\leq\|A'\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(B^2AA^{\mathsf T})
          -\varepsilon^{-1}
              \|M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\end{aligned}$$ The last line is the Hilbert–Schmidt Cauchy–Schwarz inequality followed by $2ab\leq a^2+b^2$. Since $A$ vanishes at both endpoints, integration and the definition of the action yield $$\begin{aligned}
\label{eq:action-penalty}
    J_\delta(-K)-J_\delta(K)
       \leq{}&\mathcal E(A)
       +\int_\Omega\Bigl[
            \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((AA^{\mathsf T})^q\bigr)
            -\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((K^2-B^2)AA^{\mathsf T}\bigr)
            \notag\\[-2pt]
       &\hspace{40mm}
            -\varepsilon^{-1}
                \|M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
                         \Bigr]\,dx.
\end{aligned}$$

We next bound the integral uniformly as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. The bounds in <a href="#eq:path-bounds" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:path-bounds">[eq:path-bounds]</a>, together with the boundedness of $U$, place all pairs $$(A_\varepsilon(x),B_\varepsilon(x)),
    \qquad x\in\overline\Omega,\quad0<\varepsilon\leq1,$$ in one compact set $\mathcal K_\delta\subset
\mathbb R^{n\times d}\times\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}_n$. On this set define the continuous functions $$\begin{aligned}
    F(A,B)&=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((AA^{\mathsf T})^q\bigr)
               -\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((K^2-B^2)AA^{\mathsf T}\bigr),\\
    r_\delta(A,B)&=\|M_\delta(B)-AA^{\mathsf T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\end{aligned}$$ If $r_\delta(A,B)=0$, then $M_\delta(B)=AA^{\mathsf T}\geq0$, and Proposition <a href="#prop:field-comparison" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:field-comparison">8</a> gives $F(A,B)\leq E_\delta$. This is the only point at which positivity of the field is needed; the constructed paths themselves need not have this property.

<a id="eq:penalty-limit"></a>
Compactness now implies $$\label{eq:penalty-limit}
    \limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}
       \sup_{(A,B)\in\mathcal K_\delta}
          \bigl(F(A,B)-\varepsilon^{-1}r_\delta(A,B)\bigr)
       \leq E_\delta.$$ Indeed, a failure would give $\eta>0$, a sequence $\varepsilon_j\downarrow0$, and points $(A_j,B_j)\in\mathcal K_\delta$ at which the expression exceeds $E_\delta+\eta$. Boundedness of $F$ then forces $r_\delta(A_j,B_j)=O(\varepsilon_j)$. A convergent subsequence has limit $(A_*,B_*)$ with $r_\delta(A_*,B_*)=0$ and, by continuity, $F(A_*,B_*)\geq E_\delta+\eta$. This contradicts the preceding comparison.

<a id="eq:regularized-action-bound"></a>
Apply <a href="#eq:penalty-limit" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:penalty-limit">[eq:penalty-limit]</a> in <a href="#eq:action-penalty" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:action-penalty">[eq:action-penalty]</a>, bound $\mathcal E(A_\varepsilon)$ by the supremum over lower triangular $C$, and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. We obtain $$\label{eq:regularized-action-bound}
    J_\delta(-K)-J_\delta(K)
       \leq\sup_{C\in\mathcal L}\mathcal E(CU)
                       +|\Omega|E_\delta.$$ All compactness estimates so far were for fixed $\delta$. This is sufficient: the supremum on the right is independent of $\delta$, and the remaining limit concerns only the explicit endpoint values and the error term.

By <a href="#eq:field-endpoints" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-endpoints">[eq:field-endpoints]</a>, $$J_\delta(-K)-J_\delta(K)
       =\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}
               \mu_\delta(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds.$$ For $z\geq0$, the formula $\mu_\delta(z)=(z+\delta)^\sigma-\delta^\sigma$ gives $$0\leq\mu_\delta(z)\leq z^\sigma,
    \qquad \mu_\delta(z)\longrightarrow z^\sigma
            \quad\text{as }\delta\downarrow0.$$ Dominated convergence therefore sends the endpoint difference to $$\sum_{i=1}^n\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds.$$ The error $E_\delta$ in <a href="#eq:field-error" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:field-error">[eq:field-error]</a> tends to zero. Letting $\delta\downarrow0$ in <a href="#eq:regularized-action-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:regularized-action-bound">[eq:regularized-action-bound]</a> proves the finite-interval action inequality. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:spectral"></a>
### The spectral inequality and its sharp constant

We now apply Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> to finite families of negative eigenfunctions, following the spectral passage of the scalar companion <a id="pm-cite-24"></a>[[ScalarCompanion](#ref-ScalarCompanion), Section 7]. The lower triangular coefficients in that theorem preserve the spectral bound on each successive span. A trace version of Young’s inequality then bounds the action by the potential integral. We first justify this passage for the measurable potentials in Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.

<a id="pm-h-18"></a>
#### The form domain and negative spectrum

<a id="stmt-lem-form-compactness"></a>
<div id="lem:form-compactness" class="lemma">

**Lemma 10**. *Let $m\geq1$, let $p>1$, and let $W:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C^{m\times m}$ be a measurable Hermitian positive semidefinite function satisfying $\int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)<\infty$. The form $$h_W[\psi]=\int_\mathbb R\|\psi'\|^2
              -\int_\mathbb R\langle\psi,W\psi\rangle,
 \qquad \psi\in H^1(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m),$$ is closed and bounded below. Multiplication by $W^{1/2}$ is compact from $H^1(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m)$ to $L^2(\mathbb R;\mathbb C^m)$. The associated self-adjoint operator has only isolated eigenvalues of finite multiplicity below zero, with possible accumulation only at zero.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-form-compactness"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-form-compactness</summary>

<a id="eq:form-bound"></a>
*Proof.* Put $w(x)=\|W(x)\|_{\mathop{\mathrm{op}}}$. Positivity gives $w^p\leq\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)$, so $w\in L^p(\mathbb R)$. The one-dimensional Sobolev estimate $$\|\psi\|_\infty^2\leq2\|\psi\|_2\|\psi'\|_2$$ holds also for vector-valued functions, by applying the scalar estimate to their norm. Hölder’s inequality and interpolation therefore give $$\begin{aligned}
 \int_\mathbb R\langle\psi,W\psi\rangle
 &\leq\|w\|_p\|\psi\|_{2p/(p-1)}^2 \notag\\
 &\leq 2^{1/p}\|w\|_p
           \|\psi'\|_2^{1/p}\|\psi\|_2^{2-1/p} \notag\\
 &\leq\varepsilon\|\psi'\|_2^2+C_\varepsilon\|\psi\|_2^2
 \qquad(\varepsilon>0).                            \label{eq:form-bound}
\end{aligned}$$ The last step is Young’s inequality. Consequently a sufficiently shifted form norm is equivalent to the $H^1$ norm. The potential form is continuous on $H^1$, so the form is closed and bounded below.

For compactness, truncate the multiplier both in space and in size: $$T_{R,L}\psi
   =\mathbf1_{\{|x|\leq R,\ w(x)\leq L\}}W^{1/2}\psi.$$ Restriction to $[-R,R]$ followed by the compact embedding $H^1([-R,R];\mathbb C^m)\hookrightarrow L^2([-R,R];\mathbb C^m)$ and bounded multiplication shows that $T_{R,L}$ is compact. If $T\psi=W^{1/2}\psi$, Hölder’s inequality and the same Sobolev embedding imply $$\|(T-T_{R,L})\psi\|_2^2
 \leq C\big\|w\mathbf1_{\{|x|>R\}\cup\{w>L\}}\big\|_p
           \|\psi\|_{H^1}^2.$$ The coefficient tends to zero as $R,L\to\infty$, proving that $T$ is compact.

Finally, fix $a>0$. If the spectral subspace of $H_W$ in $(-\infty,-a]$ were infinite-dimensional, it would contain an $L^2$-orthonormal sequence $(\psi_j)$. Semiboundedness and the spectral theorem place this sequence in the form domain with uniformly bounded shifted form norm, hence uniformly bounded $H^1$ norm. A weakly convergent subsequence in $H^1$ has limit zero, because an orthonormal sequence converges weakly to zero in $L^2$. Compactness of $T$ would then give $\|T\psi_j\|_2\to0$, whereas $$-a\geq h_W[\psi_j]
     =\|\psi_j'\|_2^2-\|T\psi_j\|_2^2
     \geq-\|T\psi_j\|_2^2,$$ a contradiction. Every such spectral subspace is therefore finite-dimensional, which proves the assertion about the negative spectrum. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-19"></a>
#### From the action to spectral moments

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-form-compactness-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-form-compactness</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.* Recall that $\sigma=\gamma-\tfrac12$, $p=1+\sigma$ and $q=1+1/\sigma$. We first prove the inequality for real symmetric potentials. If there are no negative eigenvalues, it is immediate. Otherwise fix any finite nonempty list of negative eigenvalues, respecting their multiplicities. Since complex conjugation preserves each eigenspace, we may choose corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions $\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n$ that are real-valued.

The action theorem requires functions supported on a bounded interval. Choose real smooth compactly supported approximations to the $\phi_i$ in $H^1$. Their Gram matrices converge to $I_n$, and their form matrices converge to the diagonal matrix of the selected eigenvalues, by <a href="#eq:form-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:form-bound">[eq:form-bound]</a>. Multiplying each approximating family by the inverse square root of its Gram matrix makes its rows orthonormal without changing their common compact support. The form matrices still converge to the same diagonal matrix. For sufficiently good approximations they are negative definite; diagonalize them by a real orthogonal change of rows.

For one such family, let $U\in H^1_0(\Omega;\mathbb R^{n\times m})$ be the matrix whose rows are the resulting functions $u_i$, where the bounded interval $\Omega$ contains their supports in its interior. Extend these rows by zero when evaluating the whole-line form. They satisfy $$\int_\Omega UU^{\mathsf T}=I_n,
 \qquad h_W[u_i,u_j]=-k_i^2\delta_{ij},
 \qquad k_1\geq\cdots\geq k_n>0,$$ where $h_W[\cdot,\cdot]$ is the polarized form and the numbers $-k_i^2$ converge, along the approximations, to the selected eigenvalues in increasing order. Set $K=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(k_1,\ldots,k_n)$.

<a id="eq:spectral-action-upper"></a>
Let $C$ be any real lower triangular matrix and write $A=CU$. Its $i$th row is $a_i=\sum_{j\leq i}C_{ij}u_j$, so <a id="eq:triangular-spectral-order"></a>$$\label{eq:triangular-spectral-order}
 h_W[a_i]+k_i^2\|a_i\|_{L^2}^2
   =\sum_{j\leq i}C_{ij}^2(k_i^2-k_j^2)\leq0.$$ Summing over $i$ bounds the quadratic terms of the action. Since $AA^{\mathsf T}$ and $A^{\mathsf T}A$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues, $$\label{eq:spectral-action-upper}
 \mathcal E(CU)
 \leq\int_\Omega
      \bigl\{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(WA^{\mathsf T}A)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}((A^{\mathsf T}A)^q)\bigr\}\,dx.$$

The right side has a pointwise bound that does not require its two matrices to commute. Indeed, scalar maximization gives, for $y,z\geq0$, <a id="eq:scalar-young-sharp"></a>$$\label{eq:scalar-young-sharp}
 yz-z^q\leq D_\sigma y^p,
 \qquad D_\sigma=\frac{\sigma^\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{1+\sigma}}.$$ To apply it, diagonalize an arbitrary positive semidefinite matrix $Z$ in an orthonormal basis $(v_j)$, with eigenvalues $z_j$. Set $y_j=\langle v_j,Wv_j\rangle$. Convexity of $t\mapsto t^p$, applied to the spectral measure of $W$ in $v_j$, gives $y_j^p\leq\langle v_j,W^pv_j\rangle$. Hence <a id="eq:trace-young-sharp"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-young-sharp}
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(WZ)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(Z^q)
   =\sum_j(y_jz_j-z_j^q)
   \leq D_\sigma\sum_j y_j^p
   \leq D_\sigma\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p).$$ Using $Z=A^{\mathsf T}A$ in <a href="#eq:spectral-action-upper" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:spectral-action-upper">[eq:spectral-action-upper]</a>, we obtain $$\sup_{C\ \mathrm{lower\ triangular}}\mathcal E(CU)
    \leq D_\sigma\int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)\,dx.$$ All terms are finite for each $C$: the rows of $A$ are bounded and supported on $\overline\Omega$, and $W$ is locally integrable.

Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> supplies the reverse bound in terms of the $k_i$. Writing $\mathrm B$ for Euler’s beta function, the substitution $s=k_it$ gives $$\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds
  =k_i^{2\sigma+1}\mathrm B(\tfrac12,1+\sigma)
  =k_i^{2\gamma}\mathrm B(\tfrac12,1+\sigma).$$ We conclude that <a id="eq:finite-spectral-moment"></a>$$\label{eq:finite-spectral-moment}
 \sum_{i=1}^n k_i^{2\gamma}
   \leq\frac{D_\sigma}{\mathrm B(\tfrac12,1+\sigma)}
            \int_\mathbb R\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)\,dx.$$ The coefficient is exactly the one in the theorem: <a id="eq:constant-identity"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
 \frac{D_\sigma}{\mathrm B(\tfrac12,1+\sigma)}
 &=\frac{\sigma^\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{1+\sigma}}
       \frac{\Gamma(\sigma+3/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\sigma+1)} \notag\\
 &=\left(\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}\right)^\sigma
       \frac{\Gamma(\sigma+3/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\sigma+2)}
   =C_\gamma.                                           \label{eq:constant-identity}
\end{aligned}$$ Letting the approximations converge proves the bound for the chosen finite list of eigenvalues. The sum of all negative eigenvalue moments is the supremum of these finite sums. Thus the same bound holds for $\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(H_W)_-^\gamma$, and in particular this trace is finite.

For a complex Hermitian potential, write $W=E+iF$ with real matrices $E,F$. Hermiticity means $E^{\mathsf T}=E$ and $F^{\mathsf T}=-F$, so $$\widetilde W=\begin{pmatrix}E&-F\\ F&E\end{pmatrix}$$ is real symmetric. The constant complex unitary $$\mathcal U=\frac1{\sqrt2}
             \begin{pmatrix}I&iI\\ I&-iI\end{pmatrix}$$ satisfies $\mathcal U\widetilde W\mathcal U^*
=W\oplus\overline W$. It follows that $\widetilde W\geq0$ and $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\widetilde W^p)=2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(W^p)$. Since $\mathcal U$ also commutes with differentiation, the same change of coordinates gives $$H_{\widetilde W}\simeq H_W\oplus H_{\overline W}.$$ Complex conjugation intertwines the two summands and preserves eigenvalue multiplicities. The spectral moment for $\widetilde W$ is consequently twice that for $W$. Applying the real inequality in dimension $2m$ and dividing both sides by two proves the asserted inequality for all Hermitian $W$.

It remains to verify sharpness. Put $r=1/\sigma>1$ and consider the scalar potential and function $$V(x)=(r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx),
 \qquad \psi(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^{1/r}(rx).$$ The identity $\psi'/\psi=-\tanh(rx)$ gives $H_V\psi=-\psi$, and $\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R)$. We determine the full negative spectrum by the standard factorization method; see <a id="pm-cite-25"></a>[[BenguriaLoss2000](#ref-BenguriaLoss2000), Sections II–III] for its use in Lieb–Thirring inequalities. Define $Q=\partial_x+\tanh(rx)$ on $H^1(\mathbb R)$. Direct multiplication yields $$H_V=Q^*Q-1,
 \qquad QQ^*-1=-\partial_x^2+(r-1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx)\geq0.$$ Thus $H_V\geq-1$, and its eigenspace at $-1$ is $\ker Q$, which is spanned by $\psi$. If $\lambda\in(-1,0)$ were an eigenvalue with eigenfunction $u$, then $Qu\ne0$. The bounded potential gives $u\in H^2$, so $Qu\in L^2$. The intertwining identity $(QQ^*-1)Qu=\lambda Qu$ holds in distributions. Boundedness of the partner potential then implies $Qu\in H^2$, so this identity contradicts nonnegativity. Therefore $-1$ is the only negative eigenvalue and is simple. Finally, $$\int_\mathbb RV^p\,dx
  =\frac{(r+1)^p}{r}\,\mathrm B(\tfrac12,p)
  =\frac{\mathrm B(\tfrac12,1+\sigma)}{D_\sigma}
  =C_\gamma^{-1}.$$ The scalar inequality is an equality for $V$. Embedding $V$ in one diagonal channel and setting the other channels to zero gives equality in every matrix dimension, proving that $C_\gamma$ is optimal. ◻
