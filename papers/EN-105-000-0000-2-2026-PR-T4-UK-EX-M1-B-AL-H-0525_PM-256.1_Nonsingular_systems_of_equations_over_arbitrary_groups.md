## 1. Abstract and result contract

> Every finite nonsingular system of equations over an arbitrary group has a simultaneous solution in an overgroup. This proves Howie's conjecture on nonsingular systems.

- **CLM-01** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): Let $G$ be any group, let $1\le m\le n$ be integers, and let $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in G*F_n$. If their exponent-sum matrix has row rank $m$ over $\mathbb Q$, then the canonical homomorphism $$G\longrightarrow (G*F_n)/\langle\!\langle w_1,\ldots,w_m\rangle\!\rangle$$ is injective. Equivalently, the system has a simultaneous solution in an overgroup of $G$.
  - Explanation: Every finite system of equations over an arbitrary group whose integer exponent-sum matrix has full rational row rank admits a simultaneous solution in an overgroup, equivalently making the canonical homomorphism from the coefficient group into the quotient injective.
- **CLM-02** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-fixed-space)): Let $r\geq 0$ and $n\geq 1$, and let $k_1,\ldots,k_r$ be positive integers. For each $j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, let $\mathcal B_j$ be a finite ordered list of pairs $(i,s)$ with $1\leq i\leq r$ and $s\in\{1,-1\}$; repetitions are allowed. Set $$d_j=\sum_{(i,s)\in\mathcal B_j}k_i, \qquad b_{ij}=\sum_{\substack{(i',s)\in\mathcal B_j\\i'=i}}s, \qquad B=(b_{ij}).$$ Suppose that $B$ has row rank $r$ over $\mathbb Q$. For arbitrary $P_j\in\mathrm U(d_j)$, define $$D=\prod_{i=1}^r\mathrm U(k_i), \qquad F_j(X)=\operatorname{diag}_{(i,s)\in\mathcal B_j}(X_i^s)P_j \quad (X\in D).$$ Then there is $X\in D$ such that $$\label{eq:fixed-space-bound} \sum_{j=1}^n\dim_{\mathbb C}\ker(F_j(X)-I_{d_j}) \ \geq\ \sum_{i=1}^r k_i.$$ Here $\mathrm U(0)$ and an empty product of groups are points, and the fixed-space dimension in dimension zero is zero.
  - Explanation: When the signed block-multiplicity matrix has full rational row rank, there is a tuple of unitary matrices in the product group such that the sum of the complex fixed-space dimensions of the resulting block unitary operators is bounded below by the sum of the block sizes.
- **CLM-03** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-phase-subadditivity)): For every group $G$, every positive integer $d$, and all unitaries $U,V\in M_d(\mathcal M)$, $$\ell(UV)\leq\ell(U)+\ell(V).$$
  - Explanation: The unnormalized spectral-phase functional on matrix algebras over the group von Neumann algebra is subadditive under multiplication of unitary operators.
- **CLM-04** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-cycle-phase)): Let $a$ be a positive integer, let $Q_1,\ldots,Q_a\in\mathcal M$ be unitary, and define $T\in M_a(\mathcal M)$ by $$T(e_t\otimes v)=e_{t+1}\otimes Q_tv\quad(1\leq t\leq a), \qquad e_{a+1}=e_1.$$ If $C=Q_aQ_{a-1}\cdots Q_1$ is the product around the cycle starting at the first coordinate, then $$\label{eq:cycle-phase} \ell(T)=\frac{a-1}{2}+\tau(h(C)).$$
  - Explanation: The spectral phase of a block cyclic shift operator weighted by unitary elements in the group von Neumann algebra equals half the cycle length minus one, plus the trace of the phase of the ordered monodromy product around the cycle.
- **CLM-05** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-planar)): Let $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in G*F_n$ have exponent-sum matrix of row rank $m$ over $\mathbb Q$. Let $\Sigma$ be a connected oriented surface of genus zero obtained from a nonempty finite collection of signed word disks for these words by attaching bands that pair every slot exactly once, always with the same variable index and opposite traversal signs. If the words on all boundary components except possibly one are trivial in $G$, then every boundary word is trivial in $G$.
  - Explanation: On a connected genus-zero surface built from signed word disks for a rationally nonsingular system by pairing slots with matching variable indices and opposite traversal signs, if all boundary component words except at most one are trivial in the coefficient group, then every boundary word is trivial.
- **CLM-06** (theorem, [source](#stmt-rem-infinite-systems)): The finite statement also implies the version with arbitrary sets of variables and equations. Assume the exponent rows, each of finite support, are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. Any element of $G$ killed in the quotient has a normal-closure expression involving only finitely many relators and finitely many variables, including those in its conjugators. The corresponding finite exponent matrix still has independent rows, so Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> makes that element trivial.
  - Explanation: Coefficient injectivity extends from finite systems to arbitrary sets of variables and equations whose finitely supported exponent rows are linearly independent over the rationals, because any normal-closure relation in the quotient uses only finitely many relators and variables.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **System of equations over a group** ([source](#sec:intro)): A system over $G$ consists of words $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in G*F_n$, interpreted as equations $w_i=1$.
- **Solution over a coefficient group** ([source](#sec:intro)): A solution over $G$ is a group $H$ containing an isomorphic copy of $G$, together with elements $h_1,\ldots,h_n\in H$ at which all the words evaluate to the identity.
- **Exponent-sum matrix** ([source](#sec:intro)): Define the *exponent-sum matrix* $A=(a_{ij})\in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb Z)$ by the homomorphism $$G*F_n\longrightarrow\mathbb Z^n, \qquad G\longmapsto0,\quad x_j\longmapsto e_j: \qquad w_i\longmapsto(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in}).$$
- **Nonsingular system** ([source](#sec:intro)): The system is *nonsingular* if $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb Q}A=m$.
- **Normal closure in the free product** ([source](#sec:intro)): Here $\langle\!\langle w_1,\ldots,w_m\rangle\!\rangle$ denotes normal closure in $G*F_n$.
- **Primitive cohomology class** ([source](#eq:fixed-primitivity)): We will choose the generators to satisfy this compatibility and to be *primitive*: if $\mu$ is group multiplication, then $$\label{eq:fixed-primitivity} \mu^*e_{a,d}=e_{a,d}\otimes1+1\otimes e_{a,d}.$$
- **Group von Neumann algebra** ([source](#pm-h-7)): We work in the von Neumann algebra $$\mathcal M=\{T\in\mathcal B(\ell^2(G)):TR_a=R_aT \text{ for every }a\in G\},$$ which contains every $L(a)$.
- **Unnormalized matrix trace** ([source](#pm-h-7)): For a positive integer $d$, regard $M_d(\mathcal M)$ as operators on $\mathbb C^d\otimes\ell^2(G)$ and set $$\label{eq:group-trace} \tau_d(T)=\sum_{j=1}^d(T_{jj})_{1,1},\qquad \tau=\tau_1.$$
- **Spectral-phase functional** ([source](#pm-h-7)): Choose the phase with its cut at $1$ by $$\label{eq:phase-definition} h(e^{2\pi i t})=t-\lfloor t\rfloor,\qquad \ell(U)=\tau_d(h(U)).$$
- **Signed word disk, slots, and corner arcs** ([source](#eq:slot-conventions)): A *word disk of type $i$ and sign $+$ or $-$* is an oriented disk whose boundary reads $w_i$ or $w_i^{-1}$, respectively. Its variable letters occupy disjoint closed intervals called *slots*, separated by nonempty *corner arcs* carrying the coefficients.
- **Slot permutations and boundary words** ([source](#eq:planar-euler)): Let $\mathcal D$ be the set of slots. The successor permutation $\sigma$ follows the oriented boundary of each disk; the fixed-point-free involution $\theta$ pairs the ends of each band.

## 3. Proof roadmap

- **STEP-01** [claim](#stmt-thm-fixed-space) / [proof](#proof-stmt-thm-fixed-space): Allocate target dimensions via nested nonzero minors of the signed block-multiplicity matrix and evaluate the top-degree rational cohomology pullback of Poincaré dual fixed-space incidence classes. -> Existence of a unitary tuple X in D whose block operators have total fixed-space dimension at least the sum of the block sizes.
- **STEP-02** [claim](#stmt-lem-phase-subadditivity) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-phase-subadditivity): Differentiate smooth circle functions along a unitary path in the faithful unnormalized trace and approximate the discontinuous fractional-phase function from the right of the cut. -> Subadditivity inequality for the spectral phase of a product of unitary operators.
- **STEP-03** [claim](#stmt-lem-cycle-phase) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-cycle-phase): Average the spectral phase over roots of unity by diagonal unitary conjugation and apply the scalar fractional-part identity to the diagonal operator power. -> Exact formula expressing the spectral phase of T in terms of (a-1)/2 and the phase trace of the cycle monodromy C.
- **STEP-04** [claim](#stmt-prop-planar) / [proof](#proof-stmt-prop-planar): Factor the surface boundary operator as S*Theta = (SJ)(J*Theta), combine the involution phase with the simultaneous fixed-space bound to obtain an upper bound, and compare with the exact boundary-cycle sum in the one-sided cut limit. -> Triviality in G of the remaining boundary word when all other boundary component words on the planar surface are trivial.
- **STEP-05** [claim](#stmt-thm-main) / [proof](#sec:kernel): Realize a normal-closure kernel relation on a punctured disk, extract disjoint variable-pairing bands from generic circle-map level sets, and inductively fill innermost planar components using the planar boundary obstruction. -> Injectivity of the canonical homomorphism from G into the quotient by the normal closure of the relators, yielding a simultaneous solution in an overgroup.
- **STEP-06** [claim](#stmt-rem-infinite-systems) / [proof](#stmt-rem-infinite-systems): Restrict any normal-closure relation in an arbitrary independent system to the finitely many relators and variables occurring in the expression and apply the finite nonsingular system theorem. -> Coefficient injectivity and solvability for arbitrary sets of variables and equations with independent exponent rows.

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I-FLOW**: Boundary phases can be distributed antisymmetrically over paired slots with the prescribed sum on each boundary cycle.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **AU-OVERGROUP**: Treat the phase-distribution construction as an effective solver for arbitrary group equations. -> A boundary-phase assignment does not supply a coefficient-group embedding or solution elements.
- **AU-DISCONNECTED**: Apply the spanning-tree construction to a disconnected boundary graph with only a global zero-sum demand. -> Different components can have incompatible phase totals.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `constructive`. The paper explicitly constructs a boundary-phase assignment by removing leaves of a spanning tree. The implementation covers exact rational demands in this finite auxiliary construction; it does not construct the solution overgroup or the unitary fixed-space witness.

```python
# INPUTS: JSON list of graphs, each with n vertices, oriented edge pairs, and rational eta strings
# PRECONDITIONS: finite connected nonempty graph; eta has zero total; loops and parallel edges allowed
# OUTPUTS: exact rational edge flows with outgoing minus incoming sum equal to eta, or an error
# TIME: O(n + e) rational operations per graph; bit cost depends on rational operand sizes
# SPACE: O(n + e) plus rational operand storage
# ARITHMETIC: exact python-flint fmpq
# FAILURE: malformed, disconnected, or nonzero-total inputs return an explicit error
import json, sys
from flint import fmpq

def distribute(case):
    try:
        n = case['n']
        edges = case['edges']
        if type(n) is not int or not 1 <= n <= 32 or len(edges) > 128:
            return {'error': 'invalid'}
        if len(case['eta']) != n:
            return {'error': 'invalid'}
        eta = [fmpq(x) for x in case['eta']]
        adj = [[] for _ in range(n)]
        for j, pair in enumerate(edges):
            if len(pair) != 2 or any(type(v) is not int or not 0 <= v < n for v in pair):
                return {'error': 'invalid'}
            u, v = pair
            adj[u].append((v, j, 1))
            adj[v].append((u, j, -1))
        if sum(eta, fmpq(0)) != 0:
            return {'error': 'total'}
        parent = {0: None}
        order = [0]
        for u in order:
            for v, j, sign in adj[u]:
                if v not in parent:
                    parent[v] = (u, j, sign)
                    order.append(v)
        if len(order) != n:
            return {'error': 'disconnected'}
        flows = [fmpq(0) for _ in edges]
        residual = eta[:]
        for v in reversed(order[1:]):
            u, j, sign = parent[v]
            flows[j] = -sign * residual[v]
            residual[u] += residual[v]
        if residual[0] != 0:
            return {'error': 'residual'}
        return {'flow': [str(x) for x in flows]}
    except (ValueError, TypeError, KeyError, ZeroDivisionError):
        return {'error': 'invalid'}

print(json.dumps([distribute(c) for c in json.load(sys.stdin)]))
```

## 6. Applicability limits and search

- **LIM-01** (hypothesis): The coefficient-injectivity conclusion requires a system of group words with full rational row rank. Failure or absence of that premise leaves this theorem inapplicable; it does not prove that a solution is impossible. Blocked use: ['Inferring overgroup solvability from an unchecked rank condition.', 'Transferring injectivity to software constraints without an exact group-equation interpretation.']
- **LIM-02** (nonconstructive): The main result guarantees a solution in an overgroup. A solution in the original coefficient group, an effective word-problem procedure, and runtime bounds are ABSENT from that conclusion. Blocked use: ['Demanding that the unknowns lie in the original coefficient group.', 'Treating abstract quotient existence as an executable witness algorithm.']
- **LIM-03** (nonconstructive): The fixed-space proof establishes intersection through a nonzero cohomological evaluation. An algorithm producing the unitary tuple or certified numerical error bounds for such a tuple is ABSENT. Blocked use: ['Treating the intersection argument as a numerical construction of unitary witnesses.', 'Using approximate eigenvalues alone to certify exact fixed-space dimensions.']
- **LIM-04** (hypothesis): The fixed-space theorem uses signed block multiplicities and full rational row rank. Unsigned occurrence counts do not establish its premise. Blocked use: ['Replacing signed multiplicities by unsigned counts.', 'Applying the fixed-space lower bound after rank cancellation.']
- **LIM-05** (edge_case): Empty products and zero-dimensional unitary groups are explicitly permitted in the fixed-space theorem; their fixed-space contribution is zero. Blocked use: ['Rejecting the empty case as undefined.', 'Inventing positive fixed-space dimensions for zero-dimensional blocks.']
- **LIM-06** (approximation): The spectral phase is discontinuous at the identity from the negative-angle side. The proof requires one-sided smoothing compatible with its right-continuous choice of phase. Blocked use: ['Substituting symmetric smoothing at the cut.', 'Assuming continuity from both angular directions at the identity.']
- **LIM-07** (hypothesis): The phase formulas use the unnormalized trace. Replacing it by a normalized trace requires consistently rescaling the formulas and bounds. Blocked use: ['Mixing normalized spectral averages with the stated phase constants.']
- **LIM-08** (hypothesis): The planar obstruction requires connectedness, genus zero, complete compatible slot pairing, and triviality of all boundary words except possibly one. Arbitrary pairings do not establish planarity. Blocked use: ['Applying the obstruction to positive-genus surfaces.', 'Concluding that all boundary words vanish when several remain unchecked.']
- **LIM-09** (hypothesis): The boundary-phase distribution is a finite constructive substep on a connected graph with zero total prescribed phase. It does not compute an overgroup solution or the cohomological unitary witnesses. Blocked use: ['Using a graph flow witness as a certificate of the entire group-equation theorem.', 'Assuming arbitrary phase demands can be routed without a conservation condition.']
- **LIM-10** (hypothesis): The extension to arbitrary equation sets retains finite support and independence of the exponent rows. It reduces each killed coefficient to a finite normal-closure expression; it supplies no general infinitary witness algorithm. Blocked use: ['Extending the result to infinitary words or dependent exponent rows.', 'Treating finite-relation reduction as a uniform solver for an arbitrary equation set.']
- **LIM-11** (unknown): A connection from coefficient injectivity to implementation correctness, cryptographic hardness, or operational security is ABSENT. Blocked use: ['Claiming software or security guarantees from abstract overgroup solvability alone.']

## 7. Complete mathematical paper


Every finite nonsingular system of equations over an arbitrary group has a simultaneous solution in an overgroup. This proves Howie's conjecture on nonsingular systems.

<a id="sec:intro"></a>
### Introduction

An equation over a group permits coefficients from that group and asks whether the unknowns can be realized in a larger group without identifying distinct coefficients. The exponent sums record the dependence on the unknowns after abelianization. A system whose exponent rows are independent is called nonsingular. We prove that every such finite system is solvable over every coefficient group.

Let $G$ be a group and let $F_n=F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ be the free group on $n$ generators. A system over $G$ consists of words $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in G*F_n$, interpreted as equations $w_i=1$. A solution over $G$ is a group $H$ containing an isomorphic copy of $G$, together with elements $h_1,\ldots,h_n\in H$ at which all the words evaluate to the identity. Define the *exponent-sum matrix* $A=(a_{ij})\in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb Z)$ by the homomorphism $$G*F_n\longrightarrow\mathbb Z^n,
 \qquad G\longmapsto0,\quad x_j\longmapsto e_j:
 \qquad w_i\longmapsto(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in}).$$ The system is *nonsingular* if $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb Q}A=m$. Here $\langle\!\langle w_1,\ldots,w_m\rangle\!\rangle$ denotes normal closure in $G*F_n$.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1** (Nonsingular systems). *Let $G$ be any group, let $1\le m\le n$ be integers, and let $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in G*F_n$. If their exponent-sum matrix has row rank $m$ over $\mathbb Q$, then the canonical homomorphism $$G\longrightarrow
 (G*F_n)/\langle\!\langle w_1,\ldots,w_m\rangle\!\rangle$$ is injective. Equivalently, the system has a simultaneous solution in an overgroup of $G$.*

</div>

The equivalence follows from the universal property of the displayed quotient. If the canonical map is injective, the quotient itself supplies the overgroup and the images of the free generators supply the solution. Conversely, any solution induces a homomorphism from the quotient whose restriction to $G$ is injective. Thus solving the equations amounts to showing that their normal closure kills no nonidentity element of $G$. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> imposes no countability, finiteness, or torsion condition on $G$. The same conclusion for arbitrary sets of equations and variables with independent exponent rows follows by a finite-relation argument in Remark <a href="#rem:infinite-systems" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="rem:infinite-systems">4</a>.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
#### History and significance

The general solvability assertion for nonsingular systems is commonly called Howie’s conjecture; see <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[KlyachkoMikheenkoRomankov2024](#ref-KlyachkoMikheenkoRomankov2024), Introduction] for this formulation and terminology. Its one-equation, one-variable case is the Kervaire–Laudenbach conjecture: a nonzero exponent sum should guarantee coefficient injectivity. This is stronger than the nontriviality assertion usually called the Kervaire conjecture, which asks whether adjoining one generator and imposing one relation can annihilate a nontrivial group. Chen explains these formulations and their connection with high-dimensional knot groups <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[Chen2026](#ref-Chen2026), Section 1]. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> establishes the finite nonsingular-system assertion for arbitrary coefficient groups.

The foundational system theorem is due to Gerstenhaber and Rothaus <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[GerstenhaberRothaus1962](#ref-GerstenhaberRothaus1962)]. For a square system over a compact connected Lie group, they compute the degree of the word map from the determinant of its exponent-sum matrix. A nonzero determinant therefore forces surjectivity and a simultaneous solution in that Lie group. Their finite-group theorem also produces a finite solution overgroup, using arithmetic specialization and reduction over finite fields. A nonsingular rectangular system reduces to the square case by retaining columns of a nonzero maximal minor and setting the other variables equal to the identity. Their argument established the usefulness of compact Lie groups, cohomology, and degree theory in a problem stated entirely in group-theoretic terms.

A different line of work uses the topology of relative presentations. Howie proved solvability of finite independent systems over locally indicable groups <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[Howie1981](#ref-Howie1981), Corollary 4.2]; locally indicable means that every nontrivial finitely generated subgroup maps onto $\mathbb Z$. Klyachko’s theorem treats a single equation in one variable over a torsion-free group when the exponent sum is $\pm1$ <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[Klyachko1993](#ref-Klyachko1993)]. This unimodular hypothesis is stronger than a nonzero exponent sum. Chen later recovered that theorem by estimating the complexity of surfaces in HNN extensions <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[Chen2026](#ref-Chen2026), Theorem 6.9]. These results illustrate two complementary sources of control: algebraic conditions on the coefficient group and topological restrictions on diagrams witnessing a kernel element.

The compact-unitary method also passes to metric ultraproducts. Pestov observed its application to hyperlinear groups <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[Pestov2008](#ref-Pestov2008), Corollary 10.4], and Nitsche and Thom give the nonsingular-system statement explicitly <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[NitscheThom2022](#ref-NitscheThom2022), Theorem 1.2 and Lemma 2.1]. Here a hyperlinear, or Connes-embeddable, group is one that embeds into a metric ultraproduct of unitary groups with their normalized Hilbert–Schmidt metrics. Their stronger Theorem 1.3 replaces nonsingularity by vanishing second homology of a covering of the presentation complex obtained after deleting the coefficients. For the presentation complex itself, vanishing second homology is precisely independence of the exponent rows. Their covering criterion therefore reaches beyond nonsingular systems while retaining the hypothesis on the coefficient group.

Other developments control the solution overgroup more closely. Klyachko, Mikheenko, and Roman’kov obtain solutions within specified classes of solvable groups with suitable torsion-free abelian factors <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[KlyachkoMikheenkoRomankov2024](#ref-KlyachkoMikheenkoRomankov2024)]. Ramirez-Côté and Wise use the Banach fixed-point theorem for groups embedded in Magnus-type power-series groups; their nonsingular-system construction inverts the determinant in the coefficient ring <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[RamirezCoteWise2025](#ref-RamirezCoteWise2025), Theorems 9–10]. Such conclusions address additional structure beyond existence in an arbitrary overgroup.

The direct antecedent of our proof is the spectral-phase and planar argument in *The Kervaire theorem for groups <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[OpenAIKervaire2026](#ref-OpenAIKervaire2026), Lemma 2.1, Lemma 3.1, and Theorem 4.1]* . Its fixed-plane incidence construction treats one unimodular relator. The extension below uses products of unitary groups and rational intersection classes to accommodate several relator types with different multiplicities. The cohomological background is classical <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[Borel1953](#ref-Borel1953)]; related geometry of unitary eigenvalue strata and their intersection classes is developed by Nicolaescu <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[Nicolaescu2010](#ref-Nicolaescu2010)]. In finite dimensions, spectral-phase subadditivity also follows from Thompson’s exponential formula <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[Thompson1986](#ref-Thompson1986)]. Antezana, Larotonda, and Varela prove an approximate version, in operator norm, for embeddable $\mathrm{II}_1$ factors <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[AntezanaLarotondaVarela2012](#ref-AntezanaLarotondaVarela2012), Theorem 4.4]. Our argument instead uses the faithful trace of the group von Neumann algebra <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[Jones2009](#ref-Jones2009), Section 3.3] and proves the required phase inequality directly, so the coefficients need no unitary approximation hypothesis.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
#### Proof and technical contribution

The proof passes from a possible kernel relation to a planar surface. A finite product of conjugates of the relators is represented by disks whose boundaries read the relators or their inverses. Rectangular bands pair occurrences of the same variable with opposite signs. The remaining boundary arcs carry only coefficients from $G$. The key assertion is a boundary obstruction: on a connected planar surface of this form, if all but one boundary words are trivial in $G$, then the last is trivial as well. Removing the innermost components then proves injectivity.

Two independent estimates establish the obstruction. First, full row rank forces equal numbers of positive and negative disks of each relator type. Suppose these numbers are $k_i$ for the types present. A topological argument supplies unitary matrices mixing the $k_i$ copies of each type so that a finite-dimensional unitary matrix has at least $2\sum_i k_i$ fixed directions. Second, a spectral-phase function measures the boundary words through the left regular representation of $G$. Its subadditivity turns the fixed-space estimate into an upper bound. A direct calculation around the boundary circles attains that bound plus a nonnegative term measuring the possibly nontrivial boundary word. Euler characteristic makes the two constants agree, and faithfulness of the trace forces that word to be the identity.

The spectral-phase and planar-surface strategy comes from the one-relator unimodular theorem in <a id="pm-cite-17"></a>[[OpenAIKervaire2026](#ref-OpenAIKervaire2026)]. We give the required arguments in full. The principal extension is the multi-block fixed-space theorem in Section <a href="#sec:fixed" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:fixed">2</a>. It concerns block matrices built from independent unitary variables of possibly different sizes, followed by arbitrary fixed unitary matrices. Full row rank of the signed block-multiplicity matrix forces a simultaneous lower bound on the dimensions of their fixed spaces. The proof pairs fixed-subspace incidence cycles with the product of the unitary-variable groups. Only the maximal-length exterior terms in cohomology contribute; their coefficient is a product of nested nonzero minors. This makes rational nonsingularity sufficient and gives an intersection principle that is independent of its application to equations over groups.

Section <a href="#sec:phase" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:phase">3</a> establishes the phase inequality in the finite operator algebra of the left regular representation and computes the phase of a weighted cyclic shift. Section <a href="#sec:planar" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:planar">4</a> combines these results to prove the boundary obstruction. Section <a href="#sec:kernel" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:kernel">5</a> constructs the planar surfaces from an arbitrary kernel relation and completes the proof. The unitary topology is used only for finite complex matrices on the copy indices; the group coefficients are retained as operators throughout. This separation allows the argument to apply to arbitrary coefficient groups.

<a id="sec:fixed"></a>
### Simultaneous fixed spaces

The rank hypothesis enters the proof through the following finite-dimensional statement. A unitary matrix may occur in several blocks, with either exponent $1$ or exponent $-1$. Only the signed numbers of its occurrences matter for the lower bound on the total fixed-space dimension. The proof extends the single-matrix incidence argument of <a id="pm-cite-18"></a>[[OpenAIKervaire2026](#ref-OpenAIKervaire2026), Lemma 3.1] by using rational cohomology and nested nonzero minors to accommodate several unitary matrix variables.

<a id="stmt-thm-fixed-space"></a>
<div id="thm:fixed-space" class="theorem">

<a id="eq:fixed-space-bound"></a>
**Theorem 2** (Simultaneous fixed spaces). *Let $r\geq 0$ and $n\geq 1$, and let $k_1,\ldots,k_r$ be positive integers. For each $j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, let $\mathcal B_j$ be a finite ordered list of pairs $(i,s)$ with $1\leq i\leq r$ and $s\in\{1,-1\}$; repetitions are allowed. Set $$d_j=\sum_{(i,s)\in\mathcal B_j}k_i,
 \qquad
 b_{ij}=\sum_{\substack{(i',s)\in\mathcal B_j\\i'=i}}s,
 \qquad B=(b_{ij}).$$ Suppose that $B$ has row rank $r$ over $\mathbb Q$. For arbitrary $P_j\in\mathrm U(d_j)$, define $$D=\prod_{i=1}^r\mathrm U(k_i),
 \qquad
 F_j(X)=\operatorname{diag}_{(i,s)\in\mathcal B_j}(X_i^s)P_j
 \quad (X\in D).$$ Then there is $X\in D$ such that $$\label{eq:fixed-space-bound}
 \sum_{j=1}^n\dim_{\mathbb C}\ker(F_j(X)-I_{d_j})
 \ \geq\ \sum_{i=1}^r k_i.$$ Here $\mathrm U(0)$ and an empty product of groups are points, and the fixed-space dimension in dimension zero is zero.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-thm-fixed-space"><summary>Proof of stmt-thm-fixed-space</summary>

<a id="eq:fixed-nested-minors"></a>
<a id="eq:fixed-target-dimensions"></a>
*Proof.* *Choosing target dimensions.* The case $r=0$ is immediate. Assume $r>0$, and reorder the rows so that $k_1\geq\cdots\geq k_r$. We first allocate the desired fixed-space dimensions among the maps $F_j$. There are distinct columns $j_1,\ldots,j_r$ such that $$\label{eq:fixed-nested-minors}
 \det B[1,\ldots,t\mid j_1,\ldots,j_t]\ne 0
 \qquad (1\leq t\leq r).$$ Indeed, the first $t$ rows have rank $t$. The columns chosen at the previous step remain independent on these rows, since their restrictions to the first $t-1$ rows are independent; they can therefore be extended by one column. Define $$l_{j_t}=k_t\quad(1\leq t\leq r),
 \qquad l_j=0\quad\text{for unchosen columns}.$$ These choices satisfy $l_j\leq d_j$. To see this for $j=j_t$, the nonzero minor in <a href="#eq:fixed-nested-minors" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-nested-minors">[eq:fixed-nested-minors]</a> implies that $b_{i,j_t}\ne0$ for some $i\leq t$. The list $\mathcal B_{j_t}$ thus contains a block of size $k_i\geq k_t$. Moreover, $$\label{eq:fixed-target-dimensions}
 \sum_j l_j=\sum_i k_i,
 \qquad \sum_j l_j^2=\sum_i k_i^2=\dim_{\mathbb R}D.$$ We will obtain $\dim\ker(F_j(X)-I_{d_j})\geq l_j$ simultaneously. The equality of dimensions in <a href="#eq:fixed-target-dimensions" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-target-dimensions">[eq:fixed-target-dimensions]</a> makes this an intersection problem of complementary dimensions. We develop the cohomology classes that detect the required intersection.

<a id="eq:fixed-unitary-cohomology"></a>
<a id="eq:fixed-block-restriction"></a>
<a id="eq:fixed-primitivity"></a>
*Primitive cohomology classes.* All cohomology in this proof has coefficients in $\mathbb Q$. The classical computation of unitary-group cohomology <a id="pm-cite-19"></a>[[Borel1953](#ref-Borel1953), Proposition 9.1 and Sections 19–20] gives generators $$\label{eq:fixed-unitary-cohomology}
 H^*(\mathrm U(d);\mathbb Q)
   =\Lambda(e_{1,d},\ldots,e_{d,d}),
 \qquad |e_{a,d}|=2a-1,$$ where $\Lambda$ denotes the exterior algebra. Compatibility means that inclusion as a coordinate block, with the identity on its complement, satisfies $$\label{eq:fixed-block-restriction}
 \iota_{k,d}^*e_{a,d}=
 \begin{cases}
 e_{a,k},&a\leq k,\\
 0,&a>k.
 \end{cases}$$ We will choose the generators to satisfy this compatibility and to be *primitive*: if $\mu$ is group multiplication, then $$\label{eq:fixed-primitivity}
 \mu^*e_{a,d}=e_{a,d}\otimes1+1\otimes e_{a,d}.$$ Thus these classes add under multiplication, just as degree-one classes do on a torus.

For completeness, these properties follow together from the last-column bundle $$\mathrm U(d-1)\longrightarrow\mathrm U(d)
       \longrightarrow S^{2d-1}.$$ Start with the degree-one generator of $\mathrm U(1)=S^1$. For $d\geq2$, the base is simply connected, so the coefficient system in the multiplicative Serre spectral sequence is constant <a id="pm-cite-20"></a>[[McCleary2001](#ref-McCleary2001), Theorem 5.2 and Section 5.3]. Its second page is $$E_2^{p,q}=H^p(S^{2d-1};\mathbb Q)
              \otimes H^q(\mathrm U(d-1);\mathbb Q).$$ Its only nonzero columns are $p=0,2d-1$. The only possible differential between them is $d_{2d-1}$, which sends fiber degree $q$ to degree $q-2d+2$. Each fiber generator has degree at most $2d-3$, so its differential has negative target degree and vanishes. The differential vanishes on all their products by the product rule for the differential. Consequently the spectral sequence collapses. Its edge maps show that restriction to $\mathrm U(d-1)$ is an isomorphism in degrees below $2d-1$, and that the pullback of the sphere’s top class is nonzero. Lift each earlier generator in its degree, and take this sphere pullback as $e_{d,d}$. Their exterior products give the basis in <a href="#eq:fixed-unitary-cohomology" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-unitary-cohomology">[eq:fixed-unitary-cohomology]</a>: they give that basis in the associated graded algebra supplied by the spectral sequence, and their squares vanish because they have odd degree and the coefficients are rational. This also proves compatibility with the standard inclusions.

To check primitivity, subtract the two summands on the right of <a href="#eq:fixed-primitivity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-primitivity">[eq:fixed-primitivity]</a> from its left side. The difference restricts to zero on either factor at the identity, so every term in its Künneth decomposition has positive degree in both factors. Each such degree is at most $2d-2$. Restriction to $\mathrm U(d-1)\times\mathrm U(d-1)$ is injective on all these bidegrees, by the edge-map isomorphism just proved. On this product the difference vanishes: for $a<d$ this is the induction hypothesis, and for $a=d$ it follows because $e_{d,d}$ is pulled back by the last-column projection, which is constant on the fiber. This proves <a href="#eq:fixed-primitivity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-primitivity">[eq:fixed-primitivity]</a>; the case $d=1$ is immediate. Coordinate-block inclusions in other positions are conjugate to the standard one, and conjugation is homotopic to the identity in the connected group $\mathrm U(d)$. Finally, inversion sends $e_{a,d}$ to $-e_{a,d}$, by pulling <a href="#eq:fixed-primitivity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-primitivity">[eq:fixed-primitivity]</a> back along $X\mapsto(X,X^{-1})$.

*The fixed-space incidence class.* We next construct a class that detects a fixed space of dimension at least $l$, for $0\leq l\leq d$. Let $$R(d,l)=\{(W,E): W\in\mathrm U(d),\ E\in
        \operatorname{Gr}_l(\mathbb C^d),\ W|_E=I_E\},
 \qquad p(W,E)=W,$$ where $\operatorname{Gr}_l(\mathbb C^d)$ is the complex Grassmannian of $l$-dimensional subspaces. Over a subspace $E$, the possible $W$ are precisely the unitary transformations of $E^\perp$, extended by the identity on $E$. Orthonormal frame charts therefore make $R(d,l)$ a smooth bundle over the Grassmannian with fiber $\mathrm U(d-l)$. It is compact and without boundary, and $$\dim_{\mathbb R}R(d,l)=2l(d-l)+(d-l)^2=d^2-l^2.$$ The complex orientation of the Grassmannian and an orientation of $\mathrm U(d-l)$ orient this bundle: changes of frame act on the fiber by conjugation and preserve orientation. Choose orientations and let $$\alpha(d,l)=\operatorname{PD}\bigl(p_*[R(d,l)]\bigr)
        \in H^{l^2}(\mathrm U(d);\mathbb Q).$$ Here $\operatorname{PD}$ is Poincaré duality <a id="pm-cite-21"></a>[[Hatcher2002](#ref-Hatcher2002), Theorem 3.30]. It applies to the homology class pushed forward by $p$; the image of $p$ need not itself be a submanifold. Its image consists exactly of those $W$ with $\dim\ker(W-I_d)\geq l$.

We recall the geometric interpretation of this class. If $M$ is a closed oriented manifold of dimension $l^2$ and $f:M\to\mathrm U(d)$, then $\langle f^*\alpha(d,l),[M]\rangle$ is the intersection number of $f$ and $p$. When these maps are transverse, it is the signed count of pairs $(x,(W,E))$ with $f(x)=p(W,E)$. This follows by intersecting $f\times p$ with the diagonal of $\mathrm U(d)\times\mathrm U(d)$: transversality makes its inverse image a compact oriented zero-manifold, whose signed count gives the intersection pairing. In particular, disjoint images give intersection number zero: disjoint maps from compact manifolds have disjoint sufficiently small transverse perturbations. The same interpretation holds for products of these maps and classes.

<a id="eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term"></a>
The part of $\alpha(d,l)$ we need is $$\label{eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term}
 \alpha(d,l)=c_{d,l}e_{1,d}\cdots e_{l,d}
       +\text{terms with fewer than $l$ generators},
 \qquad c_{d,l}\ne0.$$ For $l=0$, the map $p$ is the identity and we take $\alpha(d,0)=1$ and $c_{d,0}=1$. For $l>0$, a product of $u$ distinct generators has degree at least $1+3+\cdots+(2u-1)=u^2$. Since $\alpha(d,l)$ has degree $l^2$, its expansion contains at most $l$ generators in each term, and the sole possible term of length $l$ is the one displayed in <a href="#eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term">[eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term]</a>.

To show that its coefficient is nonzero, consider $$f:\mathrm U(l)\longrightarrow\mathrm U(d),
 \qquad f(X)=X\oplus(-I_{d-l}).$$ There is exactly one intersection pair for $f$ and $p$: it has $X=I_l$, $W=W_0=I_l\oplus(-I_{d-l})$, and $E=E_0=\mathbb C^l\oplus0$. Indeed, the second summand has no fixed vectors, while an $l$-dimensional fixed space in the first summand forces $X=I_l$. This intersection is transverse. Identify the tangent space at $W_0$ with skew-Hermitian matrices by right multiplication by $W_0^{-1}$. Variations of $f$ fill the upper-left block; variations of $p$ in the fiber over $E_0$ fill the lower-right block. For a linear map $Z:E_0\to E_0^\perp$, put $$T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-Z^*\\ Z&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ The path $(e^{tT}W_0e^{-tT},e^{tT}E_0)$ lies in $R(d,l)$ and its image under $p$ has tangent vector $$[T,W_0]W_0^{-1}=2T.$$ These vectors supply every off-diagonal direction. The unique intersection therefore has multiplicity $1$ or $-1$. The map $f$ is homotopic to the coordinate inclusion, so <a href="#eq:fixed-block-restriction" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-block-restriction">[eq:fixed-block-restriction]</a> computes its pullback. Terms with an index above $l$ vanish; any remaining term with fewer than $l$ generators has degree less than $\dim\mathrm U(l)=l^2$. Only the displayed term in <a href="#eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term">[eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term]</a> can account for the nonzero intersection number, proving $c_{d,l}\ne0$. The same argument includes $l=d$, when the complementary blocks are absent.

<a id="eq:fixed-generator-pullback"></a>
*Computing the intersection number.* We now apply these classes to the maps in the statement. Write $e_{a,k_i}^{(i)}$ for the generator pulled back from the $i$th factor of $D$. Primitivity, compatibility with block inclusions, and the inversion formula give $$\label{eq:fixed-generator-pullback}
 F_j^*e_{a,d_j}
   =\sum_{i:\,k_i\geq a}b_{ij}e_{a,k_i}^{(i)}
       \qquad(1\leq a\leq d_j).$$ To justify the use of multiplication here, write a block diagonal map as the product of maps that act on one block and are the identity on all others. Each positive occurrence contributes one generator and each negative occurrence its negative. Right multiplication by $P_j$ is homotopic to the identity and has no effect on cohomology.

Consider the top-degree class $$\Omega=\prod_{j=1}^nF_j^*\alpha(d_j,l_j)
       \in H^{\sum_i k_i^2}(D;\mathbb Q).$$ The exterior algebra $H^*(D;\mathbb Q)$ has altogether $\sum_i k_i$ generators, all of positive degree. A nonzero monomial of top degree must contain every one of them. By <a href="#eq:fixed-generator-pullback" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-generator-pullback">[eq:fixed-generator-pullback]</a>, pullback preserves the number of generators in each term, unless that term vanishes. It follows that any use of a shorter term from <a href="#eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term">[eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term]</a> contributes zero to $\Omega$. Thus we need only compute the product of the displayed leading terms.

For each $a\in\{1,\ldots,k_1\}$, put $t_a=|\{i:k_i\geq a\}|$. The rows with $k_i\geq a$ are $1,\ldots,t_a$, and the selected columns with $l_j\geq a$ are $j_1,\ldots,j_{t_a}$. Grouping the exterior factors of degree $2a-1$ together gives $$\prod_{t=1}^{t_a}
   \left(\sum_{i=1}^{t_a}b_{i,j_t}e_{a,k_i}^{(i)}\right)
 =\det B[1,\ldots,t_a\mid j_1,\ldots,j_{t_a}]
       \prod_{i=1}^{t_a}e_{a,k_i}^{(i)}.$$ Consequently the coefficient of the top monomial in $\Omega$, up to an overall sign, is <a id="eq:fixed-top-coefficient"></a>$$\label{eq:fixed-top-coefficient}
 \left(\prod_{j=1}^n c_{d_j,l_j}\right)
 \prod_{a=1}^{k_1}
   \det B[1,\ldots,t_a\mid j_1,\ldots,j_{t_a}].$$ Every factor is nonzero, by <a href="#eq:fixed-nested-minors" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-nested-minors">[eq:fixed-nested-minors]</a> and <a href="#eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term">[eq:fixed-incidence-leading-term]</a>. Hence $\langle\Omega,[D]\rangle\ne0$.

Finally, let $F=(F_1,\ldots,F_n)$ and let $p_{\mathrm{tot}}=\prod_j p$ be the map from $\prod_jR(d_j,l_j)$ to $\prod_j\mathrm U(d_j)$. Up to the harmless orientation sign, $\Omega$ is the pullback by $F$ of the Poincaré dual of the cycle defined by $p_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Its nonzero evaluation implies that these two maps have intersecting images. At an intersection, $F_j(X)$ fixes an $l_j$-plane for every $j$, which gives <a href="#eq:fixed-space-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-space-bound">[eq:fixed-space-bound]</a> by <a href="#eq:fixed-target-dimensions" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-target-dimensions">[eq:fixed-target-dimensions]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:phase"></a>
### Spectral phase

The planar argument will associate unitary operators to the boundary circles of a surface. We need a nonnegative numerical invariant that detects the identity, is subadditive under multiplication, and can be calculated on a weighted cyclic shift. We develop these properties in the operator algebra of an arbitrary group, following the spectral-phase argument of <a id="pm-cite-22"></a>[[OpenAIKervaire2026](#ref-OpenAIKervaire2026), Lemma 2.1 and Section 4.3].

<a id="pm-h-7"></a>
#### The trace and the choice of phase

Let $G$ be any group, and let $\{\delta_x:x\in G\}$ be the standard orthonormal basis of $\ell^2(G)$. Define left and right translations by $$L(a)\delta_x=\delta_{ax},\qquad
 R_a\delta_x=\delta_{xa^{-1}}.$$ They commute. We work in the von Neumann algebra $$\mathcal M=\{T\in\mathcal B(\ell^2(G)):TR_a=R_aT
             \text{ for every }a\in G\},$$ which contains every $L(a)$. For a positive integer $d$, regard $M_d(\mathcal M)$ as operators on $\mathbb C^d\otimes\ell^2(G)$ and set <a id="eq:group-trace"></a>$$\label{eq:group-trace}
 \tau_d(T)=\sum_{j=1}^d(T_{jj})_{1,1},\qquad \tau=\tau_1.$$ Here $(T_{jj})_{1,1}$ is the coefficient of $\delta_1$ in $T_{jj}\delta_1$. The trace is *unnormalized*: $\tau_d(I)=d$. For the group von Neumann algebra and this trace construction, see <a id="pm-cite-23"></a>[[Jones2009](#ref-Jones2009), Section 3.3].

We record why this is a faithful normal trace, including when $G$ is uncountable. If $T\in\mathcal M$ and $T\delta_1=\sum_x t_x\delta_x$, commutation with right translations gives $$T_{x,y}=t_{xy^{-1}}.$$ For $A,B\in\mathcal M$ with corresponding coefficient families $a,b$, $$\tau(AB)=\sum_{x\in G}a_{x^{-1}}b_x
         =\sum_{x\in G}b_{x^{-1}}a_x=\tau(BA).$$ These sums converge absolutely by Cauchy–Schwarz; each square-summable family has countable support. Summing over the matrix indices proves traciality of $\tau_d$. Positivity and normality follow from its expression as a finite sum of vector functionals. If $T\geq0$ and $\tau_d(T)=0$, then $$0=\sum_{j=1}^d
   \bigl\|T^{1/2}(e_j\otimes\delta_1)\bigr\|^2.$$ The square root commutes with all simultaneous right translations, so it kills $e_j\otimes\delta_x$ for every $j,x$. Thus $T=0$, proving faithfulness.

<a id="eq:phase-spectral-integral"></a>
The bounded Borel functional calculus of a normal operator in $M_d(\mathcal M)$ remains in this algebra. For a unitary $U$, its spectral projections define a finite measure $\mu_U(E)=\tau_d(\mathbf1_E(U))$ on the unit circle $\mathbb T$, and $$\label{eq:phase-spectral-integral}
 \tau_d(f(U))=\int_{\mathbb T}f\,d\mu_U,
 \qquad \mu_U(\mathbb T)=d.$$ In particular, uniformly bounded pointwise convergence of Borel functions implies convergence of these trace evaluations.

Choose the phase with its cut at $1$ by <a id="eq:phase-definition"></a>$$\label{eq:phase-definition}
 h(e^{2\pi i t})=t-\lfloor t\rfloor,\qquad
 \ell(U)=\tau_d(h(U)).$$ Thus $h(1)=0$, and $h$ is right-continuous in the angle. Since $h(U)\geq0$ and $U=\exp(2\pi i h(U))$, faithfulness gives <a id="eq:phase-faithful"></a>$$\label{eq:phase-faithful}
 \ell(U)\geq0,\qquad \ell(U)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ U=I.$$ The phase is invariant under unitary conjugation and additive on direct sums. On scalar matrices, viewed as matrices over $\mathcal M$, it is the sum of the phases of the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity.

<a id="pm-h-8"></a>
#### Subadditivity

<a id="stmt-lem-phase-subadditivity"></a>
<div id="lem:phase-subadditivity" class="lemma">

**Lemma 1** (Spectral-phase subadditivity). *For every group $G$, every positive integer $d$, and all unitaries $U,V\in M_d(\mathcal M)$, $$\ell(UV)\leq\ell(U)+\ell(V).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-phase-subadditivity"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-phase-subadditivity</summary>

<a id="eq:phase-derivative"></a>
*Proof.* Put $D=h(V)$ and join $U$ to $UV$ by the unitary path $$W_u=Ue^{2\pi i uD},\qquad 0\leq u\leq1.$$ We first calculate the change of a smooth function along this path, and then approximate the discontinuous function $h$ from the correct side of its cut. For $f\in C^\infty(\mathbb T)$ write $f'(e^{2\pi it})=\frac{d}{dt}f(e^{2\pi it})$. We claim that $$\label{eq:phase-derivative}
 \frac{d}{du}\tau_d(f(W_u))=\tau_d(f'(W_u)D).$$ Indeed, $W'_u=2\pi iW_uD$. The product and inverse rules, followed by cyclic permutation inside the trace, give for every integer $k$ $$\frac{d}{du}\tau_d(W_u^k)=2\pi i k\,\tau_d(W_u^kD).$$ This proves <a href="#eq:phase-derivative" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phase-derivative">[eq:phase-derivative]</a> for Laurent polynomials. For smooth $f$, its Fourier coefficients satisfy $\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}(1+|k|)|\widehat f(k)|<\infty$, while $\|\frac{d}{du}W_u^k\|\leq2\pi|k|\|D\|$. Thus the Fourier series and its differentiated series converge uniformly in operator norm, proving <a href="#eq:phase-derivative" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phase-derivative">[eq:phase-derivative]</a> in general.

<a id="eq:phase-smooth-bound"></a>
If $f$ is real-valued and $f'\leq1$, then $I-f'(W_u)\geq0$. Although $D$ need not commute with $W_u$, traciality gives the required inequality: $$\tau_d(D)-\tau_d(f'(W_u)D)
 =\tau_d\bigl(D^{1/2}(I-f'(W_u))D^{1/2}\bigr)\geq0.$$ Integration of <a href="#eq:phase-derivative" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phase-derivative">[eq:phase-derivative]</a> therefore yields $$\label{eq:phase-smooth-bound}
 \tau_d(f(UV))-\tau_d(f(U))\leq\tau_d(D).$$

For $0<\varepsilon<1$, choose a nonnegative smooth function $\rho_\varepsilon$ supported in $(0,\varepsilon)$ with integral one, and define $$f_\varepsilon(e^{2\pi it})
 =\int_0^\varepsilon
   \rho_\varepsilon(s)h(e^{2\pi i(t+s)})\,ds.$$ This is a smooth function on the circle, with $0\leq f_\varepsilon\leq1$. For $\delta>0$ the fractional-part function satisfies $$h(e^{2\pi i(t+\delta)})-h(e^{2\pi it})\leq\delta.$$ Averaging this inequality and taking the difference quotient shows that $f'_\varepsilon\leq1$. Right-continuity gives $f_\varepsilon(\lambda)\to h(\lambda)$ for every $\lambda\in\mathbb T$. At the cut this follows explicitly from $0\leq f_\varepsilon(1)\leq\varepsilon$; symmetric smoothing would not have this property. Apply <a href="#eq:phase-smooth-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phase-smooth-bound">[eq:phase-smooth-bound]</a> to $f_\varepsilon$ and pass to the limit by <a href="#eq:phase-spectral-integral" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phase-spectral-integral">[eq:phase-spectral-integral]</a>. Since $\tau_d(D)=\ell(V)$, the result is the stated inequality. ◻

</details>

The same right-continuity also gives, for any fixed $c\geq0$, <a id="eq:phase-right-limit"></a>$$\label{eq:phase-right-limit}
 \lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}
 \ell(e^{2\pi i c\varepsilon}U)=\ell(U).$$ In the opposite direction the identity has a jump: $\ell(e^{-2\pi i\varepsilon}I_d)=d(1-\varepsilon)$ for $0<\varepsilon<1$. These two behaviors at the cut will distinguish the possibly nontrivial boundary circle from the trivial ones in the planar argument.

<a id="pm-h-9"></a>
#### The phase of a weighted cycle

Subadditivity will give an upper bound on the operator associated to a surface. The following exact calculation will express its phase in terms of the boundary words.

<a id="stmt-lem-cycle-phase"></a>
<div id="lem:cycle-phase" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:cycle-phase"></a>
**Lemma 2** (Weighted-cycle formula). *Let $a$ be a positive integer, let $Q_1,\ldots,Q_a\in\mathcal M$ be unitary, and define $T\in M_a(\mathcal M)$ by $$T(e_t\otimes v)=e_{t+1}\otimes Q_tv\quad(1\leq t\leq a),
 \qquad e_{a+1}=e_1.$$ If $C=Q_aQ_{a-1}\cdots Q_1$ is the product around the cycle starting at the first coordinate, then $$\label{eq:cycle-phase}
 \ell(T)=\frac{a-1}{2}+\tau(h(C)).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-cycle-phase"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-cycle-phase</summary>

*Proof.* Put $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/a}$. Conjugating $T$ by the scalar diagonal matrix with entries $1,\zeta,\ldots,\zeta^{a-1}$ gives $\zeta T$. Hence $\ell(\zeta^jT)=\ell(T)$ for every integer $j$. The scalar identity $$\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}h(\zeta^j\lambda)
 =\frac{a-1}{2}+h(\lambda^a),\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb T,$$ follows by listing the fractional parts of the $a$ equally spaced angles; it holds also when one of them is zero. Applying bounded Borel functional calculus and taking the unnormalized trace gives $$a\ell(T)=\frac{a(a-1)}2+\tau_a(h(T^a)).$$ The operator $T^a$ is diagonal. Its first diagonal entry is $C$, and the other entries are the products around the same cycle with different starting coordinates. Consecutive such products are conjugate by the corresponding $Q_t$, so every diagonal entry has phase trace $\tau(h(C))$. Thus $\tau_a(h(T^a))=a\tau(h(C))$, proving <a href="#eq:cycle-phase" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:cycle-phase">[eq:cycle-phase]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:planar"></a>
### The planar boundary obstruction

We now combine the fixed-space theorem with the phase inequality. The result is a statement about a planar surface assembled from copies of the relators: if every boundary component but one reads the identity in $G$, then the remaining component does too. This extends the one-relator boundary argument of <a id="pm-cite-24"></a>[[OpenAIKervaire2026](#ref-OpenAIKervaire2026), Theorem 4.1 and Section 4]; the new fixed-space input allows the different relator types to be treated simultaneously.

<a id="eq:word-slots"></a>
Cyclically conjugating the words does not change their normal closure or their exponent sums. We may therefore write $$\label{eq:word-slots}
 w_i=x_{\nu_{i,1}}^{s_{i,1}}g_{i,1}\cdots
     x_{\nu_{i,N_i}}^{s_{i,N_i}}g_{i,N_i},
 \qquad
 g_{i,t}\in G,\quad s_{i,t}\in\{1,-1\},\quad
 1\leq \nu_{i,t}\leq n.$$ Here each occurrence is a single variable letter, coefficients may be the identity, and $N_i\geq1$ because the $i$th row of $A$ is nonzero.

<a id="eq:slot-conventions"></a>
A *word disk of type $i$ and sign $+$ or $-$* is an oriented disk whose boundary reads $w_i$ or $w_i^{-1}$, respectively. Its variable letters occupy disjoint closed intervals called *slots*, separated by nonempty *corner arcs* carrying the coefficients. On both signs of disk, index the slots by the positions $t$ in <a href="#eq:word-slots" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:word-slots">[eq:word-slots]</a>. Indices are cyclic modulo $N_i$. The boundary data are then $$\label{eq:slot-conventions}
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
 \text{disk sign}&\text{successor of }t&
 \text{variable at }t&\text{corner after }t\\ \hline
 +&t+1&x_{\nu_{i,t}}^{s_{i,t}}&g_{i,t}\\
 -&t-1&x_{\nu_{i,t}}^{-s_{i,t}}&g_{i,t-1}^{-1}
\end{array}$$ The exponent $s_{i,t}$ or $-s_{i,t}$ in this table is the slot’s *traversal sign*. Attach rectangular bands to pairs of slots so that the orientations extend across the bands. Every slot is used once, and paired slots must have the same variable index and opposite traversal signs. The remaining boundary consists of corner arcs and band sides; its word in $G$ is obtained by reading the corner labels, with no label contributed by a band side. Changing the starting corner conjugates this word, so its triviality is well defined.

<a id="stmt-prop-planar"></a>
<div id="prop:planar" class="proposition">

**Proposition 3** (Planar boundary obstruction). *Let $w_1,\ldots,w_m\in G*F_n$ have exponent-sum matrix of row rank $m$ over $\mathbb Q$. Let $\Sigma$ be a connected oriented surface of genus zero obtained from a nonempty finite collection of signed word disks for these words by attaching bands that pair every slot exactly once, always with the same variable index and opposite traversal signs. If the words on all boundary components except possibly one are trivial in $G$, then every boundary word is trivial in $G$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-planar"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-planar</summary>

*Proof.* The proof estimates one unitary operator in two ways. Mixing disks of the same type gives an upper bound for its phase. Reading the operator around the boundary gives an exact phase formula. A choice of scalar phases at the end makes the two expressions differ only by the phase of the possibly nontrivial boundary word.

*Disk counts and boundary cycles.* Let $p_i$ and $m_i$ be the numbers of positive and negative disks of type $i$. Each band pairs opposite occurrences of the same variable, whence $$\sum_i(p_i-m_i)a_{ij}=0\qquad(1\leq j\leq n).$$ Row independence gives $p_i=m_i$. In the rest of this proof, let $I$ be the set of types that occur and write $k_i=p_i=m_i\geq1$ for $i\in I$. Set <a id="eq:disk-band-counts"></a>$$\label{eq:disk-band-counts}
 K=\sum_{i\in I}k_i,\qquad b=\sum_{i\in I}N_i k_i.$$ Thus $\Sigma$ has $2K$ disks, $2b$ slots, and $b$ bands.

<a id="eq:planar-euler"></a>
Let $\mathcal D$ be the set of slots. The successor permutation $\sigma$ follows the oriented boundary of each disk; the fixed-point-free involution $\theta$ pairs the ends of each band. For $d\in\mathcal D$, write $c_d\in G$ for the label on the corner from the end of $d$ to the start of $\sigma d$, as specified in <a href="#eq:slot-conventions" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slot-conventions">[eq:slot-conventions]</a>. Starting at the start of slot $d$, a boundary path crosses a band side to the end of $\theta d$, then follows its corner to the start of $\sigma\theta d$; see Figure <a href="#fig:boundary-step" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:boundary-step">1</a>. Consequently the cycles $z$ of $\sigma\theta$ are exactly the boundary components. If $z=(d_1,\ldots,d_a)$ is written in this order, put <a id="eq:boundary-word"></a>$$\label{eq:boundary-word}
 a(z)=a,\qquad r_z=c_{\theta d_1}\cdots c_{\theta d_a}.$$ The nonempty corner arcs ensure that there is at least one boundary component. If $q$ denotes their number, genus zero gives $$\label{eq:planar-euler}
 2-q=\chi(\Sigma)=2K-b,\qquad \sum_z a(z)=2b.$$

<a id="eq:boundary-flow"></a>
*Distributing phases among boundary components.* For any real numbers $(\eta_z)_z$ with $\sum_z\eta_z=0$, we can choose real numbers $(\gamma_d)_{d\in\mathcal D}$ such that $$\label{eq:boundary-flow}
 \gamma_{\theta d}=-\gamma_d,\qquad
 \sum_{d\in z}\gamma_d=\eta_z.$$ To see this, form a graph whose vertices are the boundary cycles and whose edges are the pairs $\{d,\theta d\}$, with an end incident to the cycle containing that slot. Loops and multiple edges are allowed. Connectedness of $\Sigma$ means that $\sigma$ and $\theta$ act transitively on $\mathcal D$. Since $\sigma\theta$ and $\theta$ generate the same permutation group, this graph is connected. Choose a spanning tree and set the values on all other edges to zero. At a leaf, set the value at its end of the remaining edge equal to its prescribed sum and the value at the other end to its negative; remove the leaf and adjust the prescribed sum at its neighbor. The zero total ensures the last vertex is satisfied. This proves <a href="#eq:boundary-flow" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:boundary-flow">[eq:boundary-flow]</a>, including the one-vertex case.

Use the algebra $M_{2b}(\mathcal M)$ of Section <a href="#sec:phase" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:phase">3</a>, acting on $\mathbb C^{\mathcal D}\otimes\ell^2(G)$, and define <a id="eq:surface-operators"></a>$$\label{eq:surface-operators}
 \begin{split}
 S(e_d\otimes v)&=e_{\sigma d}\otimes L(c_d^{-1})v,\\
 \Theta(e_d\otimes v)&=e^{2\pi i\gamma_d}
                      e_{\theta d}\otimes v.
 \end{split}$$ Both operators are unitary, and <a href="#eq:boundary-flow" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:boundary-flow">[eq:boundary-flow]</a> gives $\Theta^2=I$. We identify scalar matrices with their tensor products with the identity on $\ell^2(G)$; their trace $\tau_{2b}$ is therefore the ordinary matrix trace.

*The upper phase bound.* We will choose a scalar unitary involution $J$ and use the factorization $S\Theta=(SJ)(J\Theta)$. We arrange that $SJ$ is an involution with known phase, and use the fixed-space theorem to give $J\Theta$ many fixed vectors. For each $i\in I$, order the $k_i$ disks of each sign. At each position $t$, their slots form two coordinate spaces, each identified with $\mathbb C^{k_i}$. Given $X_i\in\mathrm U(k_i)$, define a scalar unitary involution $J$ by sending the positive array at $(i,t)$ to the negative array by $X_i$, and the negative array back by $X_i^{-1}$. The same $X_i$ is used at every position of type $i$.

<a id="eq:disk-involution-phase"></a>
The corner conventions give <a id="eq:reversal-involution"></a>$$\label{eq:reversal-involution}
 JSJ=S^{-1}.$$ Indeed, on positive arrays $S$ moves from $t$ to $t+1$ with coefficient $L(g_{i,t}^{-1})$, whereas on negative arrays it moves from $t$ to $t-1$ with coefficient $L(g_{i,t-1})$. Applying $J$ on either side interchanges these rules; its factors $X_i$ and $X_i^{-1}$ cancel because the coefficients are constant across each array and commute with scalar matrices. Thus $SJ$ is a unitary involution. It exchanges the two disk signs, so its diagonal blocks, and hence its trace, are zero. Its $-1$ spectral projection $(I-SJ)/2$ has trace $b$, giving $$\label{eq:disk-involution-phase}
 \ell(SJ)=\frac b2.$$

<a id="eq:surface-phase-upper"></a>
We now choose the $X_i$ so that $J\Theta$ has many fixed vectors. Regroup the scalar coordinates by variable index $j$ and traversal sign into spaces $V_{j,+}$ and $V_{j,-}$. For each occurrence $(i,t)$ with $\nu_{i,t}=j$, each space has one block of dimension $k_i$: the positive or negative disk array is selected according to its traversal sign. In particular both spaces have dimension $$d_j=\sum_{\substack{i\in I,\ 1\leq t\leq N_i\\\nu_{i,t}=j}}k_i,
 \qquad \sum_j d_j=b.$$ Both $J$ and $\Theta$ exchange $V_{j,+}$ and $V_{j,-}$. Under the array coordinates, let $P_j\in\mathrm U(d_j)$ represent $\Theta:V_{j,+}\longrightarrow V_{j,-}$. On the return map $J:V_{j,-}\longrightarrow V_{j,+}$, the block at $(i,t)$ is $X_i^{-s_{i,t}}$. Hence <a id="eq:fixed-space-application"></a>$$\label{eq:fixed-space-application}
 (J\Theta)|_{V_{j,+}}
   =\operatorname{diag}_{(i,t):\,\nu_{i,t}=j}
           (X_i^{-s_{i,t}})P_j.$$ The signed block counts here are $\sum_{t:\nu_{i,t}=j}(-s_{i,t})=-a_{ij}$. The matrix formed by the rows of $-A$ indexed by $I$ has full row rank, so Theorem <a href="#thm:fixed-space" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:fixed-space">2</a> supplies $X_i$ for which $$\dim_{\mathbb C}\ker\bigl((J\Theta-I)|_{\bigoplus_j V_{j,+}}\bigr)
 \geq K.$$ There are equally many fixed vectors on $\bigoplus_jV_{j,-}$: $J$ interchanges the two sums and satisfies $J(J\Theta)J=(J\Theta)^{-1}$. Thus the full fixed-space dimension $f$ of the scalar matrix $J\Theta$ is at least $2K$. The same conjugacy makes its eigenvalue multiset invariant under $\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{-1}$. Since $h(\lambda)+h(\lambda^{-1})=1$ for $\lambda\ne1$ and is zero at $1$, $$2\ell(J\Theta)=2b-f\leq2b-2K.$$ Using $S\Theta=(SJ)(J\Theta)$, <a href="#eq:disk-involution-phase" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:disk-involution-phase">[eq:disk-involution-phase]</a> and Lemma <a href="#lem:phase-subadditivity" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:phase-subadditivity">1</a> now give $$\label{eq:surface-phase-upper}
 \ell(S\Theta)\leq\frac32 b-K.$$ This holds for every phase distribution in <a href="#eq:boundary-flow" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:boundary-flow">[eq:boundary-flow]</a>; the auxiliary choice of $J$ may depend on that distribution.

<a id="eq:boundary-phase"></a>
*The boundary formula and the cut limit.* It remains to read the left side of <a href="#eq:surface-phase-upper" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:surface-phase-upper">[eq:surface-phase-upper]</a> from the boundary. On the coordinates of a cycle $z=(d_1,\ldots,d_a)$ of $\sigma\theta$, the operator $S\Theta$ is a weighted cyclic shift $T_z$ whose step from $d_t$ to $d_{t+1}$ has weight $e^{2\pi i\gamma_{d_t}}L(c_{\theta d_t}^{-1})$. The circuit starting at $d_1$ therefore has weight $$e^{2\pi i\sum_{d\in z}\gamma_d}
 L(c_{\theta d_a}^{-1}\cdots c_{\theta d_1}^{-1})
   =e^{2\pi i\eta_z}L(r_z^{-1}).$$ Lemma <a href="#lem:cycle-phase" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:cycle-phase">2</a> yields the exact formula $$\label{eq:boundary-phase}
 \ell(T_z)=\frac{a(z)-1}{2}
       +\tau\bigl(h(e^{2\pi i\eta_z}L(r_z^{-1}))\bigr).$$

Choose the possibly exceptional component $z_0$, and for $\epsilon>0$ set $$\eta_z=-\epsilon\quad(z\ne z_0),\qquad
 \eta_{z_0}=(q-1)\epsilon.$$ These phases sum to zero, so <a href="#eq:surface-phase-upper" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:surface-phase-upper">[eq:surface-phase-upper]</a> applies. For $z\ne z_0$, the assumption $r_z=1$ makes the last term of <a href="#eq:boundary-phase" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:boundary-phase">[eq:boundary-phase]</a> equal to $1-\epsilon$ when $0<\epsilon<1$. For $z_0$, the scalar phase approaches zero from the nonnegative side. Thus $h(e^{2\pi i(q-1)\epsilon}\lambda)\to h(\lambda)$ at every point of the unit circle, including $\lambda=1$. Bounded convergence in the spectral measure gives $$\tau\bigl(h(e^{2\pi i(q-1)\epsilon}L(r_{z_0}^{-1}))\bigr)
 \longrightarrow \tau\bigl(h(L(r_{z_0}^{-1}))\bigr).$$ This also covers $q=1$, when the scalar phase is identically zero. Summing <a href="#eq:boundary-phase" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:boundary-phase">[eq:boundary-phase]</a> over all cycles and using <a href="#eq:planar-euler" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-euler">[eq:planar-euler]</a>, we obtain $$\begin{aligned}
 \lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}\ell(S\Theta)
 &=\frac{2b-q}{2}+q-1+\tau\bigl(h(L(r_{z_0}^{-1}))\bigr)\\
 &=\frac32 b-K+\tau\bigl(h(L(r_{z_0}^{-1}))\bigr).
\end{aligned}$$ The upper bound <a href="#eq:surface-phase-upper" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:surface-phase-upper">[eq:surface-phase-upper]</a> forces the last trace to be zero. Faithfulness of $\tau$ and nonnegativity of $h$ imply $L(r_{z_0}^{-1})=I$. Applying this operator to $\delta_1$ gives $r_{z_0}=1$, as required. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:kernel"></a>
### From a kernel relation to a solution

We now apply Proposition <a href="#prop:planar" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:planar">3</a> to prove Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>. The required surfaces must be embedded in a disk: an arbitrary pairing of variable occurrences would not guarantee planarity. Following the construction in <a id="pm-cite-25"></a>[[OpenAIKervaire2026](#ref-OpenAIKervaire2026), Section 5], we obtain the bands as level arcs of the variable-circle maps on a punctured disk. This also ensures that the coefficient map is constant along every band.

<a id="pm-h-12"></a>
#### A punctured disk for the relation

<a id="eq:normal-product"></a>
Let $g\in G$ lie in the normal closure of the relators. By definition, there is a finite expression in $G*F_n$ $$\label{eq:normal-product}
 g=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{u}
       b_\alpha w_{i_\alpha}^{\epsilon_\alpha}b_\alpha^{-1},
 \qquad b_\alpha\in G*F_n,\qquad\epsilon_\alpha\in\{1,-1\}.$$ If $u=0$, then $g=1$. Assume $u>0$. Use the cyclic representatives of the relators chosen in Section <a href="#sec:planar" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:planar">4</a>; conjugating a relator only changes the corresponding conjugators in <a href="#eq:normal-product" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:normal-product">[eq:normal-product]</a>.

Choose a based CW presentation space $Y$ with $\pi_1(Y)=G$, and put $$Y'=Y\vee S^1_1\vee\cdots\vee S^1_n.$$ The added circles represent the variables, so $\pi_1(Y')=G*F_n$. Choose based loops in $Y$ for the coefficients and for $g$. No asphericity or finiteness property of $Y$ is needed.

Let $\Delta$ be an oriented polygonal disk and remove the interiors of $u$ disjoint polygonal disks $D_\alpha$ in its interior. On the resulting punctured disk $$P=\Delta\setminus\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{u}\operatorname{int}D_\alpha$$ there is a map $f:P\to Y'$ with these boundary values: the outer boundary reads $g$ in $Y$, and $\partial D_\alpha$, oriented as the boundary of the missing disk, reads $w_{i_\alpha}^{\epsilon_\alpha}$. Each variable letter is represented by one monotone traversal of its circle. The intervening coefficient intervals are mapped to the chosen loops in $Y$; retain an interval even when its label is the identity.

Here is an explicit reason that these values extend over $P$. Cut $P$ along disjoint arcs from the outer boundary to the holes, and map those arcs to the loops for $b_\alpha$. Place their outer endpoints in a constant portion of the outer loop, with the holes encountered in reverse order after the outer traversal. The boundary of the cut disk reads $$g\bigl(b_u w_{i_u}^{\epsilon_u}b_u^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}
  \cdots
  \bigl(b_1 w_{i_1}^{\epsilon_1}b_1^{-1}\bigr)^{-1}.$$ It is nullhomotopic by <a href="#eq:normal-product" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:normal-product">[eq:normal-product]</a>. A nullhomotopy extends the map across the cut disk. The two copies of each cut arc have the same map with opposite traversal, so the extension descends to $P$. Notice that the induced boundary orientation of $P$ on a hole is opposite to the missing-disk orientation; this explains the inverses in the displayed word.

<a id="pm-h-13"></a>
#### Disjoint bands from circle levels

Let $f_j:P\to S^1_j=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ be the projection of $f$ that collapses all the other wedge summands, and let $v:P\to Y$ be the coefficient projection. We construct bands on which $v$ is exactly constant, without changing the boundary values of any $f_j$.

Choose a common sufficiently fine finite triangulation of $P$ and piecewise affine circle maps $\widetilde f_j$ satisfying $$\widetilde f_j|_{\partial P}=f_j|_{\partial P},
 \qquad
 \operatorname{dist}(\widetilde f_j(p),f_j(p))<\frac1{12}
 \quad(p\in P).$$ These approximations can be constructed directly. The boundary maps are already piecewise affine in the angular coordinate. Subdivide to respect their breakpoints and so finely that the image of each triangle under each $f_j$ lies in a short arc of the circle. Lift that arc to $\mathbb R$ and interpolate its vertex values. The lifts on a shared edge differ by an integer, so the interpolants agree as circle maps. Uniform continuity gives the stated error bound.

For each $j$, choose $t_j\in(1/3,2/3)$ that is not the image of any vertex under $\widetilde f_j$. The level set $\widetilde f_j^{-1}(t_j)$ is a finite disjoint union of polygonal circles and properly embedded polygonal arcs. On each triangle it consists of line segments, and avoidance of vertices makes the segments join without branching. Since the approximation is fixed on the boundary, there is exactly one endpoint for each occurrence of $x_j^{\pm1}$ on a hole and no endpoint on the outer boundary or on a coefficient interval.

These level sets are disjoint for different indices. Indeed, at a point of $\widetilde f_j^{-1}(t_j)$ the original $f_j$ is at distance more than $1/4$ from the circle basepoint. Thus $f$ lands in the $j$th variable circle away from the wedge point, and all its other circle projections vanish. The same remains true in a neighborhood of the level set. In particular, $v$ is constant at the basepoint on that neighborhood. This argument uses the original wedge-valued map $f$; the separately approximated maps need not themselves combine to a map into the wedge.

Discard the closed level curves and choose thin, mutually disjoint rectangular neighborhoods of the proper arcs inside these neighborhoods. They are the bands. Each meets the holes in small intervals inside the corresponding variable traversals, and every occurrence has exactly one such interval. Coorientation of a circle level makes the two endpoint crossing signs of an arc opposite with respect to the boundary orientation of $P$. Reversing both orientations to the missing-disk orientations leaves the signs opposite. Hence each band pairs the same variable with opposite traversal signs, as required in Proposition <a href="#prop:planar" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:planar">3</a>.

Use the smaller attachment intervals as the slots. The pieces of a variable traversal outside its slot project constantly to $Y$, so shrinking the slots does not change any coefficient label between them. Moreover, $v$ is constant on every band. Reinsert the disks $D_\alpha$ as the signed word disks. Their union with the bands is a compact surface embedded in the interior of $\Delta$, with every slot paired. Every connected component is planar, and its boundary words are exactly the loops supplied by $v$ on those boundary circles.

<a id="pm-h-14"></a>
#### Filling from the inside out

It remains to account for the possibility of several components nested inside one another. Each connected component has one exterior boundary circle whose Jordan disk contains the component; its other boundary circles enclose the inner complementary disks. The exterior Jordan disks of distinct components are disjoint or nested. Choose a component whose exterior disk is minimal under inclusion. Its inner complementary disks contain no other component, and hence contain no missing word disk. The coefficient map $v$ is already defined on each of them. Their boundary words are therefore trivial in $G$.

Proposition <a href="#prop:planar" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:planar">3</a> now implies that the exterior boundary word of the chosen component is also trivial. Its exact boundary loop extends to a map of its Jordan disk into $Y$. Replace $v$ inside that disk by this extension. The two maps agree on the boundary, so they paste continuously. No other component lies in the disk, and the coefficient labels and band constants on all remaining components are unchanged.

Repeating this finite procedure removes all components. The result is a map $\Delta\to Y$ extending the original outer loop for $g$. Consequently $g=1$ in $G$. This proves the injectivity in Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>; its quotient supplies the simultaneous solution. 0◻

<a id="stmt-rem-infinite-systems"></a>
<div id="rem:infinite-systems" class="remark">

*Remark 4*. The finite statement also implies the version with arbitrary sets of variables and equations. Assume the exponent rows, each of finite support, are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. Any element of $G$ killed in the quotient has a normal-closure expression involving only finitely many relators and finitely many variables, including those in its conjugators. The corresponding finite exponent matrix still has independent rows, so Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> makes that element trivial.
