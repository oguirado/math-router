## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We resolve affirmatively the remaining cases of the scalar one-dimensional Lieb–Thirring conjecture: for every $`\frac12\lt \gamma\lt \frac32`$, the optimal constant is the one-bound-state constant. The estimate holds for every nonnegative potential in $`L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)`$, with all negative eigenvalues included.

- **C1** (bound, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): For every $1/2<\gamma<3/2$ and every nonnegative $W\in L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)$, $$\label{eq:main} \operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma \le 2\left(\frac{\gamma-1/2}{\gamma+1/2}\right)^{\gamma-1/2} L_{\gamma,1}^{\mathrm{cl}}\int_\mathbb RW^{\gamma+1/2}.$$ The constant is optimal and equals $L_{\gamma,1}^{(1)}$. More precisely, if $r=(\gamma-1/2)^{-1}$, equality is attained by $W(x)=(r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx)$.
  - Explanation: The sharp scalar spectral moment bound holds on the line for the stated exponent interval and all admissible nonnegative potentials. The displayed potential attains equality; the result does not classify every optimizer.
- **C2** (bound, [source](#stmt-thm-action)): Let $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_N)^{\mathsf T}\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R^N)$ satisfy $\int_I uu^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_N$, and let $k_i>0$. Then $$\label{eq:action-bound} \sup_{C\text{ lower triangular}}\mathcal E_K(Cu) \ge \sum_{i=1}^N\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds.$$ The supremum is over all real lower triangular $N\times N$ matrices.
  - Explanation: The action of lower triangular combinations of an arbitrary real orthonormal family has the stated lower bound, retaining the individual positive eigenvalue scales.
- **C3** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-hs-calculus)): If a real function $h$ is $L$-Lipschitz on an interval containing the spectra of two Hermitian matrices $A$ and $B$, then $\\|h(A)-h(B)\\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\le L\\|A-B\\|_{\mathrm{HS}}$.
  - Explanation: A scalar Lipschitz bound controls matrix functional calculus in the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, without an operator norm assertion.
- **C4** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-field-regularity)): The limit in <a href="#eq:X-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:X-M">[eq:X-M]</a> is locally uniform. The resulting map $M$ is locally Lipschitz. Moreover, $$\label{eq:J} J(B)=-\int_0^1\operatorname{tr}\bigl(BM(tB)\bigr)\,dt$$ is $C^1$ and satisfies <a href="#eq:framework-gradient" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:framework-gradient">[eq:framework-gradient]</a>.
  - Explanation: The symmetric cutoff defines a locally Lipschitz matrix field and a continuously differentiable trace primitive with the stated derivative.
- **C5** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-mu)): The function $\mu$ is locally Lipschitz and strictly increasing, with $\mu(0)=0$. For $z\ge0$, $$\label{eq:mu-positive} \mu(z)=(z+\delta)^\sigma-\delta^\sigma, \qquad \mu'(z)=\sigma(z+\delta)^{\sigma-1}.$$ For every fixed $b,z\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:shift} c\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^R \bigl(f((s-b)^2+z)-f(s^2)\bigr)\,ds=\mu(z).$$
  - Explanation: The scalar comparison function is strictly increasing and has the stated regularized power and shift identities.
- **C6** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-jensen)): For every $B\in\operatorname{Sym}_N$ and every real unit vector $e$, $$\label{eq:jensen} e^{\mathsf T}M(B)e\le \mu\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\bigr).$$ If a standard basis vector $e_i$ is an eigenvector of $B$ with eigenvalue $b_i$, then $$\label{eq:common-eigenvector} M(B)e_i=\mu(k_i^2-b_i^2)e_i.$$ For the primitive in <a href="#eq:J" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:J">[eq:J]</a>, $$\label{eq:boundary-J} J(-K)-J(K)=\sum_{i=1}^N\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}\mu(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds.$$
  - Explanation: Scalar concavity gives a directional field bound. Coordinate eigenvectors give an exact identity, and diagonal integration determines the primitive boundary difference.
- **C7** (bound, [source](#stmt-prop-rankone)): For every $B\in\operatorname{Sym}_N$ and every $a\in\mathbb R^N$, $$\label{eq:rankone} M(B)=aa^{\mathsf T}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^{\mathsf T}(K^2-B^2)a\ge \|a\|^{2q}.$$
  - Explanation: When the field equals the outer product of a vector with itself, the quadratic deficit controls the nonlinear action term.
- **C8** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-resolvent-tangent)): For $y\ge0$ the function $f$ of <a href="#eq:f" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:f">[eq:f]</a> has the representation $$\label{eq:resolvent-f} f(y)=c_\beta\int_0^\infty v^\beta \left(\frac1{\delta+v}-\frac1{y+\delta+v}\right)\,dv, \qquad c_\beta^{-1}=\int_0^\infty\frac{t^\beta}{(1+t)^2}\,dt.$$ If $X,Y\ge0$ are Hermitian matrices, then $$\label{eq:tangent} f(X)\le f(Y)+T_Y(X-Y),$$ where, in an eigenbasis of $Y$ with eigenvalues $y_i$, $$\label{eq:divdiff} (T_Y Z)_{ij}=f[y_i,y_j]Z_{ij},\qquad f[x,y]=\int_0^1 f'(\tau x+(1-\tau)y)\,d\tau.$$ For $x\ne y$, this is $(f(x)-f(y))/(x-y)$; for $x=y$ it is $f'(x)$.
  - Explanation: The resolvent formula yields a matrix tangent upper bound on positive semidefinite matrices, including repeated eigenvalues.
- **C9** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-average)): In an eigenbasis of $B$ with eigenvalues $z_i$, the map $\mathcal P$ is $$\label{eq:P-kernel} (\mathcal P Z)_{ij}=P_{ij}Z_{ij},\qquad P_{ij}=\int_0^1 \left(1+\frac{\tau(1-\tau)(z_i-z_j)^2}{d+\delta}\right)^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau.$$ There is an even probability measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb R$ such that $$\label{eq:P-average} \mathcal P(Z)=\int_\mathbb Re^{itB}Ze^{-itB}\,d\nu(t).$$ In particular $\mathcal P(I)=I$, and $\mathcal P$ is self-adjoint for the trace pairing on Hermitian matrices.
  - Explanation: The integrated tangent map is a Schur multiplier and an average of unitary conjugations with an even probability measure.
- **C10** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-trace-average)): With $B,D$ and $\mathcal P$ as above, $$\label{eq:trace-average} \operatorname{tr}(M(B)D)\ge\operatorname{tr}(M(B)\mathcal P(D)).$$
  - Explanation: Trace monotonicity removes the unitary average in the pairing needed for the rank-one comparison.
- **C11** (construction, [source](#stmt-path-existence)): Let $I=(x_-,x_+)$ be a bounded interval, let $u\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R^N)$ satisfy $$\int_I u(x)u(x)^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_N,$$ and let $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)$ with every $k_i>0$. Suppose $M$ and $\mu$ have the regularity and properties <a href="#path:equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:equivariance">[path:equivariance]</a>–<a href="#path:coordinate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:coordinate">[path:coordinate]</a> just stated. For every $\varepsilon>0$, there are a real lower triangular matrix $C$ and a path $B\in C^1(\overline I;\operatorname{Sym}_N)$ such that, with $a=Cu$, $$\label{path:equation} \varepsilon B'=M(B)-aa^{\mathsf T}, \qquad B(x_-)=K,\qquad B(x_+)=-K.$$ These choices satisfy $$\label{path:operator-bound} \\|B(x)\\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\\|K\\|_{\mathrm{op}} \qquad(x\in I).$$ For fixed $M$, $K$, and $I$, the matrices $C$ can be chosen bounded uniformly for $0<\varepsilon\leq1$.
  - Explanation: A lower triangular coefficient matrix and a connecting path exist with prescribed endpoints and the stated bounds. This is an existence result without a finite construction algorithm.
- **C12** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-path-parity)): Let $Q$ be a smooth $d$-dimensional manifold without boundary, and let $E\to Q$ be a smooth real vector bundle of rank $d$. Suppose $s_\theta$, $0\leq\theta\leq1$, is a continuous homotopy of sections and $$\{(q,\theta)\in Q\times[0,1]:s_\theta(q)=0\}$$ is compact. If $s_0$ is smooth and has an odd number of zeros, all transverse, then $s_1$ has a zero.
  - Explanation: Compactness and an odd initial transverse zero count ensure that a continuous homotopy of bundle sections retains a final zero.
- **C13** (theorem, [source](#stmt-spectral-forms)): Let $p>1$ and $0\leq W\in L^p(\mathbb R)$. The form $h_{-W}$ is closed and bounded below on $H^1(\mathbb R)$. For its associated self-adjoint operator $H_{-W}$, every spectral projection onto $(-\infty,-\alpha]$, with $\alpha>0$, has finite rank. Thus its negative spectrum consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, with possible accumulation only at zero. On a bounded interval $I$, the corresponding Dirichlet form on $H^1_0(I)$ is closed and bounded below and has compact resolvent.
  - Explanation: The Schrödinger forms are closed and semibounded; negative spectral projections bounded away from zero have finite rank. Bounded-interval Dirichlet operators have compact resolvent.
- **C14** (bound, [source](#stmt-spectral-dirichlet)): Let $I$ be a bounded interval and $0\leq W\in L^p(I)$. If $-k_1^2,\ldots,-k_N^2$ are the first $N$ negative Dirichlet eigenvalues of $-d^2/dx^2-W$, ordered so that $k_1\geq\cdots\geq k_N>0$, then $$\label{spectral:dirichlet-bound} \sum_{j=1}^N k_j^{2\gamma} \leq \ell_\sigma\int_I W^p.$$
  - Explanation: Ordered negative Dirichlet eigenvalues obey the moment bound on a bounded interval under the stated potential assumptions.
- **C15** (bound, [source](#stmt-spectral-signed)): Let $V$ be real measurable with $V_-\in L^p(\mathbb R)$, and suppose $$\mathcal D_V =\left\{v\in H^1(\mathbb R):\int_\mathbb RV_+\|v\|^2<\infty\right\}$$ is dense in $L^2(\mathbb R)$. This holds in particular if $V_+\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb R)$. The form $$h_V[v]=\int_\mathbb R\|v'\|^2+\int_\mathbb RV_+\|v\|^2-\int_\mathbb RV_-\|v\|^2, \qquad v\in\mathcal D_V,$$ is closed and bounded below. Its associated operator $H_V$ has discrete negative spectrum and satisfies $$\operatorname{Tr}(H_V)_-^\gamma\leq\ell_\sigma\int_\mathbb RV_-^p.$$
  - Explanation: The negative-part moment bound extends to signed potentials on their dense weighted form domain. Local integrability of the positive part is sufficient for density.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Positive and negative parts** ([source](#sec:intro)): Write $t_+=\max\{t,0\}$ and $t_-=\max\{-t,0\}$.
- **Schrödinger form and associated operator** ([source](#sec:intro)): On the line, let $0\le W\in L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)$ and let $H_{-W}$ be the self-adjoint operator associated with the quadratic form $$h_{-W}[v]=\int_\mathbb R\|v'(x)\|^2\,dx-\int_\mathbb RW(x)\|v(x)\|^2\,dx, \qquad v\in H^1(\mathbb R).$$
- **Negative spectral moment** ([source](#sec:intro)): Write $$\operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma =\sum_{\lambda_j(H_{-W})<0}\|\lambda_j(H_{-W})\|^\gamma,$$ counting multiplicities; before the inequality is proved this sum is allowed to be infinite.
- **Optimal spectral constant** ([source](#sec:intro)): The optimal constant is $$L_{\gamma,1}= \sup_{\substack{0\le W\in L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)\\W\not\equiv0}} \frac{\operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma}{\displaystyle\int_\mathbb RW^{\gamma+1/2}}.$$
- **Hilbert–Schmidt matrix space** ([source](#sec:action)): Write $\operatorname{Sym}_N$ for the real symmetric $N\times N$ matrices, with inner product $\operatorname{tr}(AB)$ and norm $\\|A\\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=(\operatorname{tr}(A^2))^{1/2}$.
- **Matrix order** ([source](#sec:action)): The notation $A\le B$ means that $B-A$ is positive semidefinite.
- **Action functional** ([source](#sec:action)): For a positive diagonal matrix $K=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)$ and $a\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R^N)$, define $$\label{eq:action} \mathcal E_K(a)=\int_I\bigl(\|a'\|^2+a^{\mathsf T}K^2a-\|a\|^{2q}\bigr)\,dx.$$
- **Auxiliary scalar function and normalization** ([source](#eq:f)): Set $\beta=\sigma-\tfrac12\in(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and define $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ by $$\label{eq:f} f(0)=0,\qquad f'(y)=(\max\{y,0\}+\delta)^{\beta-1}, \qquad c=\frac{\sigma}{\displaystyle\int_\mathbb R(1+s^2)^{\beta-1}\,ds}.$$
- **Regularized matrix field** ([source](#eq:X-M)): For $B\in\operatorname{Sym}_N$, put $$\label{eq:X-M} \begin{split} X_s(B)&=K^2-2sB+s^2I_N,\\ M_R(B)&=c\int_{-R}^R\bigl(f(X_s(B))-f(s^2)I_N\bigr)\,ds,\\ M(B)&=\lim_{R\to\infty}M_R(B). \end{split}$$
- **Scalar comparison function** ([source](#pm-h-8)): Define $$\label{eq:mu} \mu(z)=c\int_\mathbb R\bigl(f(s^2+z)-f(s^2)\bigr)\,ds, \qquad z\in\mathbb R.$$
- **Spectral constants** ([source](#sec:spectral)): Write $$\label{spectral:constant} A_\sigma=\frac{\sigma^\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{1+\sigma}}, \qquad \ell_\sigma=\frac{A_\sigma}{\mathrm B(1/2,1+\sigma)},$$ where $\mathrm B$ denotes the beta function.

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I-spectral**: The sharp spectral moment estimate includes every negative eigenvalue with multiplicity.
- **I-action**: The action supremum over real lower triangular coefficient matrices bounds the sum of the individual scale integrals.
- **I-rankone**: The nonlinear action comparison requires exact equality between the regularized field and the outer product of the vector.
- **I-order**: The triangular spectral reduction uses eigenfunctions ordered from the lowest eigenvalue upward.
- **I-limit**: The penalty parameter is removed at fixed regularization before the regularization is removed.
- **I-signed**: The signed-potential extension bounds the spectral moment using the negative part on a dense weighted form domain.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **A-scope**: Apply the sharp constant to matrix-valued potentials or operators in other dimensions. -> The auxiliary matrices do not extend the scalar spectral theorem to those operators.
- **A-range**: Extend the main theorem to endpoint or lower exponents. -> The main theorem supplies no such extension.
- **A-discrete**: Treat a discretized spectrum as a certified continuum spectral moment. -> A finite discretization need not control omitted eigenvalues or approximation error.
- **A-signed**: Insert a signed potential directly into the nonnegative-potential formula. -> The relevant integral uses the negative part and the weighted form domain must be dense.
- **A-approximate**: Replace the exact rank-one field constraint by a numerically small residual. -> The comparison supplies no finite residual tolerance guaranteeing the same inequality.
- **A-optimizers**: Use the displayed equality potential to classify all optimizers. -> An exhibited equality case does not prove uniqueness or completeness.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `not_applicable`. The spectral estimate is a continuum inequality. The connecting path is obtained by parity continuation of a compact zero set, and the action argument removes the penalty parameter at fixed regularization before removing regularization. No finite algorithm computing that path, its coefficients, or the full negative spectral moment with certified error is supplied; the explicit equality potential establishes sharpness rather than such an algorithm.

  grad : ℝ → ℂ
  val_memLp : MemLp val 2 volume
  grad_memLp : MemLp grad 2 volume
  weak_derivative : ∀ φ : ℝ → ℂ, ContDiff ℝ (↑(⊤ : ℕ∞) : WithTop ℕ∞) φ → HasCompactSupport φ →
    (∫ x : ℝ, val x * deriv φ x) = -(∫ x : ℝ, grad x * φ x)

def l2Pairing (u v : H1) : ℂ := ∫ x : ℝ, star (u.val x) * v.val x

/-- The polarized form of -d²/dx² - W, on exactly H¹. -/
def schrodingerForm (W : ℝ → ℝ) (u v : H1) : ℂ :=
  (∫ x : ℝ, star (u.grad x) * v.grad x) -
    ∫ x : ℝ, (W x : ℂ) * star (u.val x) * v.val x

/-- Weak eigenfunction at the negative energy -k². -/
def IsNegativeEigenfunction (W : ℝ → ℝ) (k : ℝ) (u : H1) : Prop :=
  0 < k ∧ ∀ v : H1, schrodingerForm W v u = -(k ^ 2 : ℝ) * l2Pairing v u

def IsOrthonormalFamily {N : ℕ} (u : Fin N → H1) : Prop :=
  ∀ i j, l2Pairing (u i) (u j) = if i = j then 1 else 0

/-- An unlimited finite-family-size supremum, in ℝ≥0∞, includes the entire sum. -/
def negativeMoment (γ : ℝ) (W : ℝ → ℝ) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ (N : ℕ) (u : Fin N → H1) (k : Fin N → ℝ)
    (_ : IsOrthonormalFamily u) (_ : ∀ i, IsNegativeEigenfunction W (k i) (u i)),
      ∑ i, (ENNReal.ofReal (k i)) ^ (2 * γ)

/-- The moment retaining at most one negative eigenvalue. -/
def oneStateMoment (γ : ℝ) (W : ℝ → ℝ) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ (u : H1) (k : ℝ) (_ : l2Pairing u u = 1)
    (_ : IsNegativeEigenfunction W k u), (ENNReal.ofReal k) ^ (2 * γ)

def Admissible (γ : ℝ) (W : ℝ → ℝ) : Prop :=
  (∀ᵐ x : ℝ, 0 ≤ W x) ∧ MemLp W (ENNReal.ofReal (γ + 1 / 2)) volume

def potentialMass (γ : ℝ) (W : ℝ → ℝ) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ∫⁻ x : ℝ, (ENNReal.ofReal (W x)) ^ (γ + 1 / 2)

def semiclassicalConstant (γ : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  Real.Gamma (γ + 1) / (2 * Real.sqrt Real.pi * Real.Gamma (γ + 3 / 2))

def sharpConstant (γ : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  2 * ((γ - 1 / 2) / (γ + 1 / 2)) ^ (γ - 1 / 2) * semiclassicalConstant γ

def optimalConstant (γ : ℝ) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ (W : ℝ → ℝ) (_ : Admissible γ W) (_ : 0 < potentialMass γ W),
    negativeMoment γ W / potentialMass γ W

def oneStateConstant (γ : ℝ) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ (W : ℝ → ℝ) (_ : Admissible γ W) (_ : 0 < potentialMass γ W),
    oneStateMoment γ W / potentialMass γ W

def equalityPotential (γ : ℝ) (x : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  let r := (γ - 1 / 2)⁻¹
  (r + 1) * (Real.cosh (r * x))⁻¹ ^ 2

/-- The full spectral bound, optimal constants, and attainment at the explicit potential. -/

<a id="sec:intro"></a>
### Introduction

The Lieb–Thirring inequality bounds the negative eigenvalues of a Schrödinger operator by an integral of its potential. Write $t_+=\max\{t,0\}$ and $t_-=\max\{-t,0\}$. On the line, let $0\le W\in L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)$ and let $H_{-W}$ be the self-adjoint operator associated with the quadratic form $$h_{-W}[v]=\int_\mathbb R|v'(x)|^2\,dx-\int_\mathbb RW(x)|v(x)|^2\,dx,
 \qquad v\in H^1(\mathbb R).$$ For $\gamma>1/2$, this form is closed and bounded below, and its negative spectrum is discrete. We give the needed form and compactness arguments in Section <a href="#sec:spectral" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:spectral">7</a>. Write $$\operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma
   =\sum_{\lambda_j(H_{-W})<0}|\lambda_j(H_{-W})|^\gamma,$$ counting multiplicities; before the inequality is proved this sum is allowed to be infinite. The optimal constant is $$L_{\gamma,1}=
 \sup_{\substack{0\le W\in L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)\\W\not\equiv0}}
 \frac{\operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma}{\displaystyle\int_\mathbb RW^{\gamma+1/2}}.$$ Two natural comparisons are the semiclassical constant <a id="eq:semiclassical"></a>$$\label{eq:semiclassical}
 L_{\gamma,1}^{\mathrm{cl}}
 =\frac1{2\pi}\int_\mathbb R(1-\xi^2)_+^\gamma\,d\xi
 =\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{2\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\gamma+3/2)}$$ and the optimal constant when only the lowest eigenvalue is retained, denoted by $L_{\gamma,1}^{(1)}$. The one-dimensional Lieb–Thirring conjecture predicts the one-bound-state constant for $1/2<\gamma<3/2$ and the semiclassical constant for $\gamma\ge3/2$.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1**. *For every $1/2<\gamma<3/2$ and every nonnegative $W\in L^{\gamma+1/2}(\mathbb R)$, <a id="eq:main"></a>$$\label{eq:main}
 \operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma
 \le 2\left(\frac{\gamma-1/2}{\gamma+1/2}\right)^{\gamma-1/2}
       L_{\gamma,1}^{\mathrm{cl}}\int_\mathbb RW^{\gamma+1/2}.$$ The constant is optimal and equals $L_{\gamma,1}^{(1)}$. More precisely, if $r=(\gamma-1/2)^{-1}$, equality is attained by $W(x)=(r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx)$.*

</div>

The estimate includes rough, unbounded potentials and infinitely many negative eigenvalues. Corollary <a href="#spectral:signed" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="spectral:signed">15</a> gives the corresponding inequality for signed potentials on their natural dense weighted form domain. The theorem concerns scalar potentials on the line; the matrices used below encode finite orthonormal families. Its sharpness assertion exhibits an equality potential without classifying all optimizers.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
#### History and relation to prior work

Lieb and Thirring introduced these spectral inequalities in their work on the kinetic energy of fermions and the stability of matter (Lieb and Thirring 1975, 1976) <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[LiebThirring1975](#ref-LiebThirring1975), [LiebThirring1976](#ref-LiebThirring1976)]. At exponent one, optimizing over the potential makes the spectral inequality equivalent to a lower bound on the kinetic energy of an orthonormal family in terms of its density. On the line, for a finite orthonormal family $(u_j)\subset H^1(\mathbb R)$, this density is $\rho=\sum_j|u_j|^2$, and the corresponding kinetic bound controls $\int\rho^3$ by $\sum_j\int|u_j'|^2$. The one-state variational problem goes back to Keller (Keller 1961) <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[Keller1961](#ref-Keller1961)]. The conjecture asks whether allowing arbitrarily many bound states can improve on its constant in the lower exponent range. We verify the one-state equality potential and its normalization directly.

The scalar upper endpoint predates the general Lieb–Thirring inequalities. Inverse-scattering trace identities for the Korteweg–de Vries equation give the sharp value $L_{3/2,1}=3/16$ (Zakharov and Faddeev 1971; Gardner et al. 1974) <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[ZakharovFaddeev1971](#ref-ZakharovFaddeev1971), [GardnerGreeneKruskalMiura1974](#ref-GardnerGreeneKruskalMiura1974)]; Lieb and Thirring explicitly credit Gardner, Greene, Kruskal and Miura in (Lieb and Thirring 1976, sec. 4(B)) <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[LiebThirring1976](#ref-LiebThirring1976)]. Aizenman and Lieb’s monotonicity argument extends the semiclassical equality to all larger exponents (Aizenman and Lieb 1978) <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[AizenmanLieb1978](#ref-AizenmanLieb1978)]. At the other endpoint, Weidl first proved finiteness of $L_{1/2,1}$ (Weidl 1996) <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[Weidl1996](#ref-Weidl1996)], and Hundertmark, Lieb and Thomas obtained its sharp value $1/2$ (Hundertmark, Lieb, and Thomas 1998, Theorem 1) <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[HundertmarkLiebThomas1998](#ref-HundertmarkLiebThomas1998)]. Laptev and Weidl established the operator-valued upper-endpoint estimate and used dimension lifting to obtain the sharp semiclassical constants for every dimension and $\gamma\ge3/2$ (Laptev and Weidl 2000, Theorems 2.1, 2.2 and 3.1) <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[LaptevWeidl2000](#ref-LaptevWeidl2000)].

Quantitative estimates and orthonormal-function methods narrowed the remaining gap. Hundertmark, Laptev and Weidl proved $L_{\gamma,1}\le2L_{\gamma,1}^{\mathrm{cl}}$ for $1/2\le\gamma<3/2$ (Hundertmark, Laptev, and Weidl 2000, Theorem 4.1) <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[HLW2000](#ref-HLW2000)]. At exponent one, Dolbeault, Laptev and Loss extended the method of Eden and Foias to matrix-valued potentials (Eden and Foias 1991; Dolbeault, Laptev, and Loss 2008) <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[EdenFoias1991](#ref-EdenFoias1991), [DLL2008](#ref-DLL2008)]. Their all-dimensional semiclassical ratio was $\pi/\sqrt3$; subsequent bounds were $1.456$ by Frank, Hundertmark, Jex and Nam (Frank et al. 2021, Theorem 1) <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[FHJN2021](#ref-FHJN2021)] and the reported $1.44655$ of Corso and Ried (Corso and Ried 2025, Corollary 1.7) <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[CorsoRied2025](#ref-CorsoRied2025)]. These bounds preceded the sharp exponent-one theorem described below. In the full one-dimensional strict interval, Levitt’s numerical investigation observed convergence to the one-state profile or splitting into increasingly separated copies (Levitt 2014, sec. 4.1) <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[Levitt2014](#ref-Levitt2014)]. Such computations support the conjectured value but do not bound the contribution of every orthonormal family. That uniform bound is the obstacle addressed by our action inequality.

Read and Schulz proved the case $\gamma=1$ in (Read and Schulz 2026, Theorem 1) <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[ReadSchulz2026](#ref-ReadSchulz2026)], obtaining the sharp constant $4/(3\sqrt3\pi)$. Their proof extends Benguria and Loss’s scalar cumulative-mass argument (Benguria and Loss 2004, sec. 2) <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[BenguriaLoss2004](#ref-BenguriaLoss2004)] to orthonormal families through a matrix correction to the kinetic energy. Their operator-valued extension also gives the semiclassical ratio $2/\sqrt3$ in every dimension at exponent one (Read and Schulz 2026, Theorem 2 and Section 2) <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[ReadSchulz2026](#ref-ReadSchulz2026)]. Our proof retains the individual eigenvalue scales in a finite-interval action inequality. Its main analytic step is a rank-one comparison for an energy-dependent regularized matrix field. A continuation argument chooses a lower triangular coefficient matrix and a path joining two prescribed matrix endpoints. Together these yield the action inequality for every $1/2<\gamma<3/2$. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> therefore resolves the remaining open-interval cases of the one-dimensional Lieb–Thirring conjecture positively; the case $\gamma=1$ is already covered by Read–Schulz.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
#### Proof strategy

Put $\sigma=\gamma-1/2\in(0,1)$. The central result is a variational inequality on a bounded interval, independent of any potential. Fix $I=(x_-,x_+)$. Given $N$ orthonormal real functions $u_i\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R)$ and positive numbers $k_i$, let $u=(u_i)_{i=1}^N$ and $K=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(k_i)$. We prove that the action $$\mathcal E_K(a)=\int_I\bigl(|a'|^2+a^{\mathsf T}K^2a
                         -|a|^{2+2/\sigma}\bigr)\,dx$$ has supremum at least $\sum_i\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds$ among functions $a=Cu$ with $C$ lower triangular. Section <a href="#sec:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action">2</a> states this precisely in Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> and explains the role of triangularity.

The difficulty is to obtain this lower bound for an arbitrary orthonormal family while retaining its individual scales $k_i$. The argument replaces a direct optimization over $C$ by a path with prescribed matrix endpoints. Section <a href="#sec:field" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:field">3</a> constructs a regularized map $B\mapsto M_\delta(B)$ from real symmetric $N\times N$ matrices to themselves, and a scalar primitive whose change from $K$ to $-K$ gives the required endpoint integral. Section <a href="#sec:rankone" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:rankone">4</a> proves the analytic comparison that controls the nonlinear action term when $M_\delta(B)=aa^{\mathsf T}$. Its integrated tangent map is an average of unitary conjugations; a trace inequality removes this average from the comparison.

Section <a href="#sec:path" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:path">5</a> chooses $C$ and a path from $K$ to $-K$ by continuation modulo row signs. Compactness and the exclusion of zero rows make the continuation possible even when $C$ loses rank. Section <a href="#sec:action-limit" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action-limit">6</a> combines the path with the primitive: failure of the rank-one constraint contributes a negative square to the action identity. Compactness allows the penalty parameter to tend to zero at fixed regularization. Only then is the regularization removed.

Section <a href="#sec:spectral" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:spectral">7</a> returns to the potential. Take the $u_i$ to be Dirichlet eigenfunctions with eigenvalues $-k_i^2$, ordered so that $k_1\ge\cdots\ge k_N$. Lower triangularity lets the eigenfunction equations bound the quadratic part of the action by $\int W|a|^2$. A scalar maximization bounds the whole action by a multiple of $\int W^{1+\sigma}$. Finally, cutoff trial spaces pass from bounded intervals to the line, and finite partial sums exhaust all negative eigenvalues. The sharpness calculation uses the explicit potential in Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.

<a id="sec:action"></a>
### The finite-interval action inequality

Fix $$0<\sigma<1,\qquad p=1+\sigma,\qquad q=1+\frac1\sigma.$$ All matrices below are finite dimensional. Write $\operatorname{Sym}_N$ for the real symmetric $N\times N$ matrices, with inner product $\operatorname{tr}(AB)$ and norm $\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=(\operatorname{tr}(A^2))^{1/2}$. The notation $A\le B$ means that $B-A$ is positive semidefinite. Vectors are columns, and $|a|$ is their Euclidean norm.

Let $I=(x_-,x_+)$ be a bounded interval. For a positive diagonal matrix $K=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)$ and $a\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R^N)$, define <a id="eq:action"></a>$$\label{eq:action}
 \mathcal E_K(a)=\int_I\bigl(|a'|^2+a^{\mathsf T}K^2a-|a|^{2q}\bigr)\,dx.$$ In one dimension $H^1_0(I)$ embeds into the continuous functions, so every term in this integral is finite.

<a id="stmt-thm-action"></a>
<div id="thm:action" class="theorem">

<a id="eq:action-bound"></a>
**Theorem 2** (Action inequality). *Let $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_N)^{\mathsf T}\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R^N)$ satisfy $\int_I uu^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_N$, and let $k_i>0$. Then $$\label{eq:action-bound}
 \sup_{C\text{ lower triangular}}\mathcal E_K(Cu)
 \ge \sum_{i=1}^N\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}(k_i^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds.$$ The supremum is over all real lower triangular $N\times N$ matrices.*

</div>

The restriction to lower triangular matrices is what makes this inequality useful spectrally. If the $u_i$ are eigenfunctions ordered from the lowest eigenvalue upward, the $i$th component of $Cu$ stays in the span of the first $i$ eigenfunctions. Section <a href="#sec:spectral" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:spectral">7</a> will use this observation to bound the left side from above by an integral of the potential.

<a id="eq:framework-gradient"></a>
<a id="eq:framework-path"></a>
Here is the construction behind the lower bound. For each $\delta>0$, Section <a href="#sec:field" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:field">3</a> builds a locally Lipschitz map $M:\operatorname{Sym}_N\to\operatorname{Sym}_N$ and a $C^1$ function $J:\operatorname{Sym}_N\to\mathbb R$ such that $$\label{eq:framework-gradient}
 dJ(B)[H]=-\operatorname{tr}(M(B)H).$$ They have two decisive properties. First, Section <a href="#sec:rankone" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:rankone">4</a> proves <a id="eq:framework-rankone"></a>$$\label{eq:framework-rankone}
 M(B)=aa^{\mathsf T}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 a^{\mathsf T}(K^2-B^2)a\ge |a|^{2q}.$$ Second, Section <a href="#sec:path" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:path">5</a> finds, for every $\varepsilon>0$, a lower triangular $C$ and a path $B$ satisfying $$\label{eq:framework-path}
 \varepsilon B'=M(B)-(Cu)(Cu)^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 B(x_-)=K,\quad B(x_+)=-K.$$ The small parameter $\varepsilon$ penalizes failure of the rank-one constraint. For $a=Cu$, differentiating $J(B)+a^{\mathsf T}Ba$ along this path produces the negative term $-\varepsilon^{-1}\|M(B)-aa^{\mathsf T}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2$. Uniform bounds on the path and on $C$ then give $$\sup_C\mathcal E_K(Cu)\ge J(-K)-J(K).$$ Finally, the boundary difference tends to the right side of <a href="#eq:action-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:action-bound">[eq:action-bound]</a> as $\delta\downarrow0$. We keep $\delta$ fixed until after the limit $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.

<a id="sec:field"></a>
### A regularized matrix field

We construct the field and primitive used in Section <a href="#sec:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action">2</a>. Throughout this section $K=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)>0$ and $\delta>0$ are fixed. The scalar case explains the choice of field. For $N=1$, write $K=(k)$. If $-k\le b\le k$, putting $m=(k^2-b^2)^\sigma$ gives $$m(k^2-b^2)=m^{1+1/\sigma}.$$ Thus this field gives equality in the scalar version of the rank-one comparison, and a primitive with derivative $-m$ has the required change $\int_{-k}^k(k^2-b^2)^\sigma\,db$ from $k$ to $-k$. We seek matrix versions of the comparison and endpoint identity, with a regularization removed at the end.

<a id="eq:f"></a>
The identity $$k^2-2sb+s^2=(s-b)^2+k^2-b^2$$ suggests integrating a scalar function of the quadratic on the left: its affine dependence on $b$ will let us construct a trace primitive even when the corresponding matrices $K$ and $B$ do not commute. Set $\beta=\sigma-\tfrac12\in(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and define $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ by $$\label{eq:f}
 f(0)=0,\qquad f'(y)=(\max\{y,0\}+\delta)^{\beta-1},
 \qquad
 c=\frac{\sigma}{\displaystyle\int_\mathbb R(1+s^2)^{\beta-1}\,ds}.$$ The exponent and normalization are chosen so that, for $z\ge0$, scaling the integration variable gives $$c\int_\mathbb Rf'(s^2+z)\,ds
 =\sigma(z+\delta)^{\beta-1/2}
 =\sigma(z+\delta)^{\sigma-1}.$$ Integration in $z$ therefore produces the regularized power $(z+\delta)^\sigma-\delta^\sigma$. On the scalar interval $[-k,k]$, we will recover this expression with $z=k^2-b^2$. Keeping $f'$ constant for negative arguments extends the construction beyond that interval, as needed for the continuation argument. The resulting $f$ is $C^1$, strictly increasing, and concave on $\mathbb R$. The integral defining $c$ is finite because $\beta<1/2$; the definition also includes $\beta=0$ without a limiting convention.

<a id="eq:X-M"></a>
For $B\in\operatorname{Sym}_N$, put $$\label{eq:X-M}
 \begin{split}
 X_s(B)&=K^2-2sB+s^2I_N,\\
 M_R(B)&=c\int_{-R}^R\bigl(f(X_s(B))-f(s^2)I_N\bigr)\,ds,\\
 M(B)&=\lim_{R\to\infty}M_R(B).
 \end{split}$$ Here and below scalar functions of symmetric or Hermitian matrices are defined by the spectral theorem. The symmetric cutoff in <a href="#eq:X-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:X-M">[eq:X-M]</a> is part of the definition.

<a id="pm-h-7"></a>
#### Convergence and a primitive

We first record an elementary estimate that keeps the regularity argument in the Hilbert–Schmidt norm.

<a id="stmt-lem-hs-calculus"></a>
<div id="lem:HS-calculus" class="lemma">

**Lemma 3**. *If a real function $h$ is $L$-Lipschitz on an interval containing the spectra of two Hermitian matrices $A$ and $B$, then $\|h(A)-h(B)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\le L\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{HS}}$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-hs-calculus"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-hs-calculus</summary>

*Proof.* Choose orthonormal eigenbases $v_i$ and $w_j$ of $A$ and $B$, with respective eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and $\nu_j$. In these two bases the entries of $h(A)-h(B)$ and $A-B$ are, respectively, $$(h(\lambda_i)-h(\nu_j))\langle v_i,w_j\rangle,
 \qquad (\lambda_i-\nu_j)\langle v_i,w_j\rangle.$$ Square, sum over $i,j$, and apply the scalar Lipschitz bound. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-lem-field-regularity"></a>
<div id="lem:field-regularity" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:J"></a>
**Lemma 4**. *The limit in <a href="#eq:X-M" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:X-M">[eq:X-M]</a> is locally uniform. The resulting map $M$ is locally Lipschitz. Moreover, $$\label{eq:J}
 J(B)=-\int_0^1\operatorname{tr}\bigl(BM(tB)\bigr)\,dt$$ is $C^1$ and satisfies <a href="#eq:framework-gradient" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:framework-gradient">[eq:framework-gradient]</a>.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-field-regularity"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-field-regularity</summary>

<a id="eq:tail"></a>
*Proof.* Fix a bounded set of $B$’s. For sufficiently large $|s|$, the spectrum of $H_s(B)=K^2-2sB$ lies in $[-A|s|,A|s|]$, with $A$ independent of $B$. On this interval set $$h_s(t)=f(s^2+t)-f(s^2)-f'(s^2)t.$$ All arguments of $f$ are positive. Since $f''(y)=(\beta-1)(y+\delta)^{\beta-2}$ for $y>0$, the mean value theorem gives $$\operatorname{Lip}(h_s)=O(|s|^{2\beta-3}),\qquad
 \sup_{|t|\le A|s|}|h_s(t)|=O(|s|^{2\beta-2}).$$ It follows that $$\label{eq:tail}
 f(X_s(B))-f(s^2)I_N=-2s f'(s^2)B+R_s(B),$$ where $R_s(B)=f'(s^2)K^2+h_s(H_s(B))$ has norm $O(|s|^{2\beta-2})$. By Lemma <a href="#lem:HS-calculus" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:HS-calculus">3</a>, its Lipschitz constant as a function of $B$ has the same bound: the factor $2|s|$ from $H_s$ multiplies $O(|s|^{2\beta-3})$. The first term on the right of <a href="#eq:tail" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:tail">[eq:tail]</a> is odd in $s$ and cancels at every symmetric cutoff; the remainder is integrable. On bounded $s$-intervals, $f$ is Lipschitz and the same lemma applies directly. This proves both assertions about $M$.

For completeness we justify the primitive without differentiating $M$. Let $F$ be any $C^2$ primitive of $f$. The trace differentiation formula is <a id="eq:trace-derivative"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-derivative}
 d\operatorname{tr}F(A)[H]=\operatorname{tr}(f(A)H).$$ For polynomials it follows by cyclicity of trace. Approximation of $F$ and $F'$ on a compact spectral interval by a polynomial and its derivative proves the general formula. For $s\ne0$, the map $B\mapsto f(X_s(B))-f(s^2)I_N$ is therefore a gradient: a potential is $$-\frac{1}{2s}\operatorname{tr}F(X_s(B))-f(s^2)\operatorname{tr}B.$$ At $s=0$ the field is constant and is again a gradient. Consequently each $M_R$ has zero integral around every piecewise $C^1$ closed curve, by integration first along the curve and then in $s$. Its negative has the primitive $J_R(B)=-\int_0^1\operatorname{tr}(BM_R(tB))\,dt$. Local uniform convergence gives $J_R\to J$ and $M_R\to M$ on compact sets. Passing to the limit in the line-segment identity for $J_R$ yields $$J(B+H)-J(B)=-\int_0^1\operatorname{tr}(M(B+tH)H)\,dt.$$ Continuity of $M$ proves that $J$ is $C^1$ with the stated derivative. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-8"></a>
#### Scalar comparison and boundary values

Define <a id="eq:mu"></a>$$\label{eq:mu}
 \mu(z)=c\int_\mathbb R\bigl(f(s^2+z)-f(s^2)\bigr)\,ds,
 \qquad z\in\mathbb R.$$ This integral is absolutely convergent: on bounded $z$-sets its integrand has tail $O(|s|^{2\beta-2})$.

<a id="stmt-lem-mu"></a>
<div id="lem:mu" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:mu-positive"></a>
<a id="eq:shift"></a>
**Lemma 5**. *The function $\mu$ is locally Lipschitz and strictly increasing, with $\mu(0)=0$. For $z\ge0$, $$\label{eq:mu-positive}
 \mu(z)=(z+\delta)^\sigma-\delta^\sigma,
 \qquad \mu'(z)=\sigma(z+\delta)^{\sigma-1}.$$ For every fixed $b,z\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:shift}
 c\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^R
 \bigl(f((s-b)^2+z)-f(s^2)\bigr)\,ds=\mu(z).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-mu"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-mu</summary>

*Proof.* The tail estimate above, also for differences in $z$, proves local Lipschitz continuity. Strict increase follows from strict increase of $f$ at every $s$. Differentiation under the integral for $z\ge0$ gives $$\mu'(z)=c\int_\mathbb R(s^2+z+\delta)^{\beta-1}\,ds
 =\sigma(z+\delta)^{\beta-1/2}
 =\sigma(z+\delta)^{\sigma-1}.$$ Integration from zero proves <a href="#eq:mu-positive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:mu-positive">[eq:mu-positive]</a>.

For the shift, write $g(s)=f(s^2+z)$. It is even, and $g'(s)=2s f'(s^2+z)=O(|s|^{2\beta-1})\to0$ as $|s|\to\infty$. Suppose first that $b\ge0$. The change in the integral of $g$ under the shift is $$\int_R^{R+b}g(t)\,dt-\int_{R-b}^{R}g(t)\,dt.$$ Its absolute value is at most $b^2\sup_{|t-R|\le b}|g'(t)|$, which tends to zero. Evenness handles $b<0$ as well. Subtracting $f(s^2)$ now proves <a href="#eq:shift" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:shift">[eq:shift]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-lem-jensen"></a>
<div id="lem:jensen" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:jensen"></a>
<a id="eq:common-eigenvector"></a>
**Lemma 6** (Directional comparison). *For every $B\in\operatorname{Sym}_N$ and every real unit vector $e$, $$\label{eq:jensen}
 e^{\mathsf T}M(B)e\le
 \mu\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\bigr).$$ If a standard basis vector $e_i$ is an eigenvector of $B$ with eigenvalue $b_i$, then $$\label{eq:common-eigenvector}
 M(B)e_i=\mu(k_i^2-b_i^2)e_i.$$ For the primitive in <a href="#eq:J" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:J">[eq:J]</a>, <a id="eq:boundary-J"></a>$$\label{eq:boundary-J}
 J(-K)-J(K)=\sum_{i=1}^N\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}\mu(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-jensen"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-jensen</summary>

*Proof.* Scalar concavity and the spectral decomposition of $X_s(B)$ give $$e^{\mathsf T}f(X_s(B))e\le f(e^{\mathsf T}X_s(B)e).$$ Set $b=e^{\mathsf T}Be$ and $z=e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-b^2$. The scalar argument on the right is $(s-b)^2+z$. Integrate at a symmetric cutoff and use Lemma <a href="#lem:mu" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:mu">5</a> to obtain <a href="#eq:jensen" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:jensen">[eq:jensen]</a>. This uses scalar concavity only; no operator concavity on the whole real line is asserted.

For <a href="#eq:common-eigenvector" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:common-eigenvector">[eq:common-eigenvector]</a>, $e_i$ is also an eigenvector of $X_s(B)$, with eigenvalue $(s-b_i)^2+k_i^2-b_i^2$. Functional calculus and <a href="#eq:shift" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:shift">[eq:shift]</a> give the identity. Finally, restrict $dJ=-\operatorname{tr}(M\,dB)$ to diagonal matrices and integrate along the diagonal segment from $K$ to $-K$. Each coordinate contributes $$-\int_{k_i}^{-k_i}\mu(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds
 =\int_{-k_i}^{k_i}\mu(k_i^2-s^2)\,ds,$$ as claimed. ◻

</details>

In particular $M(K)=M(-K)=0$. We shall also use the following symmetry. If $S$ is a diagonal matrix with entries $\pm1$, then $SKS=K$ and <a id="eq:sign-equivariance"></a>$$\label{eq:sign-equivariance}
 M(SBS)=SM(B)S.$$ This follows already at each cutoff. The matrix $K$ stays fixed throughout; we do not require equivariance under conjugations that move $K$.

<a id="sec:rankone"></a>
### The rank-one comparison

The field $M$ was chosen so that its rank-one values control the nonlinear term in the action. We now prove that property. The parameters $K,\sigma$ and $\delta$ remain fixed as in Section <a href="#sec:field" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:field">3</a>.

<a id="stmt-prop-rankone"></a>
<div id="prop:rankone" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:rankone"></a>
**Proposition 7**. *For every $B\in\operatorname{Sym}_N$ and every $a\in\mathbb R^N$, $$\label{eq:rankone}
 M(B)=aa^{\mathsf T}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 a^{\mathsf T}(K^2-B^2)a\ge |a|^{2q}.$$*

</div>

The proof compares $X_s(B)$ with a matrix that is a function of $B$ alone. Its tangent map, integrated in $s$, is an average of unitary conjugations. Trace monotonicity then removes this average from the needed inequality.

<a id="pm-h-10"></a>
#### A tangent inequality on positive matrices

Divided differences describe the derivative of matrix functional calculus, as in the work of Daleckii and Krein (Daleckii and Krein 1965) <a id="pm-cite-17"></a>[[DaleckiiKrein1965](#ref-DaleckiiKrein1965)]; see (Hiai 2010, Theorem 2.3.1) <a id="pm-cite-18"></a>[[Hiai2010](#ref-Hiai2010)] for a modern finite-dimensional formulation. We prove the required derivative formula and tangent inequality directly from the resolvent representation below.

<a id="stmt-lem-resolvent-tangent"></a>
<div id="lem:resolvent-tangent" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:resolvent-f"></a>
<a id="eq:tangent"></a>
<a id="eq:divdiff"></a>
**Lemma 8**. *For $y\ge0$ the function $f$ of <a href="#eq:f" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:f">[eq:f]</a> has the representation $$\label{eq:resolvent-f}
 f(y)=c_\beta\int_0^\infty v^\beta
 \left(\frac1{\delta+v}-\frac1{y+\delta+v}\right)\,dv,
 \qquad
 c_\beta^{-1}=\int_0^\infty\frac{t^\beta}{(1+t)^2}\,dt.$$ If $X,Y\ge0$ are Hermitian matrices, then $$\label{eq:tangent}
 f(X)\le f(Y)+T_Y(X-Y),$$ where, in an eigenbasis of $Y$ with eigenvalues $y_i$, $$\label{eq:divdiff}
 (T_Y Z)_{ij}=f[y_i,y_j]Z_{ij},\qquad
 f[x,y]=\int_0^1 f'(\tau x+(1-\tau)y)\,d\tau.$$ For $x\ne y$, this is $(f(x)-f(y))/(x-y)$; for $x=y$ it is $f'(x)$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-resolvent-tangent"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-resolvent-tangent</summary>

*Proof.* The integrand in <a href="#eq:resolvent-f" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:resolvent-f">[eq:resolvent-f]</a> is $O(v^\beta)$ near zero and $O(v^{\beta-2})$ at infinity. The range $-1<\beta<1$ suffices for convergence. Differentiation gives $$c_\beta\int_0^\infty\frac{v^\beta}{(y+\delta+v)^2}\,dv
 =(y+\delta)^{\beta-1}=f'(y),$$ and both sides of <a href="#eq:resolvent-f" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:resolvent-f">[eq:resolvent-f]</a> vanish at zero.

Put $A=Y+(\delta+v)I$ and $Z=X-Y$. Since $A$ and $A+Z$ are positive definite, the resolvent identity gives $$-(A+Z)^{-1}
 =-A^{-1}+A^{-1}ZA^{-1}
   -A^{-1}Z(A+Z)^{-1}ZA^{-1}
 \le -A^{-1}+A^{-1}ZA^{-1}.$$ Integrating proves <a href="#eq:tangent" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:tangent">[eq:tangent]</a>. The coefficient multiplying $Z_{ij}$ in its linear term is $$c_\beta\int_0^\infty
 \frac{v^\beta}{(y_i+\delta+v)(y_j+\delta+v)}\,dv=f[y_i,y_j].$$ This also covers repeated eigenvalues. All these integrals converge absolutely, since $\delta>0$. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-11"></a>
#### The integrated tangent map

Suppose that $M(B)=aa^{\mathsf T}$ and $m=|a|^2>0$. By Lemma <a href="#lem:jensen" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:jensen">6</a>, for every unit $e$, $$0\le e^{\mathsf T}M(B)e
 \le\mu\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\bigr).$$ Strict increase of $\mu$ implies $e^{\mathsf T}K^2e\ge(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2$. Consequently <a id="eq:X-positive"></a>$$\label{eq:X-positive}
 e^{\mathsf T}X_s(B)e
 =(s-e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2
   +e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\ge0$$ for every real $s$: the matrices $X_s(B)$ are positive semidefinite.

To apply the tangent inequality, we compare $X_s(B)$ with $(sI-B)^2+dI$, which is diagonal in an eigenbasis of $B$. Choose $d>0$ so that its integrated scalar value is $\mu(d)=m$: <a id="eq:D-d-Y"></a>$$\label{eq:D-d-Y}
 D=K^2-B^2,\qquad
 d=(m+\delta^\sigma)^{1/\sigma}-\delta,\qquad
 Y_s=(sI-B)^2+dI.$$ This choice matches the nonzero eigenvalue of $M(B)$. When we pair the integrated tangent inequality with $M(B)$, the identity $M(B)^2=mM(B)$ will cancel the contribution $mI$ against the left side. We have $X_s(B)=Y_s+(D-dI)$; we do not assert that $D\ge0$.

<a id="eq:M-tangent"></a>
Apply Lemma <a href="#lem:resolvent-tangent" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:resolvent-tangent">8</a> to $X_s(B)\ge0$ and $Y_s>0$, and denote the tangent map at $Y_s$ by $T_s$. Subtract $f(s^2)I$ and integrate symmetrically. In an eigenbasis of $B$, the shift identity <a href="#eq:shift" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:shift">[eq:shift]</a> gives $c\int_\mathbb R(f(Y_s)-f(s^2)I)\,ds=mI$, with the integral understood at symmetric cutoffs. Thus $$\label{eq:M-tangent}
 M(B)\le mI+\mu'(d)\mathcal P(D-dI),
 \qquad \mathcal P=\frac{c}{\mu'(d)}\int_\mathbb RT_s\,ds.$$ The integral of the tangent maps converges absolutely: for large $|s|$, their matrix coefficients are $O(|s|^{2\beta-2})$.

<a id="stmt-lem-average"></a>
<div id="lem:average" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:P-kernel"></a>
<a id="eq:P-average"></a>
**Lemma 9**. *In an eigenbasis of $B$ with eigenvalues $z_i$, the map $\mathcal P$ is $$\label{eq:P-kernel}
 (\mathcal P Z)_{ij}=P_{ij}Z_{ij},\qquad
 P_{ij}=\int_0^1
 \left(1+\frac{\tau(1-\tau)(z_i-z_j)^2}{d+\delta}\right)^{\sigma-1}\,d\tau.$$ There is an even probability measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb R$ such that $$\label{eq:P-average}
 \mathcal P(Z)=\int_\mathbb Re^{itB}Ze^{-itB}\,d\nu(t).$$ In particular $\mathcal P(I)=I$, and $\mathcal P$ is self-adjoint for the trace pairing on Hermitian matrices.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-average"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-average</summary>

*Proof.* The eigenvalues of $Y_s$ are $y_i=(s-z_i)^2+d$. Formula <a href="#eq:divdiff" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:divdiff">[eq:divdiff]</a> yields $$f[y_i,y_j]=\int_0^1
 \bigl((s-\tau z_i-(1-\tau)z_j)^2+d+\delta
       +\tau(1-\tau)(z_i-z_j)^2\bigr)^{\beta-1}\,d\tau.$$ These scalar integrands are nonnegative. Tonelli’s theorem and a shift in $s$ evaluate their integral by the same calculation as $\mu'(d)$ in Lemma <a href="#lem:mu" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:mu">5</a>, giving <a href="#eq:P-kernel" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:P-kernel">[eq:P-kernel]</a>.

For $h\ge0$ and $x\in\mathbb R$, the gamma integral gives <a id="eq:gamma-mixture"></a>$$\label{eq:gamma-mixture}
 (1+hx^2)^{\sigma-1}
 =\frac1{\Gamma(1-\sigma)}
   \int_0^\infty v^{-\sigma}e^{-v}e^{-vhx^2}\,dv.$$ The density $v^{-\sigma}e^{-v}/\Gamma(1-\sigma)$ has total mass one. For fixed $v,h$, $e^{-vhx^2}$ is the characteristic function of a centered Gaussian with variance $2vh$; when $h=0$, use the point mass at zero. Choose $\tau$ uniformly in $[0,1]$ and put $h=\tau(1-\tau)/(d+\delta)$. The resulting mixture of these symmetric Gaussian measures is an even probability measure $\nu$, independent of $i,j$. Its characteristic function at $z_i-z_j$ is precisely $P_{ij}$, which proves <a href="#eq:P-average" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:P-average">[eq:P-average]</a>. The diagonal coefficients are one. Finally, changing $t$ to $-t$ in <a href="#eq:P-average" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:P-average">[eq:P-average]</a> and using cyclicity of trace proves self-adjointness. ◻

</details>

#### Trace monotonicity and contraction

**Lemma 10**. *With $B,D$ and $\mathcal P$ as above, <a id="eq:trace-average"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-average}
 \operatorname{tr}(M(B)D)\ge\operatorname{tr}(M(B)\mathcal P(D)).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-trace-average"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-trace-average</summary>

*Proof.* We use an elementary instance of the generalized Klein trace inequality (Petz 1994, Proposition 3, p. 289) <a id="pm-cite-19"></a>[[Petz1994](#ref-Petz1994)]: for any increasing real function $g$ and Hermitian matrices $A_1,A_2$, <a id="eq:trace-monotonicity"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-monotonicity}
 \operatorname{tr}\bigl((g(A_1)-g(A_2))(A_1-A_2)\bigr)\ge0.$$ Indeed, in eigenbases of the two matrices the trace equals $$\sum_{i,j}(g(\lambda_i)-g(\nu_j))(\lambda_i-\nu_j)
       |\langle v_i,w_j\rangle|^2\ge0.$$ Apply this to $g=f$, $A_1=UX_s(B)U^*$ and $A_2=X_s(B)$, with $U=e^{itB}$. Since $U$ commutes with $(sI-B)^2$, expansion of the trace gives <a id="eq:trace-fixed-s"></a>$$\label{eq:trace-fixed-s}
 0\le\operatorname{tr}\bigl(f(X_s(B))(2D-UDU^*-U^*DU)\bigr).$$ The coefficient $2D-UDU^*-U^*DU$ has trace zero. We may therefore subtract $f(s^2)I$ inside the trace, integrate over $[-R,R]$, and pass to the symmetric-cutoff limit defining $M$. The result is $$0\le\operatorname{tr}\bigl(M(B)(2D-UDU^*-U^*DU)\bigr).$$ This passage is also uniform in $t$: the coefficient has norm at most $4\|D\|_{\mathrm{HS}}$, whereas $M_R(B)\to M(B)$ in matrix norm. Average with respect to the even measure $\nu$ from Lemma <a href="#lem:average" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:average">9</a>. Both conjugation terms average to $\mathcal P(D)$, proving the claim. ◻

</details>

*Proof of Proposition <a href="#prop:rankone" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:rankone">7</a>.* If $a=0$, both sides of <a href="#eq:rankone" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:rankone">[eq:rankone]</a> vanish. Otherwise the preceding construction applies. Multiply the matrix inequality <a href="#eq:M-tangent" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:M-tangent">[eq:M-tangent]</a> by the positive semidefinite matrix $M=aa^{\mathsf T}$ and take its trace.  \ge\operatorname{tr}(M\mathcal P(D))\ge d\operatorname{tr}M=dm.$$ For nonnegative $x,y$ and $0<\sigma<1$, $(x+y)^\sigma\le x^\sigma+y^\sigma$. Apply this with $x=d$, $y=\delta$ to get $m=(d+\delta)^\sigma-\delta^\sigma\le d^\sigma$. Thus $dm\ge m^{1+1/\sigma}=|a|^{2q}$, completing the proof. ◻

### A matrix path with prescribed endpoints

The action argument needs a symmetric matrix path from $K$ to $-K$. Its differential equation also contains a lower triangular coefficient matrix $C$, which we are free to choose. We choose $C$ by matching the terminal endpoint. At the start of a deformation, the matching problem has exactly one solution after identifying each row of $C$ with its negative. A compactness argument shows that this solution count cannot disappear modulo two.

<a id="path:equivariance"></a>
<a id="path:comparison"></a>
<a id="path:coordinate"></a>
Fix $\delta>0$ throughout the remaining construction. We use the matrix field $M=M_\delta$ and scalar function $\mu=\mu_\delta$ constructed above. Recall the properties needed here. The field $M$ is locally Lipschitz on $\operatorname{Sym}_N$, the space of real symmetric $N\times N$ matrices. The function $\mu$ is locally Lipschitz and strictly increasing, with $\mu(0)=0$. For $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)$ and every diagonal sign matrix $S$, $$\label{path:equivariance}
 M(SBS)=SM(B)S.$$ For every real unit vector $e$, $$\label{path:comparison}
 e^{\mathsf T}M(B)e
 \leq \mu\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-(e^{\mathsf T}Be)^2\bigr).$$ Finally, writing $e_i$ for the $i$th standard basis vector, $$\label{path:coordinate}
 Be_i=t e_i
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 M(B)e_i=\mu(k_i^2-t^2)e_i.$$ The equivariance in <a href="#path:equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:equivariance">[path:equivariance]</a> only concerns sign matrices, which commute with the fixed matrix $K$.

<a id="stmt-path-existence"></a>
<div id="path:existence" class="proposition">

<a id="path:equation"></a>
<a id="path:operator-bound"></a>
**Proposition 11** (Connecting path). *Let $I=(x_-,x_+)$ be a bounded interval, let $u\in H^1_0(I;\mathbb R^N)$ satisfy $$\int_I u(x)u(x)^{\mathsf T}\,dx=I_N,$$ and let $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)$ with every $k_i>0$. Suppose $M$ and $\mu$ have the regularity and properties <a href="#path:equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:equivariance">[path:equivariance]</a>–<a href="#path:coordinate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:coordinate">[path:coordinate]</a> just stated. For every $\varepsilon>0$, there are a real lower triangular matrix $C$ and a path $B\in C^1(\overline I;\operatorname{Sym}_N)$ such that, with $a=Cu$, $$\label{path:equation}
 \varepsilon B'=M(B)-aa^{\mathsf T},
 \qquad B(x_-)=K,\qquad B(x_+)=-K.$$ These choices satisfy $$\label{path:operator-bound}
 \|B(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|K\|_{\mathrm{op}}
 \qquad(x\in I).$$ For fixed $M$, $K$, and $I$, the matrices $C$ can be chosen bounded uniformly for $0<\varepsilon\leq1$.*

</div>

The parity principle is the compact-one-manifold argument underlying mod-two degree; see (Milnor 1965, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-20"></a>[[Milnor1965](#ref-Milnor1965)]. The smooth regular-value facts used below are given in (Milnor 1965, secs. 2–3) <a id="pm-cite-21"></a>[[Milnor1965](#ref-Milnor1965)]. We prove the needed relative version for continuous families of bundle sections here. A section of a vector bundle assigns to each base point a vector in the fiber above it. A zero is *transverse* if the derivative of the section in any local trivialization maps onto that fiber.

<a id="stmt-path-parity"></a>
<div id="path:parity" class="lemma">

**Lemma 12** (Continuation modulo two). *Let $Q$ be a smooth $d$-dimensional manifold without boundary, and let $E\to Q$ be a smooth real vector bundle of rank $d$. Suppose $s_\theta$, $0\leq\theta\leq1$, is a continuous homotopy of sections and $$\{(q,\theta)\in Q\times[0,1]:s_\theta(q)=0\}$$ is compact. If $s_0$ is smooth and has an odd number of zeros, all transverse, then $s_1$ has a zero.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-path-parity"><summary>Proof of stmt-path-parity</summary>

*Proof.* Suppose that $s_1$ has no zero. Choose a relatively compact smooth domain $\Omega\subset Q$ containing the projection of the entire zero set. Such a domain is obtained by taking a regular level of a smooth compactly supported function equal to one near that projection. Fix a smooth fiber metric on $E$. On the compact set $$(\partial\Omega\times[0,1])
 \ \cup\ (\overline\Omega\times\{1\}),$$ the norm of the section has a strictly positive minimum.

Reparametrize the homotopy so that it is identically $s_0$ on a small initial collar. It can then be uniformly approximated on $\overline\Omega\times[0,1]$ by a smooth section that agrees with $s_0$ on a smaller initial collar. To construct this approximation, use finitely many bundle charts, approximate the continuous coefficient functions by smooth functions, and combine them with a smooth partition of unity. A cutoff supported inside the original collar splices this approximation with the smooth section $s_0$. Choose the approximation error smaller than one quarter of the positive boundary minimum. The approximating section therefore has no zeros near the spatial boundary or the final boundary.

We can also make this smooth section transverse to zero while keeping it unchanged near the initial boundary. Here are the finite-dimensional details. In an initial collar its zeros are already transverse, since the derivative in the $Q$ directions is onto at each zero of $s_0$. The remaining possible zeros lie in a compact subset of the interior. Choose finitely many compactly supported local frame sections whose values span every fiber on a neighborhood of this subset, with supports away from the initial boundary and the forbidden boundaries. Adding linear combinations of these sections gives a finite-dimensional family whose derivative in the new parameters is onto wherever transversality is still needed. Restrict the parameters to a small open ball in $\mathbb R^m$, where $m$ is the number of frame sections. Smallness preserves the positive gap on the compact complement of the spanning neighborhood and the initial collar, so no new zeros appear there. The universal zero set in the interior is a smooth manifold of dimension $m+1$. Sard’s theorem applied to its projection onto $\mathbb R^m$ provides arbitrarily small regular parameters. At a zero, write the universal derivative as $Av+Db$, with $v$ a $(q,\theta)$ variation and $b$ a parameter variation. Surjectivity of the projection on the kernel means that $Av=-Db$ is solvable for every $b$. Where perturbations are needed, $D$ is onto, so this is equivalent to $A$ being onto; elsewhere the section is already transverse. Thus regular parameters make the fixed-parameter section transverse. Smallness in the smooth topology preserves the transversality already present on the overlap with the initial collar, and smallness in norm preserves the boundary gap.

The zero set of the resulting section is a compact smooth one-dimensional manifold. Its boundary consists exactly of the zeros of $s_0$ on $\Omega\times\{0\}$. A compact one-dimensional manifold has an even number of boundary points, contradicting the assumed odd count. No orientation of $Q$ or $E$ is required. ◻

</details>

<details id="proof-stmt-path-parity-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-path-parity</summary>

*Proof of Proposition <a href="#path:existence" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="path:existence">11</a>.* Put $\kappa=\|K\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ and $\ell=x_+-x_-$, and use the continuous representative of $u$. Choose a smooth nonnegative function $\chi$ on $\operatorname{Sym}_N$, invariant under orthogonal conjugation, compactly supported, and equal to one whenever $\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa$. For example, take $\chi(B)=\eta(\operatorname{tr}B^2)$ with $\eta$ equal to one on $[0,N\kappa^2]$ and zero outside a larger bounded interval. The field $\widehat M=\chi M$ is bounded and globally Lipschitz.

<a id="path:homotopy-ode"></a>
*The terminal matching problem.* Let $\mathcal L$ denote the vector space of real lower triangular $N\times N$ matrices. For $C\in\mathcal L$ and $0\leq\theta\leq1$, solve $$\label{path:homotopy-ode}
 \varepsilon B'=\theta\widehat M(B)-(Cu)(Cu)^{\mathsf T},
 \qquad B(x_-)=K.$$ The bounded globally Lipschitz field and continuous forcing give a unique solution throughout $I$. The integral equation and Gronwall’s inequality give continuous dependence on $(C,\theta)$. Thus $$\Phi_\theta(C)=B(x_+)+K$$ is a continuous homotopy of maps from $\mathcal L$ to $\operatorname{Sym}_N$.

At a zero of $\Phi_\theta$, integration of <a href="#path:homotopy-ode" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:homotopy-ode">[path:homotopy-ode]</a> and orthonormality of $u$ give <a id="path:coefficient-identity"></a>$$\label{path:coefficient-identity}
 CC^{\mathsf T}
 =2\varepsilon K+\theta\int_I\widehat M(B(x))\,dx.$$ Consequently, <a id="path:homotopy-bound"></a>$$\label{path:homotopy-bound}
 \|C\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
 \leq 2\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}K
       +\ell\sup_B|\operatorname{tr}\widehat M(B)|.$$ For fixed $\varepsilon$, the full zero set in $\mathcal L\times[0,1]$ is therefore compact.

<a id="path:terminal-equivariance"></a>
*Rows cannot vanish at a matching solution.* Let $G$ be the group of diagonal sign matrices. Equation <a href="#path:equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:equivariance">[path:equivariance]</a>, invariance of $\chi$, and uniqueness of the flow show that $$\label{path:terminal-equivariance}
 \Phi_\theta(SC)=S\Phi_\theta(C)S
 \qquad(S\in G).$$ Suppose that row $i$ of $C$ vanishes, and let $S_i$ change only its sign. Then $S_iC=C$, so uniqueness gives $S_iBS_i=B$ throughout the flow. Hence $Be_i=b_i e_i$. By <a href="#path:coordinate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:coordinate">[path:coordinate]</a>, $$\varepsilon b_i'
 =\theta\chi(B(x))\mu(k_i^2-b_i^2),
 \qquad b_i(x_-)=k_i.$$ Regarding $\theta\chi(B(x))/\varepsilon$ as a known continuous coefficient, local Lipschitz uniqueness for this scalar equation gives $b_i\equiv k_i$. Since $k_i>0$, the terminal value cannot be $-k_i$. Thus no zero of $\Phi_\theta$ has a vanishing row.

Let $\mathcal L^\times\subset\mathcal L$ be the open subset on which every row is nonzero. The compact joint zero set lies inside $\mathcal L^\times\times[0,1]$, and hence a positive distance away from every zero-row stratum. We do not require $C$ to be invertible.

*One zero modulo signs persists.* The action of $G$ on $\mathcal L^\times$ is free: changing the sign of a nonzero row cannot fix $C$. Its quotient $$Q=\mathcal L^\times/G$$ is therefore a smooth manifold of dimension $d=N(N+1)/2$. Concretely, each row $i$ belongs to $\mathbb R^i\setminus\{0\}$ and is identified with its negative. Sign conjugation on symmetric matrices defines the rank-$d$ vector bundle $$E=(\mathcal L^\times\times\operatorname{Sym}_N)/G
 \longrightarrow Q,
 \qquad
 (C,Z)\sim(SC,SZS).$$ By <a href="#path:terminal-equivariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:terminal-equivariance">[path:terminal-equivariance]</a>, the maps $\Phi_\theta$ descend to a continuous homotopy of sections of this bundle. Their joint zero set remains compact.

At $\theta=0$, $$\Phi_0(C)=2K-\varepsilon^{-1}CC^{\mathsf T}.$$ The lower triangular solutions of $CC^{\mathsf T}=2\varepsilon K$ are exactly $$C=\operatorname{diag}
   (\pm\sqrt{2\varepsilon k_1},\ldots,
    \pm\sqrt{2\varepsilon k_N}).$$ Indeed, the first row determines $C_{11}$, the other entries of the first column then vanish, and the same argument applies successively to the remaining principal blocks. These solutions form one $G$-orbit. At any one of them, writing $C=\operatorname{diag}(c_i)$, $$D\Phi_0(C)[H]
 =-\varepsilon^{-1}(HC^{\mathsf T}+CH^{\mathsf T})
 \qquad(H\in\mathcal L).$$ Its diagonal entries are $-2c_iH_{ii}/\varepsilon$, and its entries with $i>j$ are $-c_jH_{ij}/\varepsilon$. Since every $c_i\ne0$, this linear map is an isomorphism onto the symmetric matrices. The initial quotient section has exactly one transverse zero. Lemma <a href="#path:parity" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="path:parity">12</a> now gives a zero of $\Phi_1$.

*The endpoints remove the cutoff.* Take a path obtained from this zero. For a fixed real unit vector $e$, put $h(x)=e^{\mathsf T}B(x)e$. Whenever $|h(x)|>\kappa$, <a href="#path:comparison" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:comparison">[path:comparison]</a> implies $$\begin{aligned}
 \varepsilon h'(x)
 &\leq\chi(B(x))
    \mu\bigl(e^{\mathsf T}K^2e-h(x)^2\bigr)
       -|e^{\mathsf T}Cu(x)|^2
 \leq0.
\end{aligned}$$ Here $e^{\mathsf T}K^2e\leq\kappa^2<h(x)^2$; the last inequality uses $\chi\geq0$ and remains true where $\chi=0$. Both endpoint values of $h$ lie in $[-\kappa,\kappa]$. A component of $\{h>\kappa\}$ cannot start from the initial side: on that component $h$ is nonincreasing, whereas its left endpoint has value $\kappa$. Similarly, a component of $\{h<-\kappa\}$ cannot return to the final endpoint: its right endpoint would have value $-\kappa$, again contradicting nonincrease. Thus $|h(x)|\leq\kappa$ throughout $I$.

Since $e$ was arbitrary, this proves <a href="#path:operator-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:operator-bound">[path:operator-bound]</a>. Therefore $\chi(B(x))=1$ everywhere, and the path solves the uncut equation <a href="#path:equation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="path:equation">[path:equation]</a>. Finally, its integrated equation gives, for $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, $$\label{path:uniform-coefficients}
 \|C\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
 \leq 2\operatorname{tr}K
 +\ell\sup_{\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\kappa}
             |\operatorname{tr}M(B)|.$$ This is the asserted bound on $C$. ◻

</details>

### From the matrix path to the action

We now prove Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a>. The matrix comparison controls points where $M(B)=aa^{\mathsf T}$ exactly. The differential equation supplies a penalty for the remaining points, and compactness makes that penalty effective without requiring convergence of the chosen coefficients $C$.

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a>.* Fix $\delta>0$, and use the field $M$ and primitive $J$ from Section <a href="#sec:field" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:field">3</a>. ### From the action inequality to spectral moments

We apply Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> first to ordered Dirichlet eigenfunctions. We then pass to the line using finite-dimensional trial spaces. This last step treats all negative eigenvalues, without assuming in advance that their moment is finite.

Throughout this section, $0<\sigma<1$, $p=1+\sigma$, $q=1+1/\sigma$, and $\gamma=\sigma+1/2$. Write <a id="spectral:constant"></a>$$\label{spectral:constant}
 A_\sigma=\frac{\sigma^\sigma}{(1+\sigma)^{1+\sigma}},
 \qquad
 \ell_\sigma=\frac{A_\sigma}{\mathrm B(1/2,1+\sigma)},$$ where $\mathrm B$ denotes the beta function.

<a id="pm-h-16"></a>
#### Quadratic forms and negative spectrum

For $0\leq W\in L^p(\mathbb R)$, define $$h_{-W}[v]=\int_\mathbb R|v'|^2-\int_\mathbb RW|v|^2,
 \qquad v\in H^1(\mathbb R).$$ We record the facts needed to define its operator and to exhaust its negative eigenvalues. We use the association of a densely defined closed semibounded form with its self-adjoint operator, and the spectral description of the operator and its form domain, in (Teschl 2009, Theorem 2.13, Theorems 3.7–3.8 and (3.50)–(3.51)) <a id="pm-cite-22"></a>[[Teschl2009](#ref-Teschl2009)]. All theorem numbers here refer to the 2009 first edition.

<a id="stmt-spectral-forms"></a>
<div id="spectral:forms" class="lemma">

**Lemma 13**. *Let $p>1$ and $0\leq W\in L^p(\mathbb R)$. The form $h_{-W}$ is closed and bounded below on $H^1(\mathbb R)$. For its associated self-adjoint operator $H_{-W}$, every spectral projection onto $(-\infty,-\alpha]$, with $\alpha>0$, has finite rank. Thus its negative spectrum consists of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, with possible accumulation only at zero. On a bounded interval $I$, the corresponding Dirichlet form on $H^1_0(I)$ is closed and bounded below and has compact resolvent.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-spectral-forms"><summary>Proof of stmt-spectral-forms</summary>

<a id="spectral:form-bound"></a>
*Proof.* Put $p'=p/(p-1)$. The one-dimensional Sobolev inequality and interpolation give $$\|v\|_{2p'}^2
 \leq \|v\|_\infty^{2/p}\|v\|_2^{2-2/p}
 \leq 2^{1/p}\|v'\|_2^{1/p}\|v\|_2^{2-1/p}.$$ Consequently Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities imply that, for every $\eta>0$, there is $b_{\eta,W}<\infty$ such that $$\label{spectral:form-bound}
 \int_\mathbb RW|v|^2
 \leq \eta\|v'\|_2^2+b_{\eta,W}\|v\|_2^2.$$ The KLMN Theorem (Teschl 2009, Theorem 6.24) <a id="pm-cite-23"></a>[[Teschl2009](#ref-Teschl2009)] now gives closure and semiboundedness, with form domain exactly $H^1(\mathbb R)$.

Multiplication by $\sqrt W$ is compact from $H^1(\mathbb R)$ to $L^2(\mathbb R)$. Indeed, choose nonnegative bounded, compactly supported $W_n$ converging to $W$ in $L^p$. Multiplication by $\sqrt{W_n}$ is compact by local Rellich compactness, and $$\| (\sqrt W-\sqrt{W_n})v\|_2^2
 \leq \|W-W_n\|_p\|v\|_{2p'}^2
 \leq C_p\|W-W_n\|_p\|v\|_{H^1}^2.$$ Thus these multiplication operators converge in operator norm.

If the spectral subspace of $H_{-W}$ for $(-\infty,-\alpha]$ were infinite-dimensional, it would contain an orthonormal sequence $v_n$. Semiboundedness places this entire spectral subspace in the form domain. The lower bound obtained from <a href="#spectral:form-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="spectral:form-bound">[spectral:form-bound]</a> with $\eta=1/2$, together with $h_{-W}[v_n]\leq-\alpha$, bounds this sequence in $H^1$. After passing to a subsequence, it converges weakly to zero in $H^1$, because it is orthonormal in $L^2$. Compactness then gives $\sqrt Wv_n\to0$ in $L^2$, contradicting $$-\alpha\geq h_{-W}[v_n]\geq-\|\sqrt Wv_n\|_2^2.$$ This proves the negative-spectrum assertion. On a bounded interval, the same form estimate follows by zero extension. The form norm is equivalent to the $H^1_0$ norm, whose embedding into $L^2(I)$ is compact; hence the Dirichlet operator has compact resolvent. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-17"></a>
#### The Dirichlet estimate

The order of the eigenfunctions is what makes the triangular coefficient restriction in Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> useful.

<a id="stmt-spectral-dirichlet"></a>
<div id="spectral:dirichlet" class="proposition">

<a id="spectral:dirichlet-bound"></a>
**Proposition 14**. *Let $I$ be a bounded interval and $0\leq W\in L^p(I)$. If $-k_1^2,\ldots,-k_N^2$ are the first $N$ negative Dirichlet eigenvalues of $-d^2/dx^2-W$, ordered so that $k_1\geq\cdots\geq k_N>0$, then $$\label{spectral:dirichlet-bound}
 \sum_{j=1}^N k_j^{2\gamma}
 \leq \ell_\sigma\int_I W^p.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-spectral-dirichlet"><summary>Proof of stmt-spectral-dirichlet</summary>

*Proof.* Choose real orthonormal eigenfunctions $u_1,\ldots,u_N$; these exist because the Dirichlet form is real. Put $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_N)^{\mathsf T}$ and $K=\operatorname{diag}(k_1,\ldots,k_N)$. For every real lower triangular matrix $C$, write $a=Cu$. Its $i$th component lies in the span of $u_1,\ldots,u_i$, and the eigenfunction equations in form sense give $$\int_I \bigl(|a_i'|^2-W|a_i|^2+k_i^2|a_i|^2\bigr)
 =\sum_{j\leq i} C_{ij}^2(k_i^2-k_j^2)\leq0.$$ Summing over $i$ and putting $m=|a|^2$, we obtain $$\mathcal E_K(a)\leq\int_I(Wm-m^q)
 \leq A_\sigma\int_I W^{1+\sigma}.$$ The last inequality follows by maximizing $Wm-m^q$ over $m\geq0$; the maximizer is $(W/q)^\sigma$.

Theorem <a href="#thm:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:action">2</a> therefore yields $$\sum_{j=1}^N\int_{-k_j}^{k_j}(k_j^2-s^2)^\sigma\,ds
 \leq A_\sigma\int_I W^{1+\sigma}.$$ Substituting $s=k_jt$ in each integral gives $\mathrm B(1/2,1+\sigma)k_j^{2\sigma+1}$, proving <a href="#spectral:dirichlet-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="spectral:dirichlet-bound">[spectral:dirichlet-bound]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="spectral:constant-identification"></a>
The beta–gamma identity identifies the constant without any limiting argument in $\sigma$: $$\begin{aligned}
\label{spectral:constant-identification}
 \ell_\sigma
 &=\left(\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}\right)^\sigma
   \frac{\Gamma(\sigma+3/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\sigma+2)}
 \notag\\
 &=2\left(\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}\right)^\sigma
   L_{\gamma,1}^{\mathrm{cl}}.
\end{aligned}$$

<a id="pm-h-18"></a>
#### Exhaustion of the line

Let $0\leq W\in L^p(\mathbb R)$. If there are no negative eigenvalues, the trace inequality is immediate. Otherwise fix any finite number $N$ of them, counted with multiplicity and ordered increasingly: $$\lambda_1\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_N<0.$$ Let $\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N\in H^1(\mathbb R)$ be corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions. Choose smooth cutoffs $\chi_R$ equal to one on $[-R,R]$, supported in $I_R=(-2R,2R)$, and satisfying $0\leq\chi_R\leq1$ and $\|\chi_R'\|_\infty\leq c/R$. Then $$v_{j,R}=\chi_R\phi_j\longrightarrow\phi_j
 \quad\hbox{in }H^1(\mathbb R).$$ Hölder’s inequality and the embedding $H^1\subset L^{2p'}$ show that the potential form is continuous under this convergence.

<a id="spectral:line-bound"></a>
For clarity, let $G_R$ and $Q_R$ be the Gram and form matrices of the $N$ functions $v_{j,R}$. Then $$G_R\longrightarrow I_N,
 \qquad
 Q_R\longrightarrow\Lambda
 :=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N)$$ in matrix norm. In particular the functions are linearly independent for large $R$. If $g_R=\|G_R-I_N\|_{\mathrm{op}}<1$ and $e_R=\|Q_R-\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{op}}$, their Rayleigh quotients differ uniformly from the limiting coefficient-space quotients by at most $$\eta_R=\frac{e_R+\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{op}}g_R}{1-g_R}
 \longrightarrow0.$$ Applying the form-domain min–max principle (Teschl 2009, Theorem 4.10 and the following form-domain extension) <a id="pm-cite-24"></a>[[Teschl2009](#ref-Teschl2009)] to the span of $v_{1,R},\ldots,v_{j,R}$ gives, for every $j\leq N$, $$\lambda_j(I_R)\leq\lambda_j+\eta_R,$$ where $\lambda_j(I_R)$ is the $j$th Dirichlet eigenvalue on $I_R$. All these levels are negative for sufficiently large $R$. Proposition <a href="#spectral:dirichlet" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="spectral:dirichlet">14</a> now implies $$\sum_{j=1}^N(-\lambda_j-\eta_R)^\gamma
 \leq \sum_{j=1}^N|\lambda_j(I_R)|^\gamma
 \leq \ell_\sigma\int_{I_R}W^p
 \leq \ell_\sigma\int_\mathbb RW^p.$$ Letting $R\to\infty$ proves the estimate for every finite partial sum. Taking their supremum gives $$\label{spectral:line-bound}
 \operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma
 \leq \ell_\sigma\int_\mathbb RW^p.$$ Thus the full moment is finite even when there are infinitely many negative eigenvalues. No approximation of $W$ by smoother potentials is needed.

<a id="pm-h-19"></a>
#### Signed potentials

To include unbounded positive parts, we specify the form domain in the signed-potential statement.

<a id="stmt-spectral-signed"></a>
<div id="spectral:signed" class="corollary">

**Corollary 15**. *Let $V$ be real measurable with $V_-\in L^p(\mathbb R)$, and suppose $$\mathcal D_V
 =\left\{v\in H^1(\mathbb R):\int_\mathbb RV_+|v|^2<\infty\right\}$$ is dense in $L^2(\mathbb R)$. This holds in particular if $V_+\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb R)$. The form $$h_V[v]=\int_\mathbb R|v'|^2+\int_\mathbb RV_+|v|^2-\int_\mathbb RV_-|v|^2,
 \qquad v\in\mathcal D_V,$$ is closed and bounded below. Its associated operator $H_V$ has discrete negative spectrum and satisfies $$\operatorname{Tr}(H_V)_-^\gamma\leq\ell_\sigma\int_\mathbb RV_-^p.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-spectral-signed"><summary>Proof of stmt-spectral-signed</summary>

*Proof.* The positive form $\int|v'|^2+\int V_+|v|^2$ is closed on $\mathcal D_V$, by completeness of $H^1$ and of the weighted $L^2$ space. Estimate <a href="#spectral:form-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="spectral:form-bound">[spectral:form-bound]</a>, with $W=V_-$, proves closure and semiboundedness after subtraction of the negative part. If $V_+\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$, then $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)\subset\mathcal D_V$, proving the asserted density. The compactness argument in Lemma <a href="#spectral:forms" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="spectral:forms">13</a> still applies. On a negative spectral subspace bounded away from zero, the same relative form bound controls the $H^1$ norm of unit vectors, since the positive potential term can be discarded. Compactness of multiplication by $\sqrt{V_-}$ then excludes an infinite orthonormal sequence in that subspace: the integral against $V_-$ would tend to zero along a subsequence, whereas its negative form energy stays bounded away from zero. Thus every such spectral projection has finite rank.

For $v\in\mathcal D_V\subset H^1(\mathbb R)$, one has $h_V[v]\geq h_{-V_-}[v]$. The min–max principle, with this inclusion of form domains, bounds each negative eigenvalue of $H_V$ below by the corresponding negative eigenvalue of $H_{-V_-}$; in particular, if $H_V$ has $j$ negative eigenvalues, so does $H_{-V_-}$. Summation and <a href="#spectral:line-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="spectral:line-bound">[spectral:line-bound]</a> prove the result. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-20"></a>
#### Sharpness

Put $r=1/\sigma>1$ and consider $$W(x)=(r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx),
 \qquad
 \psi(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^{1/r}(rx).$$ Direct differentiation gives $$\frac{\psi'}{\psi}=-\tanh(rx),
 \qquad
 \frac{\psi''}{\psi}=1-(r+1)\mathop{\mathrm{sech}}^2(rx).$$ The function $\psi$ and its first two derivatives decay exponentially. Thus $\psi\in H^2(\mathbb R)$, the operator domain for this bounded $W$, and $H_{-W}\psi=-\psi$. Substitution $t=rx$, followed by $z=\tanh t$, gives $$\int_\mathbb RW^p
 =\frac{(r+1)^p}{r}\,\mathrm B(1/2,p),
 \qquad
 \left(\int_\mathbb RW^p\right)^{-1}=\ell_\sigma.$$ The eigenvalue $-1$ gives $\operatorname{Tr}(H_{-W})_-^\gamma\geq1$; the upper bound <a href="#spectral:line-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="spectral:line-bound">[spectral:line-bound]</a> gives the reverse inequality. This completes the proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>. Hence this potential attains equality, and the constant in <a href="#spectral:constant-identification" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="spectral:constant-identification">[spectral:constant-identification]</a> is optimal. This establishes $L_{\gamma,1}=L_{\gamma,1}^{(1)}$ throughout $1/2<\gamma<3/2$.
