## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We construct a smooth nonquadratic entire graph in dimension ten that solves the classical affine maximal equation and has positive-definite Hessian everywhere. This gives a smooth counterexample to the entire-graph affine Bernstein assertion in dimension ten. Hessian positivity is pointwise; no global uniform lower bound or completeness of the Berwald–Blaschke metric is asserted.

- **C1** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): There exists a nonquadratic function $u\in C^\infty(\mathbb R^{10})$ with $D^2u$ positive definite at every point such that $$\sum_{i,j=1}^{10}U^{ij}\partial_{ij} (\det D^2u)^{-11/12}=0 \quad\hbox{on }\mathbb R^{10}, \qquad U=\det(D^2u)(D^2u)^{-1}.$$
  - Explanation: A smooth nonquadratic entire classical affine maximal graph exists in dimension ten with pointwise positive definite Hessian; uniform Hessian lower bounds and affine metric completeness are not asserted.
- **C2** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-prop-reduction)): Let $F,P$ be positive smooth functions on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^m$, and suppose $H=D_x^2F$ is positive definite. If $$\label{eq:reduced} \det H=FP^{-\gamma},\qquad \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(H^{-1}D_x^2P)+kP/F=0,$$ then $$u(x,t)=\sqrt{F(x)^2+t^2}$$ has positive definite Hessian on $\Omega\times\mathbb R$ and satisfies <a href="#eq:affine" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:affine">[eq:affine]</a> in dimension $n=m+1$.
  - Explanation: Positive profiles satisfying the reduced equations yield a positive definite Hessian and solve the classical affine maximal equation through the square-root ansatz.
- **C3** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-local)): There is $\varepsilon>0$ and a solution $F,P$ of <a href="#eq:radial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:radial">[eq:radial]</a> on $0<r<\varepsilon$ such that $F(\|x\|)$ and $P(\|x\|)$ extend smoothly to $\|x\|<\varepsilon$, with $$F(0)=P(0)=1,\qquad D_x^2F(0)=I,\qquad D_x^2P(0)=-\frac{11}{9}I.$$ Moreover $F,P,y,y'>0$ and $P'<0$ for $0<r<\varepsilon$.
  - Explanation: An integral contraction in the square-radius variable constructs smooth radial profiles through the origin with the stated Hessians and local signs.
- **C4** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-lem-autonomous)): On the interval of continuation, $A,B>0$, and the functions $A,B,D$ satisfy $$\label{eq:autonomous} \begin{aligned} \dot A&=A(1+B-A),\\ \dot B&=B\bigl(9+A+(12/11)BD-9B\bigr),\\ \dot D&=(11-D)A-8D+BD(1-D/11). \end{aligned}$$ Moreover $(A,B,D)\to(0,1,0)$ as $r\downarrow0$, and all three coordinates are positive for sufficiently small $r>0$.
  - Explanation: Logarithmic derivatives satisfy an autonomous polynomial system; the limiting local data enter the positive part of the invariant box.
- **C5** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-box)): The box $$\label{eq:box} \mathcal B=[0,9/2]\times[0,7/2]\times[0,33/7]$$ is forward invariant for <a href="#eq:autonomous" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:autonomous">[eq:autonomous]</a>. The solution arising from Lemma <a href="#lem:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:local">3</a> lies in $\mathcal B$ throughout its interval of continuation.
  - Explanation: A stationary upper corner and nonnegative cross derivatives control the upper faces; the lower faces point inward, proving forward invariance.
- **C6** (construction, [source](#stmt-prop-global)): The solution in Lemma <a href="#lem:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:local">3</a> extends to every $r>0$, with $F,P,y,y'>0$. Its radial extensions to $\mathbb R^9$ are smooth, and $D_x^2F$ is positive definite everywhere.
  - Explanation: The invariant box bounds logarithmic derivatives on every finite radius interval and prevents reaching the boundary of the regular ODE domain.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Classical affine maximal equation, cofactor matrix and determinant power** ([source](#eq:affine)): For a smooth function $u:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ with positive definite Hessian, write $$\label{eq:affine} \sum_{i,j=1}^n U^{ij}w_{ij}=0,\qquad U^{ij}=\det(D^2u)(D^2u)^{-1}_{ij},\qquad w=(\det D^2u)^{-(n+1)/(n+2)}.$$
- **Pointwise Hessian positivity** ([source](#eq:affine)): Throughout this paper, positivity of the Hessian means pointwise positive definiteness, not a global lower bound by a fixed positive matrix.
- **Radial derivative y** ([source](#eq:radial)): Write primes for radial derivatives and $y=F'$.
- **Regular radial ODE** ([source](#eq:regular)): Explicitly, with $Z=P'$ and $$G(r,F,P,y)=\frac{r^8FP^{-12/11}}{y^8},$$ it reads $$\label{eq:regular} F'=y,\qquad P'=Z,\qquad y'=G,\qquad Z'=-8\frac Gy Z-\frac{11r^8P^{-1/11}}{y^8}.$$
- **Logarithmic derivative ratios** ([source](#sec:global)): Define $$\label{eq:ratios} A=\frac{ry}{F},\qquad B=\frac{ry'}y, \qquad C=-\frac{rP'}P,$$ and let a dot mean differentiation with respect to $\log r$.
- **Normalized ratio D** ([source](#eq:ratios)): Since $B>0$, we may also set $D=C/B$.

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I_BOX**: The specified box is forward invariant for the autonomous logarithmic-derivative system.
- **I_HESSIAN**: The constructed entire graph has pointwise positive-definite Hessian and is nonquadratic.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **AU_COMPLETENESS**: Treat the example as a benchmark requiring a globally uniform Hessian lower bound. -> Pointwise positive definiteness does not establish that stronger input contract.
- **AU_NUMERIC**: Declare global ODE existence or affine maximality from a finite grid or numerical trajectory. -> Finite samples do not establish smooth existence through the origin or continuation to every radius.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `constructive`. Finite constructive algebraic subcertificate of Lemma 5: exact stationary corner, nonnegative cross derivatives and inward face signs for the stated rational polynomial vector field. ### Introduction

<a id="eq:affine"></a>
For a smooth function $u:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ with positive definite Hessian, write $$\label{eq:affine}
 \sum_{i,j=1}^n U^{ij}w_{ij}=0,\qquad
 U^{ij}=\det(D^2u)(D^2u)^{-1}_{ij},\qquad
 w=(\det D^2u)^{-(n+1)/(n+2)}.$$ This is the classical affine maximal equation, the Euler–Lagrange equation of the affine area functional $\int (\det D^2u)^{1/(n+2)}$ under compactly supported variations; see (Trudinger and Wang 2000) <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[TrudingerWang2000](#ref-TrudingerWang2000)]. The entire-graph form of the affine Bernstein problem asks whether every such solution must be a quadratic polynomial. Throughout this paper, positivity of the Hessian means pointwise positive definiteness, not a global lower bound by a fixed positive matrix.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1**. *There exists a nonquadratic function $u\in C^\infty(\mathbb R^{10})$ with $D^2u$ positive definite at every point such that $$\sum_{i,j=1}^{10}U^{ij}\partial_{ij}
       (\det D^2u)^{-11/12}=0
 \quad\hbox{on }\mathbb R^{10},
 \qquad U=\det(D^2u)(D^2u)^{-1}.$$*

</div>

Thus the smooth entire-graph assertion has a negative answer in dimension ten. No growth assumption or completeness assumption for the Berwald–Blaschke metric is imposed here.

<a id="eq:singular"></a>
The classical problem has several formulations involving different notions of completeness. Chern formulated the two-dimensional entire-graph problem (Chern 1979) <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[Chern1979](#ref-Chern1979)]. Calabi proved rigidity in dimension two assuming both Euclidean completeness and completeness of the affine metric (Calabi 1982) <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[Calabi1982](#ref-Calabi1982)]. Trudinger and Wang later proved that affine completeness implies Euclidean completeness for locally uniformly convex hypersurfaces of dimension at least two (Trudinger and Wang 2002) <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[TrudingerWang2002](#ref-TrudingerWang2002)]. For the entire-graph problem, Trudinger and Wang proved that smooth locally uniformly convex entire affine maximal graphs over $\mathbb R^2$ are elliptic paraboloids (Trudinger and Wang 2000) <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[TrudingerWang2000](#ref-TrudingerWang2000)]. In the same paper, they exhibited the singular dimension-ten example $$\label{eq:singular}
 u_0(x,t)=\sqrt{|x|^9+t^2},\qquad (x,t)\in\mathbb R^9\times\mathbb R,$$ see (Trudinger and Wang 2000, sec. 7) <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[TrudingerWang2000](#ref-TrudingerWang2000)]. This example is not smooth at the origin and does not have positive definite Hessian everywhere. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> supplies a smooth example satisfying precisely these stronger pointwise requirements. The global section-based uniform convexity hypothesis in (Trudinger and Wang 2000, Corollary 4.3) <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[TrudingerWang2000](#ref-TrudingerWang2000)] is stronger than the Hessian positivity assumed here.

Related equations with $w=(\det D^2u)^{-\theta}$ admit nonquadratic Euclidean-complete examples for other ranges of $\theta$ (Du 2024; Sun, Xing, and Xu 2026) <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[Du2024](#ref-Du2024), [SunXingXu2026](#ref-SunXingXu2026)]. The latter range extends to $\theta=n/(n+1)$, but does not include the classical value $\theta=11/12$ when $n=10$.

Our construction keeps the relation $u^2-t^2=F(x)^2$ suggested by <a href="#eq:singular" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:singular">[eq:singular]</a>, but replaces $F(x)=|x|^{9/2}$ by a smooth strictly convex radial function with $F(0)=1$. Section <a href="#sec:reduction" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:reduction">2</a> gives a dimension-independent reduction of this ansatz to two second-order equations for positive functions $F$ and $P$. Section <a href="#sec:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:local">3</a> constructs their radial solution smoothly through the origin. The main obstacle is then global existence: a priori the auxiliary function $P$ could vanish at a finite radius. Section <a href="#sec:global" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:global">4</a> rules this out using a bounded invariant region for logarithmic derivatives, and completes the proof. The decisive change of derivative variables makes each component of the resulting vector field nondecreasing in the other two variables on a suitable box. A single stationary corner then controls all of its upper faces.

<a id="sec:reduction"></a>
### Reduction to a radial system

We first isolate the calculation that turns the fourth-order equation into a system for two functions of one fewer variable. For this section let $m\geq1$, so that the graph has $m+1$ independent variables $(x,t)\in\mathbb R^m\times\mathbb R$. Set $$k=m+2,\qquad \gamma=\frac{m+3}{m+2}.$$ We introduce $P$ so that, for the ansatz below, the determinant power $w$ separates into $P(x)$ times a fixed function of $t/F(x)$.

<a id="stmt-prop-reduction"></a>
<div id="prop:reduction" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:reduced"></a>
**Proposition 2**. *Let $F,P$ be positive smooth functions on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^m$, and suppose $H=D_x^2F$ is positive definite. If $$\label{eq:reduced}
 \det H=FP^{-\gamma},\qquad
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(H^{-1}D_x^2P)+kP/F=0,$$ then $$u(x,t)=\sqrt{F(x)^2+t^2}$$ has positive definite Hessian on $\Omega\times\mathbb R$ and satisfies <a href="#eq:affine" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:affine">[eq:affine]</a> in dimension $n=m+1$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-reduction"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-reduction</summary>

<a id="eq:hessian"></a>
<a id="eq:det"></a>
*Proof.* Direct differentiation, interpreted as an identity of quadratic forms, gives $$\label{eq:hessian}
 D^2u=\frac Fu H+\frac{F^2}{u^3}
          \left(dt-\frac tF\,dF\right)^2.$$ The first term is positive on the $x$ directions, and the second is positive on the remaining direction. Thus $D^2u>0$. Put $\zeta=t/F$ and use the pointwise basis $$X_i=e_i+\zeta F_i e_t\quad(1\leq i\leq m),\qquad e_t.$$ Its change-of-basis matrix has determinant one. In this basis, <a href="#eq:hessian" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:hessian">[eq:hessian]</a> has diagonal blocks $(F/u)H$ and $F^2/u^3$, so $$\label{eq:det}
 \det D^2u=F^{m+2}u^{-(m+3)}\det H.$$ The first equation in <a href="#eq:reduced" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reduced">[eq:reduced]</a> is equivalent to $P=(F/\det H)^{(m+2)/(m+3)}$. Therefore <a id="eq:w"></a>$$\label{eq:w}
 w=(\det D^2u)^{-(m+2)/(m+3)}=P h(\zeta),
 \qquad h(\zeta)=(1+\zeta^2)^{k/2}.$$

<a id="eq:trace"></a>
We evaluate the ordinary Hessian of $w$ in the same pointwise basis. Since $d\zeta(X_i)=0$, twice differentiating $t=F\zeta$ gives $$D^2\zeta(X_i,X_j)=-\frac\zeta F F_{ij}.$$ Consequently $$D^2w(X_i,X_j)=hP_{ij}-\frac PF\zeta h'F_{ij},
 \qquad w_{tt}=\frac P{F^2}h''.$$ Taking the trace against the inverse blocks of $D^2u$ yields $$\label{eq:trace}
 \frac Fu\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((D^2u)^{-1}D^2w\bigr)
 =h\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(H^{-1}D_x^2P)
  +\frac PF\bigl((1+\zeta^2)h''-m\zeta h'\bigr).$$ Differentiation of $h$ gives $$(1+\zeta^2)h''-m\zeta h'
 =k(1+\zeta^2)^{k/2-1}
       \bigl(1+(k-1-m)\zeta^2\bigr)=kh,$$ because $k=m+2$. The second equation in <a href="#eq:reduced" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reduced">[eq:reduced]</a> makes <a href="#eq:trace" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:trace">[eq:trace]</a> zero. Multiplication by the positive determinant in <a href="#eq:det" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:det">[eq:det]</a> gives <a href="#eq:affine" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:affine">[eq:affine]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="eq:radial"></a>
We now take $F$ and $P$ radial, and use the same letters for their profiles in $r=|x|$. Write primes for radial derivatives and $y=F'$. For $r>0$ the eigenvalues of $D_x^2F$ are $y'$ in the radial direction and $y/r$ in the $m-1$ tangential directions. Thus, whenever $y,y'>0$, $$\det D_x^2F=y'\left(\frac yr\right)^{m-1},\qquad
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\bigl((D_x^2F)^{-1}D_x^2P\bigr)
 =\frac{P''}{y'}+(m-1)\frac{P'}y.$$ Substituting into <a href="#eq:reduced" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reduced">[eq:reduced]</a>, and multiplying the second equation by $y'y^{m-1}$, gives the equivalent system $$\label{eq:radial}
 y^{m-1}y'=r^{m-1}FP^{-\gamma},\qquad
 (y^{m-1}P')'=-k r^{m-1}P^{1-\gamma},\qquad F'=y.$$ The divergence form of the second equation will allow us to start the solution at the singular point $r=0$.

<a id="sec:local"></a>
### A smooth radial solution at the origin

<a id="eq:constants"></a>
From now on fix $$\label{eq:constants}
 m=9,\qquad k=11,\qquad \gamma=12/11.$$ We construct a local solution with $F(0)=P(0)=1$. The radial equations contain divisions by $y=F'$, which vanishes at the origin. An integral formulation in $s=r^2/2$ avoids this singularity and makes smoothness in $x$ explicit.

<a id="stmt-lem-local"></a>
<div id="lem:local" class="lemma">

**Lemma 3**. *There is $\varepsilon>0$ and a solution $F,P$ of <a href="#eq:radial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:radial">[eq:radial]</a> on $0<r<\varepsilon$ such that $F(|x|)$ and $P(|x|)$ extend smoothly to $|x|<\varepsilon$, with $$F(0)=P(0)=1,\qquad D_x^2F(0)=I,\qquad
 D_x^2P(0)=-\frac{11}{9}I.$$ Moreover $F,P,y,y'>0$ and $P'<0$ for $0<r<\varepsilon$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-local"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-local</summary>

<a id="eq:q"></a>
<a id="eq:j"></a>
<a id="eq:fixed-point"></a>
*Proof.* Seek $F(r)=f(s)$ and $P(r)=p(s)$, where $s=r^2/2$. The integrated equations suggest the normalized derivative $q=y/r$ and flux $j=-y^{m-1}P'/(kr^m)$ for $r>0$. We will construct their regular extensions as functions of $s$ using the following averages. For continuous $f,p$ on $[-\delta,\delta]$ taking values in $[1/2,3/2]$, define $$\begin{aligned}
 q(s)&=\left(m\int_0^1 v^{m-1}
                  f(v^2s)p(v^2s)^{-\gamma}\,dv\right)^{1/m},
                  \label{eq:q}\\
 j(s)&=\int_0^1 v^{m-1}p(v^2s)^{1-\gamma}\,dv.
                  \label{eq:j}
\end{aligned}$$ On the closed subset $$\mathcal K=\{(f,p)\in C([-\delta,\delta])^2:
          \|f-1\|_\infty,\|p-1\|_\infty\leq1/2\},$$ consider $$\label{eq:fixed-point}
 \mathcal T(f,p)(s)=
 \left(1+\int_0^s q(a)\,da,
       \ 1-k\int_0^s j(a)q(a)^{1-m}\,da\right).$$ The functions $q,j$ have uniform positive upper and lower bounds. The maps $(f,p)\mapsto q$ and $(f,p)\mapsto jq^{1-m}$ are Lipschitz in the product supremum norm, with constants independent of $\delta$: all scalar powers are evaluated on fixed positive compact intervals. Hence there are constants $M,L$ independent of $\delta$ such that $$\|\mathcal T(f,p)-(1,1)\|_\infty\leq M\delta,
 \qquad
 \|\mathcal T(f,p)-\mathcal T(\widetilde f,\widetilde p)\|_\infty
 \leq L\delta\|(f,p)-(\widetilde f,\widetilde p)\|_\infty.$$ Choose $M\delta\leq1/2$ and $L\delta<1$. The contraction mapping theorem gives a fixed point in $\mathcal K$.

The fixed point is smooth on $(-\delta,\delta)$. Indeed, continuity of $q,j$ first gives $f,p\in C^1$. If $f,p$ are $C^\ell$, differentiation under the integrals in <a href="#eq:q" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:q">[eq:q]</a>–<a href="#eq:j" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:j">[eq:j]</a> shows that $q,j$ are $C^\ell$, and <a href="#eq:fixed-point" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:fixed-point">[eq:fixed-point]</a> gives one further derivative. This proves the assertion by induction. The definition on both sides of $s=0$ ensures that $f(|x|^2/2)$ and $p(|x|^2/2)$ are smooth in $x$ at the origin.

For $r>0$, the fixed point equations give $y=rq(r^2/2)>0$. Changing variables $\rho=rv$ in <a href="#eq:q" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:q">[eq:q]</a>–<a href="#eq:j" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:j">[eq:j]</a> then yields <a id="eq:integral"></a>$$\label{eq:integral}
 y^m=m\int_0^r \rho^{m-1}F(\rho)P(\rho)^{-\gamma}\,d\rho,
 \qquad
 y^{m-1}P'=-k\int_0^r\rho^{m-1}P(\rho)^{1-\gamma}\,d\rho.$$ Differentiation proves <a href="#eq:radial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:radial">[eq:radial]</a>; the first equation implies $y'>0$, and the second integral gives $P'<0$. Finally $q(0)=1$ and $j(0)=1/m$, so $$f_s(0)=1,\qquad p_s(0)=-k/m=-11/9.$$ These are exactly the claimed Hessians of the radial extensions. Shrinking $\varepsilon$ if necessary completes the proof. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:global"></a>
### An invariant box and global continuation

The local solution now has every required property. It remains to show that it can be continued to arbitrarily large radii without losing positivity. We do this by controlling logarithmic derivatives, rather than by estimating $F$ and $P$ separately.

<a id="eq:regular"></a>
Take the maximal outward continuation of the local solution on which $F,P,y>0$. This is a regular smooth ODE for $r>0$. Explicitly, with $Z=P'$ and $$G(r,F,P,y)=\frac{r^8FP^{-12/11}}{y^8},$$ it reads $$\label{eq:regular}
 F'=y,\qquad P'=Z,\qquad y'=G,\qquad
 Z'=-8\frac Gy Z-\frac{11r^8P^{-1/11}}{y^8}.$$ In particular $y'>0$ throughout this continuation. Define <a id="eq:ratios"></a>$$\label{eq:ratios}
 A=\frac{ry}{F},\qquad B=\frac{ry'}y,
 \qquad C=-\frac{rP'}P,$$ and let a dot mean differentiation with respect to $\log r$. Since $B>0$, we may also set $D=C/B$. This normalization gives a vector field with nonnegative cross derivatives on the box in Lemma <a href="#lem:box" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:box">5</a>, allowing its upper faces to be controlled by one corner.

<a id="stmt-lem-autonomous"></a>
<div id="lem:autonomous" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:autonomous"></a>
**Lemma 4**. *On the interval of continuation, $A,B>0$, and the functions $A,B,D$ satisfy $$\label{eq:autonomous}
 \begin{aligned}
  \dot A&=A(1+B-A),\\
  \dot B&=B\bigl(9+A+(12/11)BD-9B\bigr),\\
  \dot D&=(11-D)A-8D+BD(1-D/11).
 \end{aligned}$$ Moreover $(A,B,D)\to(0,1,0)$ as $r\downarrow0$, and all three coordinates are positive for sufficiently small $r>0$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-autonomous"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-autonomous</summary>

*Proof.* Positivity of $F,y,y'$ gives $A,B>0$. Using <a href="#eq:radial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:radial">[eq:radial]</a>, one may write $$B=\frac{r^mFP^{-\gamma}}{y^m}.$$ Logarithmic differentiation of $A$ and $B$, followed by the second equation in <a href="#eq:radial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:radial">[eq:radial]</a>, gives $$\begin{aligned}
 \dot A&=A(1+B-A),\\
 \dot B&=B(m+A+\gamma C-mB),\\
 \dot C&=C(1+C-(m-1)B)+kAB.
\end{aligned}$$ For the last identity, use $\dot C=C+C^2-r^2P''/P$ and $AB=r^{m+1}P^{-\gamma}/y^{m-1}$. Thus $$\dot D=(k-D)A-(m-1)D+BD\bigl(1+(1-\gamma)D\bigr).$$ Substituting <a href="#eq:constants" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:constants">[eq:constants]</a> proves <a href="#eq:autonomous" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:autonomous">[eq:autonomous]</a>.

Lemma <a href="#lem:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:local">3</a> gives $F=1+O(r^2)$, $y=r+O(r^3)$, $y'=1+O(r^2)$, and $P'=-(11/9)r+O(r^3)$. Hence $A\to0$, $B\to1$, and $D\to0$. The positivity of $A,B$ and the local inequality $P'<0$ also give $D>0$ for small positive $r$. ◻

</details>

The singular profile <a href="#eq:singular" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:singular">[eq:singular]</a> suggests the bounds for these variables. Its radial factor $F=r^{9/2}$ has $A=9/2$ and $B=7/2$; the associated function $P=(F/\det D_x^2F)^{11/12}$ is a positive constant times $r^{-33/2}$, giving $C=33/2$ and $D=33/7$. These constants determine an invariant box for the smooth solution.

<a id="stmt-lem-box"></a>
<div id="lem:box" class="lemma">

**Lemma 5**. *The box <a id="eq:box"></a>$$\label{eq:box}
 \mathcal B=[0,9/2]\times[0,7/2]\times[0,33/7]$$ is forward invariant for <a href="#eq:autonomous" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:autonomous">[eq:autonomous]</a>. The solution arising from Lemma <a href="#lem:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:local">3</a> lies in $\mathcal B$ throughout its interval of continuation.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-box"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-box</summary>

*Proof.* Write $V=(V_1,V_2,V_3)$ for the vector field in <a href="#eq:autonomous" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:autonomous">[eq:autonomous]</a>. The upper corner $a_*=(9/2,7/2,33/7)$ satisfies $V(a_*)=0$ by direct substitution. Each $V_i$ is nondecreasing in the other two coordinates on $\mathcal B$. The nonzero cross derivatives are $$\partial_BV_1=A,\quad \partial_AV_2=B,\quad
 \partial_DV_2=(12/11)B^2,\quad
 \partial_AV_3=11-D,\quad
 \partial_BV_3=D(1-D/11),$$ all nonnegative because $0\leq D\leq33/7<11$. On each upper face, comparison with $a_*$ therefore gives the corresponding $V_i\leq0$. On the lower faces $A=0$, $B=0$, and $D=0$, the respective components are $0$, $0$, and $11A$, hence nonnegative.

These weak boundary inequalities suffice for invariance. Let $\Pi z$ be the coordinatewise projection of $z\in\mathbb R^3$ onto $\mathcal B$. The face inequalities imply $$(z-\Pi z)\cdot V(\Pi z)\leq0.$$ On any bounded neighborhood of the box, $V$ has a Lipschitz constant $L$. Along a trajectory $z$ in that neighborhood, the squared distance $d^2=|z-\Pi z|^2$ consequently satisfies $$\frac{d}{d\log r}d^2
 =2(z-\Pi z)\cdot V(z)\leq2L d^2.$$ The squared distance to a closed box is continuously differentiable, so this identity also holds when the nearest face changes. Gronwall’s inequality shows that a trajectory starting in the box cannot leave it.

Finally, Lemma <a href="#lem:autonomous" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:autonomous">4</a> gives strictly positive coordinates tending to $(0,1,0)$ as $r\downarrow0$. They therefore lie below all three upper bounds for sufficiently small $r>0$. Forward invariance proves the last assertion. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-prop-global"></a>
<div id="prop:global" class="proposition">

**Proposition 6**. *The solution in Lemma <a href="#lem:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:local">3</a> extends to every $r>0$, with $F,P,y,y'>0$. Its radial extensions to $\mathbb R^9$ are smooth, and $D_x^2F$ is positive definite everywhere.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-global"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-global</summary>

*Proof.* Suppose its maximal outer endpoint were $R<\infty$, and fix $r_0\in(0,R)$. Lemma <a href="#lem:box" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:box">5</a> gives $$0\leq A\leq9/2,\qquad 0\leq B\leq7/2,
 \qquad 0\leq C=BD\leq33/2.$$ Since the derivatives of $\log F,\log y,\log P$ with respect to $\log r$ are $A,B,-C$, respectively, integration gives, for $r_0\leq r<R$, $$\begin{aligned}
 F(r_0)&\leq F(r)\leq F(r_0)(R/r_0)^{9/2},\\
 y(r_0)&\leq y(r)\leq y(r_0)(R/r_0)^{7/2},\\
 P(r_0)(r_0/R)^{33/2}&\leq P(r)\leq P(r_0).
\end{aligned}$$ Also $|P'(r)|=C(r)P(r)/r\leq(33/2)P(r_0)/r_0$. Thus $(r,F,P,y,Z)$ remains in a compact subset of the domain $r,F,P,y>0$ of the smooth system <a href="#eq:regular" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:regular">[eq:regular]</a>. Its right-hand side is bounded there, so the state has a limit at $R$ in that domain. Local existence and uniqueness at this limit extend the solution past $R$, a contradiction.

Smoothness for $r>0$ follows from <a href="#eq:regular" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:regular">[eq:regular]</a>, and Lemma <a href="#lem:local" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:local">3</a> supplies smoothness in $x$ at the origin. The Hessian eigenvalues for $r>0$ are $y'>0$ and $y/r>0$; at the origin the Hessian is $I$. ◻

</details>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-global-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-global</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.* Take the global radial functions of Proposition <a href="#prop:global" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:global">6</a>. By <a href="#eq:radial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:radial">[eq:radial]</a>, they satisfy <a href="#eq:reduced" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reduced">[eq:reduced]</a> for $x\ne0$. The functions are smooth, $F,P>0$, and $D_x^2F>0$ everywhere, so both identities extend to $x=0$ by continuity. Proposition <a href="#prop:reduction" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:reduction">2</a> now shows that $$u(x,t)=\sqrt{F(|x|)^2+t^2},\qquad (x,t)\in\mathbb R^9\times\mathbb R,$$ is smooth, has positive definite Hessian everywhere, and solves the classical affine maximal equation in dimension ten. Finally, $u(0,t)=\sqrt{1+t^2}$ because $F(0)=1$, so $u$ is not a quadratic polynomial. ◻

</details>
