## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We construct a homogeneous polynomial of degree 16 in 23 real variables whose hyperbolicity cone has no representation by a finite homogeneous real symmetric linear matrix inequality. This disproves the geometric Generalized Lax conjecture.

- **C1** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): The polynomial <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a> is homogeneous of degree $20$ and hyperbolic with respect to $e=(I_4,I_4,0)$, with $p(e)=1$. Its closed hyperbolicity cone $K=\Lambda_+(p,e)$ is not spectrahedral: for every finite $N\geq1$ and every real linear map $L:\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb S^N$, $$K\ne\{(X,Z,y):L(X,Z,y)\succeq0\}.$$
  - Explanation: The explicit hyperbolic polynomial defines a cone excluded from every finite homogeneous real symmetric pencil representation.
- **C2** (theorem, [source](#stmt-cor-reduced)): The quotient $q=p/\det X$ extends to a homogeneous polynomial of degree $16$ in the same $23$ variables. It is hyperbolic with respect to $e$, with $q(e)=1$, and $\Lambda_+(q,e)=K$. In particular, its closed hyperbolicity cone is not spectrahedral.
  - Explanation: Removing a determinant factor preserves hyperbolicity and the same nonspectrahedral cone.
- **C3** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-form)): The form $b:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R$ defined in <a href="#eq:form" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:form">[eq:form]</a> is nonnegative and nonzero. If a real bilinear form $\ell:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R$ satisfies $$\ell(z,y)^2\leq b(z,y) \qquad\text{for every }z,y\in\mathbb R^3,$$ then $\ell=0$.
  - Explanation: The nonnegative, nonzero biquadratic form dominates no nonzero bilinear square.
- **C4** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-hyperbolic)): The polynomial $p$ in <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a> is homogeneous of degree $20$ on $\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3$, a real vector space of dimension $23$. It satisfies $p(e)=1$ and is hyperbolic with respect to $e=(I_4,I_4,0)$.
  - Explanation: The adjugate construction yields a normalized homogeneous hyperbolic polynomial.
- **C5** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-slices)): Let $K=\Lambda_+(p,e)$. For real symmetric $X,Z$ and $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$\begin{aligned} X\succ0:\qquad (X,Z,y)\in K &\ \Longleftrightarrow\ Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1}), \label{eq:slice-positive}\\ (X,Z,0)\in K &\ \Longleftrightarrow\ X\succeq0\ \text{and}\ Z\succeq0. \label{eq:slice-zero} \end{aligned}$$ Moreover, $e$ lies in the interior of $K$ in the full ambient space.
  - Explanation: Positive definite matrix inputs admit an exact cone slice test; the zero parameter slice is a product of positive semidefinite cones.
- **C6** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-prop-pencil)): Let $K=\Lambda_+(p,(I_4,I_4,0))$ for the polynomial in <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a>. Suppose that $K$ admits a representation by a finite homogeneous real symmetric linear pencil. Then there are integers $a,c\geq1$, positive linear maps $$D:\mathbb S^4\longrightarrow\mathbb S^a, \qquad E:\mathbb S^4\longrightarrow\mathbb S^c, \qquad D(I_4)=I_a,\quad E(I_4)=I_c,$$ and a linear map $B:\mathbb R^3\longrightarrow\mathbb R^{a\times c}$ such that, for every $X\succ0$, $Z\in\mathbb S^4$ and $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$\label{eq:schur-equivalence} Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1}) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E(Z)\succeq B(y)^\top D(X)^{-1}B(y).$$
  - Explanation: A hypothetical finite pencil forces positive unital maps and an equivalent Schur inequality.
- **C7** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-prop-norm)): Let $a,c\geq1$, let $D:\mathbb S^4\to\mathbb S^a$ and $E:\mathbb S^4\to\mathbb S^c$ be positive unital linear maps, and let $B:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^{a\times c}$ be linear. Assume that <a href="#eq:schur-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:schur-equivalence">[eq:schur-equivalence]</a> holds for every $X\succ0$, $Z\in\mathbb S^4$ and $y\in\mathbb R^3$, with $\Phi_y$ defined by <a href="#eq:phi" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phi">[eq:phi]</a>. Define $P(v)$ and $R(u)$ by <a href="#eq:projections" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:projections">[eq:projections]</a>. For every pair of unit vectors $u=(u_0,u'),v=(v_0,v')\in\mathbb R^4$ and every $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$b(v_0u'-u_0v',y) =\left\lVert P(v)B(y)R(u)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}. \label{eq:norm-identity}$$ Here the operator norm uses the Euclidean structures on the two block spaces.
  - Explanation: Under that hypothetical Schur equivalence, rank-one limits identify the scalar form with a squared operator norm.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **hyperbolicity** ([source](#sec:introduction)): A homogeneous polynomial $p\in\mathbb R[x_1,\ldots,x_m]$ of degree $d\geq1$ is *hyperbolic with respect to* $e\in\mathbb R^m$ if $p(e)\ne0$ and every root of $t\mapsto p(te-x)$ is real for every $x\in\mathbb R^m$.
- **closed hyperbolicity cone** ([source](#sec:introduction)): The closed hyperbolicity cone is $$\Lambda_+(p,e)=\{x\in\mathbb R^m:\lambda_j(x)\geq0\text{ for }1\leq j\leq d\}.$$
- **symmetric matrices and positivity** ([source](#sec:introduction)): We write $\mathbb S^N$ for the real symmetric $N\times N$ matrices and $A\succeq0$ (respectively, $A\succ0$) for positive semidefiniteness (respectively, positive definiteness).
- **spectrahedral cone** ([source](#sec:introduction)): A cone is *spectrahedral* if it has the form $$K=\{x\in\mathbb R^m:L(x)\succeq0\},\qquad L(x)=\sum_{i=1}^m x_iA_i,\quad A_i\in\mathbb S^N, \label{eq:spectrahedral}$$ for some finite $N\geq1$.
- **Choi–Lam form** ([source](#eq:form)): For $z,y\in\mathbb R^3$, let $$b(z,y)=\sum_{i=1}^3 z_i^2y_i^2 -2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq3}z_iy_iz_jy_j +\sum_{i=1}^3z_i^2y_{i+1}^2. \label{eq:form}$$
- **coefficient matrix** ([source](#eq:form)): Define $Q(y)\in\mathbb S^3$ by $$Q(y)_{ii}=y_i^2+y_{i+1}^2,\qquad Q(y)_{ij}=-y_iy_j\quad(i\ne j),$$ so that $b(z,y)=z^\top Q(y)z$.
- **positive-map construction** ([source](#eq:phi)): For $a\in\mathbb R$, $r\in\mathbb R^3$ and $T\in\mathbb S^3$, define the linear map $\Phi_y:\mathbb S^4\to\mathbb S^4$ by $$\Phi_y\!\begin{pmatrix}a&r^\top\\r&T\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(Q(y)T)&-r^\top Q(y)\\ -Q(y)r&aQ(y) \end{pmatrix}. \label{eq:phi}$$
- **unreduced polynomial** ([source](#eq:polynomial)): If $\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X$ denotes the adjugate of $X$, set $$p(X,Z,y)=\det\big((\det X)Z-\Phi_y(\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X)\big), \qquad (X,Z,y)\in\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3. \label{eq:polynomial}$$
- **kernel projections** ([source](#eq:projections)): For unit vectors $u,v\in\mathbb R^4$, define the orthogonal projections $$P(v)=\operatorname{proj}_{\ker D(I-vv^\top)}, \qquad R(u)=\operatorname{proj}_{\ker E(I-uu^\top)}. \label{eq:projections}$$
- **reduced polynomial** ([source](#sec:reduced)): Define the polynomial $$\label{eq:reduced-polynomial} q(X,Z,y)=\det\begin{pmatrix} I_3\otimes X&C^\top\\ C\bigl(Q(y)\otimes I_4\bigr)&Z \end{pmatrix}.$$

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **form-evaluation**: The explicit cyclic biquadratic form equals its symmetric matrix quadratic form.
- **cone-obstruction**: The constructed closed hyperbolicity cone excludes every finite homogeneous real symmetric defining pencil.
- **weak-extremality**: Every real bilinear form whose square is globally bounded by the specified biquadratic form vanishes.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **direct-lmi**: Build an exact direct semidefinite representation of the constructed cone. -> The conclusion excludes every finite defining pencil.
- **shadow-inference**: Exclude every representation with auxiliary variables. -> The direct representation obstruction does not assert an obstruction to auxiliary-variable representations.
- **finite-proof**: Treat finite evaluations as a proof that every dominated bilinear form vanishes. -> Agreement on finitely many inputs leaves the global domination hypothesis unproved.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `constructive`. The explicit biquadratic form is a finite constructive evaluation formula. The executable checks that formula on rational inputs; it does not decide the global cone or dominated-square obstructions.

```python
# INPUTS: JSON list of pairs of rational coordinate triples z and y.
# PRECONDITIONS: Every coordinate is an integer or a rational string; triples have length three.
# OUTPUTS: JSON list of exact canonical rational values of b(z,y).
# TIME: Linear in the number of pairs in rational arithmetic operations; bit cost depends on input length.
# SPACE: Linear in input and output length.
# ARITHMETIC: Exact rational arithmetic using fmpq.
# FAILURE: Malformed input raises an exception and yields nonzero exit.
import json, sys
from flint import fmpq
pairs = json.load(sys.stdin)
answers = []
for pair in pairs:
    if not isinstance(pair, list) or len(pair) != 2:
        raise ValueError('pair required')
    z, y = pair
    if len(z) != 3 or len(y) != 3:
        raise ValueError('triples required')
    z = [fmpq(str(v)) for v in z]
    y = [fmpq(str(v)) for v in y]
    diag = sum(z[i]**2*y[i]**2 for i in range(3))
    cross = sum(z[i]*y[i]*z[j]*y[j] for i in range(3) for j in range(i+1,3))
    cyclic = sum(z[i]**2*y[(i+1)%3]**2 for i in range(3))
    answers.append(str(diag - 2*cross + cyclic))
print(json.dumps(answers))
```

  fun i j => if i = j then y i ^ 2 + y (i + 1) ^ 2 else -(y i * y j)

  fun i j => Fin.cases
    (Fin.cases
      (Matrix.trace (qMatrix y * X.submatrix Fin.succ Fin.succ))
      (fun j => -(∑ k, X 0 k.succ * qMatrix y k j)) j)
    (fun i => Fin.cases

  fun i j => if h : i ≤ j then v ⟨(i, j), h⟩

## 7. Complete mathematical paper


We construct a homogeneous polynomial of degree 16 in 23 real variables whose hyperbolicity cone has no representation by a finite homogeneous real symmetric linear matrix inequality. This disproves the geometric Generalized Lax conjecture.

<a id="sec:introduction"></a>
### Introduction

A homogeneous polynomial $p\in\mathbb R[x_1,\ldots,x_m]$ of degree $d\geq1$ is *hyperbolic with respect to* $e\in\mathbb R^m$ if $p(e)\ne0$ and every root of $t\mapsto p(te-x)$ is real for every $x\in\mathbb R^m$. Write these roots, with multiplicity, as $\lambda_1(x),\ldots,\lambda_d(x)$. The closed hyperbolicity cone is $$\Lambda_+(p,e)=\{x\in\mathbb R^m:\lambda_j(x)\geq0\text{ for }1\leq j\leq d\}.$$ We write $\mathbb S^N$ for the real symmetric $N\times N$ matrices and $A\succeq0$ (respectively, $A\succ0$) for positive semidefiniteness (respectively, positive definiteness). A cone is *spectrahedral* if it has the form <a id="eq:spectrahedral"></a>$$K=\{x\in\mathbb R^m:L(x)\succeq0\},\qquad
 L(x)=\sum_{i=1}^m x_iA_i,\quad A_i\in\mathbb S^N,
 \label{eq:spectrahedral}$$ for some finite $N\geq1$. The geometric Generalized Lax conjecture asserts that every hyperbolicity cone is spectrahedral. We give a counterexample.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
#### The explicit polynomial

<a id="eq:form"></a>
<a id="eq:phi"></a>
<a id="eq:polynomial"></a>
All indices in $\{1,2,3\}$ in the following construction are cyclic. For $z,y\in\mathbb R^3$, let $$b(z,y)=\sum_{i=1}^3 z_i^2y_i^2
       -2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq3}z_iy_iz_jy_j
       +\sum_{i=1}^3z_i^2y_{i+1}^2.
 \label{eq:form}$$ This is the coefficient-one Choi–Lam biquadratic form (Choi and Lam 1977, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[choi-lam-1977](#ref-choi-lam-1977)]. Define $Q(y)\in\mathbb S^3$ by $$Q(y)_{ii}=y_i^2+y_{i+1}^2,\qquad
 Q(y)_{ij}=-y_iy_j\quad(i\ne j),$$ so that $b(z,y)=z^\top Q(y)z$. For $a\in\mathbb R$, $r\in\mathbb R^3$ and $T\in\mathbb S^3$, define the linear map $\Phi_y:\mathbb S^4\to\mathbb S^4$ by $$\Phi_y\!\begin{pmatrix}a&r^\top\\r&T\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}
    \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(Q(y)T)&-r^\top Q(y)\\
    -Q(y)r&aQ(y)
   \end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:phi}$$ The dependence on $y$ is homogeneous quadratic. If $\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X$ denotes the adjugate of $X$, set $$p(X,Z,y)=\det\big((\det X)Z-\Phi_y(\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X)\big),
 \qquad (X,Z,y)\in\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3.
 \label{eq:polynomial}$$ This formula is polynomial even when $X$ is singular. The ambient space has dimension $10+10+3=23$.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1**. *The polynomial <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a> is homogeneous of degree $20$ and hyperbolic with respect to $e=(I_4,I_4,0)$, with $p(e)=1$. Its closed hyperbolicity cone $K=\Lambda_+(p,e)$ is not spectrahedral: for every finite $N\geq1$ and every real linear map $L:\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb S^N$, $$K\ne\{(X,Z,y):L(X,Z,y)\succeq0\}.$$*

</div>

The conclusion concerns the cone itself, so it allows every possible defining pencil and every finite matrix size. The compact formula for $p$ is convenient for the proof, but one determinant factor can be removed.

<a id="stmt-cor-reduced"></a>
<div id="cor:reduced" class="corollary">

**Corollary 2**. *The quotient $q=p/\det X$ extends to a homogeneous polynomial of degree $16$ in the same $23$ variables. It is hyperbolic with respect to $e$, with $q(e)=1$, and $\Lambda_+(q,e)=K$. In particular, its closed hyperbolicity cone is not spectrahedral.*

</div>

We prove the degree reduction in Section <a href="#sec:reduced" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:reduced">7</a>, after the obstruction argument. We make no claim that the dimension or degree is minimal. The real symmetric convention also covers Hermitian pencils: for a Hermitian matrix $H=A+iB$, positivity is equivalent to positivity of the real symmetric matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&-B\\B&A\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
#### Historical context

Gårding’s theory establishes the convexity of hyperbolicity cones (Gårding 1959) <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[garding-1959](#ref-garding-1959)]. The original Lax conjecture asks for a definite determinantal representation of homogeneous hyperbolic polynomials in three variables (Lax 1958) <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[lax-1958](#ref-lax-1958)]. Helton and Vinnikov proved the corresponding definite determinantal representation theorem (Helton and Vinnikov 2007) <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[helton-vinnikov-2007](#ref-helton-vinnikov-2007)]; Lewis, Parrilo, and Ramana established its equivalence with Lax’s formulation (Lewis, Parrilo, and Ramana 2005) <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[lewis-parrilo-ramana-2005](#ref-lewis-parrilo-ramana-2005)]. In higher dimension, Brändén constructed a real-zero polynomial none of whose positive powers admits a definite determinantal representation (Brändén 2011, Theorem 3.3) <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[branden-2011](#ref-branden-2011)] <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[kummer-2016-vamos](#ref-kummer-2016-vamos)]. That obstruction does not exclude a different determinant, with additional factors, from defining the same cone. The geometric conjecture concerns this remaining possibility. Kummer exhibited the distinction concretely: the cone of the specialized Vámos polynomial has a $7\times7$ representation, although no positive power of that polynomial has a definite determinantal representation (Kummer 2016, sec. 3). Raghavendra, Ryder, Srivastava, and Weitz proved exponential lower bounds on the matrix size of spectrahedral representations for suitable hyperbolicity cones (Raghavendra et al. 2019) <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[raghavendra-ryder-srivastava-2018](#ref-raghavendra-ryder-srivastava-2018)]. Recent positive results include Netzer’s theorem for hyperbolic cubics in five variables (Netzer 2026, Theorem 3.9) <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[netzer-2026](#ref-netzer-2026)].

Kummer and Netzer prove spectrahedrality for cones of strictly hyperbolic polynomials (Kummer and Netzer 2026, Theorem 1) <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[kummer-netzer-2026-strict](#ref-kummer-netzer-2026-strict)]. Here *strictly hyperbolic* means that $p(te-x)$ has distinct roots whenever $x\notin\mathbb Re$. The polynomial <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a> fails this hypothesis: at $w=(I_4,0,0)\notin\mathbb Re$ it gives $$p(te-w)=(t-1)^{16}t^4.$$ For the reduced polynomial of Corollary <a href="#cor:reduced" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:reduced">2</a>, the same line gives $q(te-w)=(t-1)^{12}t^4$. Thus neither defining polynomial satisfies the strict-case hypothesis.

A *spectrahedral shadow* is a linear image of a set defined by an affine real symmetric linear matrix inequality. Netzer and Sanyal proved that a hyperbolicity cone is a spectrahedral shadow when every nonzero boundary point is a smooth point of the defining polynomial (Netzer and Sanyal 2015, Theorem 1.1) <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[netzer-sanyal-2015](#ref-netzer-sanyal-2015)]. Our result concerns a direct representation in the original variables; it makes no assertion about representations using auxiliary variables.

The scalar ingredient is the coefficient-one Choi–Lam biquadratic form (Choi and Lam 1977, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[choi-lam-1977](#ref-choi-lam-1977)], which reduces the coefficient of the extra cyclic squared monomials from two in Choi’s earlier nonnegative form (Choi 1975) <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[choi-1975](#ref-choi-1975)] to one. Its inability to dominate a nonzero bilinear square follows from the extremality proved in (Choi and Lam 1977, Theorem 4.4) <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[choi-lam-1977](#ref-choi-lam-1977)]; we give a short proof of precisely this property. It is called *weak extremality* by Blekherman, Raiţă, Shankar, and Sinn (Blekherman et al. 2022, Definition 2.7) <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[blekherman-raita-shankar-sinn-2022](#ref-blekherman-raita-shankar-sinn-2022)], whose characterization uses Taylor expansions of dominated squares at zeros (Blekherman et al. 2022, Theorem 3.2) <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[blekherman-raita-shankar-sinn-2022](#ref-blekherman-raita-shankar-sinn-2022)]. The correspondence between nonnegative biquadratic forms and positive maps on real symmetric matrices is classical; see (Ha 2013, sec. 3) <a id="pm-cite-17"></a>[[ha-2013](#ref-ha-2013)]. Positive maps have also been used to obtain certificates of conic polynomial stability (Dey, Gardoll, and Theobald 2021) <a id="pm-cite-18"></a>[[dey-gardoll-theobald-2021](#ref-dey-gardoll-theobald-2021)]. Here the construction turns the scalar obstruction into an obstruction to every finite pencil describing one hyperbolicity cone. A related general strategy appears in Scheiderer’s local sums-of-squares obstructions to spectrahedral shadows (Scheiderer 2018, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-19"></a>[[scheiderer-2018](#ref-scheiderer-2018)]. Our proof extracts bilinear square minorants from a differentiated operator-norm identity; it does not invoke a criterion for shadows.

González Nevado states a positive solution of the geometric conjecture in (González Nevado 2026, Theorem 53, pp. 16–17) <a id="pm-cite-20"></a>[[gonzalez-nevado-2026](#ref-gonzalez-nevado-2026)]. Appendix <a href="#app:opposition" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="app:opposition">8</a> records a counterexample to the path-containment assertion used in that argument. The construction and proof in the present paper are independent of that comparison.

<a id="pm-h-5"></a>
#### Proof strategy

The scalar obstruction is particularly rigid: $b$ is nonnegative and nonzero, but every bilinear form $\ell$ satisfying $\ell(z,y)^2\leq b(z,y)$ for all $z,y$ is zero. Section <a href="#sec:form" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:form">2</a> gives a short proof using zeros and curves along which $b$ vanishes to fourth order.

The map $\Phi_y$ translates this form into a matrix inequality. In Section <a href="#sec:hyperbolic" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:hyperbolic">3</a> we prove hyperbolicity and identify the slice of $K$ with $X\succ0$ as $$Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1}).$$ The remaining argument assumes an arbitrary pencil defining $K$ and extracts a bilinear square dominated by $b$. First, Section <a href="#sec:pencil" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:pencil">4</a> rescales the pencil along $(X,s^2Z,sy)$ and obtains an equivalent block matrix inequality. Section <a href="#sec:norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:norm">5</a> then sends $X^{-1}$ and $Z^{-1}$ toward rank-one projections; the resulting comparison yields an exact operator-norm identity for kernel projections of the pencil blocks. These projections are parametrized by the unit sphere in $\mathbb R^4$. Finally, Section <a href="#sec:tangent" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:tangent">6</a> differentiates one kernel projection on a neighborhood of constant rank in that sphere. At a suitable base point, the three-dimensional tangent space can be identified with all the $z$-variables, so this local identity controls $b$ for every $z$ and $y$. Each entry of the resulting matrix is a bilinear form whose square is bounded by $b$, giving a contradiction.

The mechanism connecting the biquadratic form to the geometry of the cone is the rescaling and subsequent first variation of these kernel projections. It applies to an arbitrary hypothetical pencil without comparing its determinant with $p$. All matrix norms below are Euclidean operator norms, denoted by $\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$; vector norms are Euclidean.

<a id="sec:form"></a>
### A biquadratic form with no dominated square

The geometric argument will produce bilinear forms whose squares are bounded by the Choi–Lam form $b$ in <a href="#eq:form" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:form">[eq:form]</a>. We prove directly the particular consequence of its classical extremality (Choi and Lam 1977, Theorem 4.4) <a id="pm-cite-21"></a>[[choi-lam-1977](#ref-choi-lam-1977)] that the geometric argument needs: every such bilinear form must vanish. This property is also called *weak extremality* (Blekherman et al. 2022, Definition 2.7) <a id="pm-cite-22"></a>[[blekherman-raita-shankar-sinn-2022](#ref-blekherman-raita-shankar-sinn-2022)].

<a id="stmt-lem-form"></a>
<div id="lem:form" class="lemma">

**Lemma 3**. *The form $b:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R$ defined in <a href="#eq:form" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:form">[eq:form]</a> is nonnegative and nonzero. If a real bilinear form $\ell:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R$ satisfies $$\ell(z,y)^2\leq b(z,y)
 \qquad\text{for every }z,y\in\mathbb R^3,$$ then $\ell=0$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-form"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-form</summary>

*Proof.* Put $a_i=z_i^2$, with indices read cyclically modulo $3$. The matrix of $b(z,y)$ as a quadratic form in $y$ is $$M(z)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 a_1+a_3&-z_1z_2&-z_1z_3\\
 -z_1z_2&a_2+a_1&-z_2z_3\\
 -z_1z_3&-z_2z_3&a_3+a_2
 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Its diagonal entries are nonnegative. Its three principal minors of order two are $$\det M(z)[\{i,i+1\}]
 =a_i^2+a_{i-1}(a_i+a_{i+1})\geq0,
 \qquad i=1,2,3,$$ where the brackets denote a principal submatrix. Finally, $$\det M(z)
 =a_1^2a_2+a_2^2a_3+a_3^2a_1-3a_1a_2a_3\geq0$$ by the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality. All principal minors are therefore nonnegative, so $M(z)\succeq0$ and $b(z,y)\geq0$. For the standard coordinate vectors $e_1,e_2,e_3$ of $\mathbb R^3$, we have $b(e_1,e_1)=1$.

Now write $\ell(z,y)=\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}z_i y_j$ and assume the stated bound. Zeros will eliminate the off-diagonal coefficients; fourth-order vanishing along curves will eliminate the diagonal coefficients. At every zero of $b$, the form $\ell$ vanishes. In particular, $$b(e_i,e_{i-1})=0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_{i,i-1}=0.$$ For each sign vector $\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^3$, we also have $b(\sigma,\sigma)=3-6+3=0$, and hence $$0=\sum_i c_{ii}
   +\sum_{i<j}(c_{ij}+c_{ji})\sigma_i\sigma_j.$$ Multiplying by $\sigma_p\sigma_q$ and averaging over the eight sign vectors gives $c_{pq}+c_{qp}=0$ for every $p<q$. Together with $c_{i,i-1}=0$, these identities force all off-diagonal entries to vanish. Thus $\ell(z,y)=\sum_i c_i z_i y_i$ for some real $c_i$.

For a cyclic index $i$ and $s\in\mathbb R$, set $$z=e_i+s e_{i-1},\qquad y=e_{i-1}+s e_i.$$ Direct substitution gives $$b(z,y)=2s^2-2s^2+s^4=s^4,
 \qquad \ell(z,y)=s(c_i+c_{i-1}).$$ For $s\ne0$, the bound implies $(c_i+c_{i-1})^2\leq s^2$. Letting $s\to0$ yields $c_i+c_{i-1}=0$ for all three indices. These equations force $c_1=c_2=c_3=0$, proving the lemma. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:hyperbolic"></a>
### Hyperbolicity and two cone slices

We first prove that the polynomial in <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a> is hyperbolic. We then identify the two slices of its closed cone that will constrain any representing pencil.

<a id="eq:rank-one"></a>
For $u=(u_0,u')$ and $v=(v_0,v')$ in $\mathbb R^4$, the block formula <a href="#eq:phi" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phi">[eq:phi]</a> gives $$u^\top\Phi_y(vv^\top)u
 = (v_0u'-u_0v')^\top Q(y)(v_0u'-u_0v')
 = b(v_0u'-u_0v',y).
 \label{eq:rank-one}$$ Indeed, the three terms on expanding the middle expression are $v_0^2u'^\top Q(y)u'$, $-2u_0v_0u'^\top Q(y)v'$, and $u_0^2v'^\top Q(y)v'$, exactly the terms from the block formula. Lemma <a href="#lem:form" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:form">3</a> therefore implies that $\Phi_y(vv^\top)\succeq0$. Every positive semidefinite real symmetric matrix is a nonnegative linear combination of such rank-one matrices. Thus $\Phi_y$ is a positive linear map: it preserves positive semidefiniteness, and hence the order $\preceq$. The same formula also gives <a id="eq:phi-scaling"></a>$$\Phi_{s y}=s^2\Phi_y\qquad(s\in\mathbb R),
 \label{eq:phi-scaling}$$ so in particular $\Phi_0=0$ and $\Phi_{-y}=\Phi_y$. This rank-one verification is the real-symmetric positive-map correspondence for biquadratic forms (Ha 2013, sec. 3) <a id="pm-cite-23"></a>[[ha-2013](#ref-ha-2013)] applied to the explicit map <a href="#eq:phi" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phi">[eq:phi]</a>.

<a id="stmt-prop-hyperbolic"></a>
<div id="prop:hyperbolic" class="proposition">

**Proposition 4**. *The polynomial $p$ in <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a> is homogeneous of degree $20$ on $\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3$, a real vector space of dimension $23$. It satisfies $p(e)=1$ and is hyperbolic with respect to $e=(I_4,I_4,0)$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-hyperbolic"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-hyperbolic</summary>

*Proof.* Each entry of $(\det X)Z-\Phi_y(\operatorname{adj}X)$ is homogeneous of degree $5$: the adjugate has degree $3$, and $\Phi_y$ is quadratic in $y$. Taking its $4\times4$ determinant gives a homogeneous polynomial of degree $20$. Since $\Phi_0=0$, evaluation at $e$ gives $p(e)=1$, so this polynomial is nonzero. The dimension is $10+10+3=23$.

<a id="eq:inverse-formula"></a>
Extend $\Phi_y$ complex linearly to complex symmetric matrices. For invertible $X$, real or complex, linearity and $\operatorname{adj}X=(\det X)X^{-1}$ give $$p(X,Z,y)=(\det X)^4\det\bigl(Z-\Phi_y(X^{-1})\bigr).
 \label{eq:inverse-formula}$$ The adjugate formula remains the definition at singular $X$.

Fix real symmetric $X,Z$ and real $y$, and let $t=a+i\eta$ with $\eta>0$. Orthogonal diagonalization of $X$ gives $$\operatorname{Im}(tI_4-X)^{-1}
 =-\eta\bigl((aI_4-X)^2+\eta^2I_4\bigr)^{-1}\prec0.$$ For a complex symmetric matrix, the entrywise imaginary part equals the Hermitian imaginary part $(A-A^*)/(2i)$ and is real symmetric. Complex linearity and positivity of $\Phi_{-y}$ consequently show that $$H=tI_4-Z-\Phi_{-y}\bigl((tI_4-X)^{-1}\bigr)
 \quad\text{satisfies}\quad
 \operatorname{Im}H\succeq\eta I_4\succ0.$$ Such an $H$ is invertible: if $Hw=0$ for a nonzero complex vector $w$, then $0=\operatorname{Im}(w^*Hw)=w^*(\operatorname{Im}H)w>0$. Also $tI_4-X$ is invertible. Equation <a href="#eq:inverse-formula" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:inverse-formula">[eq:inverse-formula]</a> therefore implies $p(te-(X,Z,y))\ne0$. The coefficients of this polynomial in $t$ are real, so conjugation also excludes roots in the lower half-plane. Finally, its leading coefficient is $p(e)=1$ by homogeneity; it has degree $20$ for every real input. All its roots are therefore real, as required. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-prop-slices"></a>
<div id="prop:slices" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:slice-positive"></a>
<a id="eq:slice-zero"></a>
**Proposition 5**. *Let $K=\Lambda_+(p,e)$. For real symmetric $X,Z$ and $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$\begin{aligned}
 X\succ0:\qquad
 (X,Z,y)\in K
 &\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 
 Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1}),
 \label{eq:slice-positive}\\
 (X,Z,0)\in K
 &\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 
 X\succeq0\ \text{and}\ Z\succeq0.
 \label{eq:slice-zero}
\end{aligned}$$ Moreover, $e$ lies in the interior of $K$ in the full ambient space.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-slices"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-slices</summary>

*Proof.* If $\lambda_1(x),\ldots,\lambda_{20}(x)$ are the roots of $t\mapsto p(te-x)$, homogeneity gives $$p(x+te)=\prod_{j=1}^{20}(t+\lambda_j(x)).$$ Thus $x\in K$ if and only if $p(x+te)\ne0$ for every real $t>0$. The strict inequality on $t$ allows zero roots and hence includes the boundary of $K$.

Suppose $X\succ0$ and set, for $t\ge0$, $$F(t)=Z+tI_4-\Phi_y\bigl((X+tI_4)^{-1}\bigr).$$ If $F(0)\succeq0$, order preservation yields $$F(t)=F(0)+tI_4+
 \Phi_y\bigl(X^{-1}-(X+tI_4)^{-1}\bigr)
 \succeq tI_4\succ0\qquad(t>0).$$ Equation <a href="#eq:inverse-formula" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:inverse-formula">[eq:inverse-formula]</a> then shows that there is no positive root, so $(X,Z,y)\in K$.

Conversely, if $F(0)\not\succeq0$, its least eigenvalue is negative. For $t>0$, positivity gives $$0\preceq\Phi_y\bigl((X+tI_4)^{-1}\bigr)
 \preceq t^{-1}\Phi_y(I_4).$$ Consequently $F(t)\succ0$ for all sufficiently large $t$. Continuity of its least eigenvalue gives a $t>0$ at which $F(t)$ is singular. Since $X+tI_4\succ0$, Equation <a href="#eq:inverse-formula" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:inverse-formula">[eq:inverse-formula]</a> now gives a positive root of $p((X,Z,y)+te)$. This proves <a href="#eq:slice-positive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-positive">[eq:slice-positive]</a> in both directions, including equality in the matrix inequality.

When $y=0$, the polynomial definition directly gives $$p(te-(X,Z,0))=\det(tI_4-X)^4\det(tI_4-Z),$$ also for singular $X$ and $Z$. Its roots are the eigenvalues of $X$, each repeated four times, and the eigenvalues of $Z$. They are all nonnegative exactly when both matrices are positive semidefinite. This proves <a href="#eq:slice-zero" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-zero">[eq:slice-zero]</a>.

Finally, $X\succ0$ and $Z-\Phi_y(X^{-1})\succ0$ define an open subset of the full ambient space. It contains $e$ and, by <a href="#eq:slice-positive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-positive">[eq:slice-positive]</a>, is contained in $K$. Thus $e$ is an interior point. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:pencil"></a>
### What a semidefinite representation would imply

The two slices in Proposition <a href="#prop:slices" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:slices">5</a> constrain every pencil representing $K$. We now turn those constraints into an exact block matrix inequality whose off-diagonal block is linear in $y$. A positive linear map preserves positive semidefiniteness; it is called *unital* if it sends the identity to the identity.

<a id="stmt-prop-pencil"></a>
<div id="prop:pencil" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:schur-equivalence"></a>
**Proposition 6**. *Let $K=\Lambda_+(p,(I_4,I_4,0))$ for the polynomial in <a href="#eq:polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polynomial">[eq:polynomial]</a>. Suppose that $K$ admits a representation by a finite homogeneous real symmetric linear pencil. Then there are integers $a,c\geq1$, positive linear maps $$D:\mathbb S^4\longrightarrow\mathbb S^a,
 \qquad E:\mathbb S^4\longrightarrow\mathbb S^c,
 \qquad D(I_4)=I_a,\quad E(I_4)=I_c,$$ and a linear map $B:\mathbb R^3\longrightarrow\mathbb R^{a\times c}$ such that, for every $X\succ0$, $Z\in\mathbb S^4$ and $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$\label{eq:schur-equivalence}
 Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1})
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 E(Z)\succeq B(y)^\top D(X)^{-1}B(y).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-pencil"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-pencil</summary>

*Proof.* Write the hypothetical pencil as $$L(X,Z,y)=L_1(X)+L_2(Z)+L_3(y).$$ We first remove the pencil’s common kernel and normalize two positive blocks. We then rescale the pencil and prove that its limit represents the same inequality.

*Compression and normalization.* We use the elementary fact that $$A+H\succeq0,\quad A-H\succeq0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ker A\subseteq\ker H.$$ Indeed, if $w\in\ker A$, the two nonnegative quadratic forms on $w$ sum to zero. A positive semidefinite matrix annihilates every vector on which its quadratic form vanishes, so both $(A+H)w$ and $(A-H)w$ are zero.

Since $e$ is interior to $K$, for every ambient vector $x$ there is an $\varepsilon>0$ with $L(e)\pm\varepsilon L(x)\succeq0$. The preceding fact shows that $\ker L(e)$ is a common kernel of the whole pencil. Compressing to its orthogonal complement preserves the represented cone and makes $L(e)\succ0$. This complement is nonzero: otherwise the pencil would vanish identically, whereas $(-I_4,I_4,0)\notin K$ by <a href="#eq:slice-zero" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-zero">[eq:slice-zero]</a>.

The same slice shows that $L_1$ and $L_2$ are positive maps. Set $U=\ker L_1(I_4)$. Positivity at $I_4\pm\varepsilon X$, for small $\varepsilon>0$, and the same kernel argument show that every $L_1(X)$ annihilates $U$. Thus, in $U^\perp\oplus U$, $$L_1(X)=\begin{pmatrix}D(X)&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ Both summands are nonzero. If $U^\perp=0$, then $L_1=0$ and the pencil accepts $(-I_4,I_4,0)$, contradicting <a href="#eq:slice-zero" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-zero">[eq:slice-zero]</a>. If $U=0$, then $L_1(I_4)\succ0$, so $L_1(I_4)-\varepsilon L_2(I_4)\succ0$ for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$. This would accept $(I_4,-\varepsilon I_4,0)$, again contradicting that slice.

Let $a=\dim U^\perp$, $c=\dim U$, and let $E(Z)$ be the lower diagonal block of $L_2(Z)$. Both $D$ and $E$ are positive maps. Moreover, $D(I_4)\succ0$ by the definition of $U$, and $E(I_4)\succ0$ because it is the compression of $L(e)\succ0$ to $U$. Apply the fixed block diagonal congruence $$\operatorname{diag}\bigl(D(I_4)^{-1/2},E(I_4)^{-1/2}\bigr)$$ and retain the notation for the resulting blocks. We now have $D(I_4)=I_a$ and $E(I_4)=I_c$.

*Rescaling the pencil.* Write the other blocks as $$L_2(Z)=\begin{pmatrix}F(Z)&C(Z)\\C(Z)^\top&E(Z)\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 L_3(y)=\begin{pmatrix}A(y)&B(y)\\B(y)^\top&G(y)\end{pmatrix}.$$ Fix $y$ and put $Z_0=\Phi_y(I_4)$. By <a href="#eq:slice-positive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-positive">[eq:slice-positive]</a> and $\Phi_{sy}=s^2\Phi_y$, the point $(I_4,s^2Z_0,sy)$ lies in $K$ for every real $s$. Its lower pencil block therefore satisfies $$s^2E(Z_0)+sG(y)\succeq0 \qquad(s\in\mathbb R).$$ Dividing by $|s|$ and taking limits through positive and negative $s$ gives $G(y)\succeq0$ and $-G(y)\succeq0$. Hence $G(y)=0$.

<a id="eq:scaled-test"></a>
For fixed $X\succ0$, $Z$ and $y$, and any real $s\ne0$, congruence of $L(X,s^2Z,sy)$ by $\operatorname{diag}(I_a,s^{-1}I_c)$ gives <a id="eq:scaled-pencil"></a>$$\label{eq:scaled-pencil}
 M_s(X,Z,y)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 D(X)+sA(y)+s^2F(Z)&B(y)+sC(Z)\\
 B(y)^\top+sC(Z)^\top&E(Z)
 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Congruence preserves positive semidefiniteness for either sign of $s$. The exact slice <a href="#eq:slice-positive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-positive">[eq:slice-positive]</a> gives $$\label{eq:scaled-test}
 M_s(X,Z,y)\succeq0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1}),
 \qquad s\ne0.$$ As $s\to0$, these matrices converge to $$M(X,Z,y)=
 \begin{pmatrix}D(X)&B(y)\\B(y)^\top&E(Z)\end{pmatrix}.$$ It follows immediately that $Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1})$ implies $M(X,Z,y)\succeq0$.

*Recovering the inequality from the limit.* For the converse, suppose that $M(X,Z,y)\succeq0$. Positivity and unitality give $$D(X)\succeq\lambda_{\min}(X)I_a\succ0.$$ For every $\delta>0$, $$M(X,Z+\delta I_4,y)
 =M(X,Z,y)+\operatorname{diag}(0,\delta I_c)
 \succ0.$$ To see strict positivity, a vector with nonzero lower component has strictly positive contribution from the added term. A nonzero vector with lower component zero has strictly positive quadratic form under $D(X)$.

Consequently, for each fixed $\delta>0$, the matrices $M_s(X,Z+\delta I_4,y)$ are positive definite for sufficiently small nonzero $s$. Equation <a href="#eq:scaled-test" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:scaled-test">[eq:scaled-test]</a> yields $Z+\delta I_4\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1})$. Letting $\delta\downarrow0$ proves the converse. We have therefore shown $$Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1})
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M(X,Z,y)\succeq0.$$ Taking the Schur complement of the positive definite block $D(X)$ gives <a href="#eq:schur-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:schur-equivalence">[eq:schur-equivalence]</a>. ◻

</details>

The maps and their finite dimensions are now fixed by the hypothetical pencil. We next recover the scalar form $b$ from <a href="#eq:schur-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:schur-equivalence">[eq:schur-equivalence]</a>; this will force a nonzero bilinear square dominated by $b$ and contradict its defining obstruction.

<a id="sec:norm"></a>
### Rank-one limits and a norm identity

<a id="eq:projections"></a>
Let $D,E,B$ be the maps supplied by Proposition <a href="#prop:pencil" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:pencil">6</a>. Thus $D$ and $E$ are positive maps with output sizes $a,c\geq1$ and are *unital*, meaning $D(I_4)=I_a$ and $E(I_4)=I_c$, and $B(y)$ is an $a\times c$ matrix. For unit vectors $u,v\in\mathbb R^4$, define the orthogonal projections $$P(v)=\operatorname{proj}_{\ker D(I-vv^\top)},
 \qquad
 R(u)=\operatorname{proj}_{\ker E(I-uu^\top)}.
 \label{eq:projections}$$ These act on $\mathbb R^a$ and $\mathbb R^c$, respectively. The next proposition recovers the scalar form <a href="#eq:form" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:form">[eq:form]</a> from the Schur threshold <a href="#eq:schur-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:schur-equivalence">[eq:schur-equivalence]</a>.

<a id="stmt-prop-norm"></a>
<div id="prop:norm" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:norm-identity"></a>
**Proposition 7**. *Let $a,c\geq1$, let $D:\mathbb S^4\to\mathbb S^a$ and $E:\mathbb S^4\to\mathbb S^c$ be positive unital linear maps, and let $B:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^{a\times c}$ be linear. Assume that <a href="#eq:schur-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:schur-equivalence">[eq:schur-equivalence]</a> holds for every $X\succ0$, $Z\in\mathbb S^4$ and $y\in\mathbb R^3$, with $\Phi_y$ defined by <a href="#eq:phi" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phi">[eq:phi]</a>. Define $P(v)$ and $R(u)$ by <a href="#eq:projections" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:projections">[eq:projections]</a>. For every pair of unit vectors $u=(u_0,u'),v=(v_0,v')\in\mathbb R^4$ and every $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$b(v_0u'-u_0v',y)
 =\left\lVert P(v)B(y)R(u)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}.
 \label{eq:norm-identity}$$ Here the operator norm uses the Euclidean structures on the two block spaces.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-norm"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-norm</summary>

<a id="eq:threshold-norm"></a>
*Proof.* Fix $u,v,y$. For $t\geq1$, set $$X_t=vv^\top+t(I-vv^\top),
 \qquad
 Z_t=uu^\top+t(I-uu^\top).$$ Both matrices are at least $I$. Positivity and unitality therefore give $D(X_t)\succeq I$ and $E(Z_t)\succeq I$, so all inverse square roots below exist. Put $$A_t=Z_t^{-1/2}\Phi_y(X_t^{-1})Z_t^{-1/2},
 \qquad
 C_t=D(X_t)^{-1/2}B(y)E(Z_t)^{-1/2}.$$ The matrix $A_t$ is positive semidefinite. For every real $r$, congruence by $Z_t^{-1/2}$ gives $$rZ_t\succeq\Phi_y(X_t^{-1})
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 r\geq\lambda_{\max}(A_t).$$ Likewise, linearity of $E$ and congruence by $E(Z_t)^{-1/2}$ give $$\begin{split}
 E(rZ_t)\succeq B(y)^\top D(X_t)^{-1}B(y)
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad rI\succeq C_t^\top C_t\\
 &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r\geq\left\lVert C_t\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}.
 \end{split}$$ Equation <a href="#eq:schur-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:schur-equivalence">[eq:schur-equivalence]</a> identifies these two closed rays in $r\in\mathbb R$, including their endpoints. Consequently, $$\lambda_{\max}(A_t)=\left\lVert C_t\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}.
 \label{eq:threshold-norm}$$ This applies to rectangular $C_t$ and includes a zero threshold.

The explicit formulas $$X_t^{-1}=vv^\top+t^{-1}(I-vv^\top),
 \qquad
 Z_t^{-1/2}=uu^\top+t^{-1/2}(I-uu^\top)$$ show that $$A_t\longrightarrow
 uu^\top\Phi_y(vv^\top)uu^\top
 =\alpha uu^\top,
 \qquad
 \alpha=u^\top\Phi_y(vv^\top)u\geq0.$$ The sign follows from positivity of $\Phi_y$. Thus the largest eigenvalue of this limit is $\alpha$, also when $\alpha=0$. Continuity of the largest eigenvalue gives $\lambda_{\max}(A_t)\to\alpha$.

<a id="eq:kernel-limit"></a>
For the other side, unitality gives $$D(X_t)=I+(t-1)D(I-vv^\top).$$ If $H\succeq0$ is a fixed finite-dimensional matrix, diagonalizing $H$ shows that $$^{-1/2}
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{proj}_{\ker H}
 \quad\text{in operator norm}.
 \label{eq:kernel-limit}$$ Indeed, the factor on an eigenvector of eigenvalue $\mu\geq0$ is $(1+(t-1)\mu)^{-1/2}$, which is one when $\mu=0$ and tends to zero otherwise. Applying <a href="#eq:kernel-limit" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:kernel-limit">[eq:kernel-limit]</a> to $D(I-vv^\top)$ and $E(I-uu^\top)$ yields $$D(X_t)^{-1/2}\longrightarrow P(v),
 \qquad
 E(Z_t)^{-1/2}\longrightarrow R(u).$$ Hence $C_t\to P(v)B(y)R(u)$ in operator norm. The dimensions $a,c$ are fixed throughout; the limits include zero and identity kernel projections. Taking limits in <a href="#eq:threshold-norm" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:threshold-norm">[eq:threshold-norm]</a> and using the rank-one identity <a href="#eq:rank-one" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:rank-one">[eq:rank-one]</a> proves <a href="#eq:norm-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:norm-identity">[eq:norm-identity]</a>. ◻

</details>

At $u=v$, the left side of <a href="#eq:norm-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:norm-identity">[eq:norm-identity]</a> vanishes, so $P(v)B(y)R(v)=0$ for every $y$. We next study the identity as $u$ varies near $v$.

<a id="sec:tangent"></a>
### The tangent obstruction

We finish the proof by taking a first-order limit in the norm identity at a point where the kernel projection varies smoothly. This produces a bilinear matrix whose entries must vanish by Lemma <a href="#lem:form" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:form">3</a>.

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-norm-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-norm</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.* Proposition <a href="#prop:hyperbolic" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:hyperbolic">4</a> establishes the polynomial’s degree, normalization, and hyperbolicity. Suppose its closed hyperbolicity cone has a real symmetric pencil representation of some finite size. Fix the maps $D,E,B$ supplied by Proposition <a href="#prop:pencil" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:pencil">6</a>, with their fixed finite dimensions $a,c$. Proposition <a href="#prop:norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:norm">7</a> gives <a href="#eq:norm-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:norm-identity">[eq:norm-identity]</a> for the projections in <a href="#eq:projections" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:projections">[eq:projections]</a>. We shall show that this identity is impossible.

Write $S^3=\{u\in\mathbb R^4:\left\lVert u\right\rVert=1\}$ and consider the symmetric matrix $$A(u)=E(I-uu^\top),\qquad
 u\in\Omega:=\{u\in S^3:u_0\ne0\}.$$ Its ranks take only finitely many values, so their maximum $\rho$ is attained at some $v\in\Omega$. If $\rho>0$, select $\rho$ independent columns of $A(v)$, and let $W(u)$ contain the same columns of $A(u)$. These columns remain independent on a neighborhood of $v$ in $\Omega$. By maximality of $\rho$, they span the range of $A(u)$ throughout that neighborhood. Symmetry of $A(u)$ then gives $$R(u)=I-W(u)\bigl(W(u)^\top W(u)\bigr)^{-1}W(u)^\top .$$ This is a smooth matrix function. If $\rho=0$, then $R(u)=I$ on $\Omega$, so it is smooth in this case as well.

<a id="eq:tangent-identity"></a>
Keep $v$ fixed. At $u=v$, Equation <a href="#eq:norm-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:norm-identity">[eq:norm-identity]</a> gives $$P(v)B(y)R(v)=0\qquad(y\in\mathbb R^3),$$ because $b(0,y)=0$. For $h=(h_0,h')\in v^\perp$, define $$Jh=v_0h'-h_0v',
 \qquad
 u_s=\frac{v+sh}{\sqrt{1+s^2\left\lVert h\right\rVert^2}}.$$ For small $s$, this path stays in the neighborhood where $R$ is smooth, and its velocity at zero is $h$. Moreover, $$v_0u_s'-(u_s)_0v'
   =\frac{s\,Jh}{\sqrt{1+s^2\left\lVert h\right\rVert^2}}.$$ Thus, for $s\ne0$, homogeneity of $b$ and <a href="#eq:norm-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:norm-identity">[eq:norm-identity]</a> give $$\frac{b(Jh,y)}{1+s^2\left\lVert h\right\rVert^2}
 =
 \left\lVert P(v)B(y)\frac{R(u_s)-R(v)}{s}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}.$$ The matrix difference quotient converges to the differential $dR_v(h)$. Passing to the limit by continuity of the operator norm yields $$\label{eq:tangent-identity}
 b(Jh,y)=\left\lVert P(v)B(y)dR_v(h)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}
 \qquad(h\in v^\perp,\ y\in\mathbb R^3).$$ Only $R$ has been differentiated; the projection $P(v)$ remains fixed. Figure <a href="#fig:local-tangent" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:local-tangent">1</a> illustrates this local variation.

The linear map $J:v^\perp\to\mathbb R^3$ is an isomorphism. Indeed, if $Jh=0$, then $v_0\ne0$ gives $h=(h_0/v_0)v$; since $h\perp v$, this forces $h=0$. Both spaces have dimension three. Consequently the matrix $$F(z,y)=P(v)B(y)dR_v(J^{-1}z),\qquad z,y\in\mathbb R^3,$$ has entries that are bilinear in $z,y$, since $B$ and the differential $dR_v$ are linear. In fixed orthonormal bases, each entry $\ell_{ij}(z,y)$ satisfies $$\ell_{ij}(z,y)^2
 \le \left\lVert F(z,y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}
 =b(z,y)
 \qquad(z,y\in\mathbb R^3).$$ Lemma <a href="#lem:form" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:form">3</a> forces every entry to vanish identically. Equation <a href="#eq:tangent-identity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:tangent-identity">[eq:tangent-identity]</a> would therefore make $b$ identically zero, contradicting $b(e_1,e_1)=1$. This excludes the hypothetical finite pencil and completes the proof. ◻

### Removing a determinant factor

The polynomial $p$ makes the positive-map construction explicit through a $4\times4$ determinant. We now remove a factor that is unnecessary for its hyperbolicity cone. A larger block determinant shows directly that the quotient remains polynomial at singular $X$.

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-norm-3"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-norm</summary>

<a id="eq:reduced-polynomial"></a>
<a id="eq:reduced-factor"></a>
*Proof of Corollary <a href="#cor:reduced" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:reduced">2</a>.* For the standard coordinate vectors $e_i$ of $\mathbb R^3$, put $$K_i=\begin{pmatrix}0&e_i^\top\\-e_i&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{4\times4},
 \qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}K_1&K_2&K_3\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{4\times12}.$$ Here $U\otimes V$ denotes the block matrix with blocks $U_{ij}V$. Direct block multiplication in <a href="#eq:phi" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:phi">[eq:phi]</a> gives, for $T\in\mathbb S^4$, $$\Phi_y(T)=\sum_{i,j=1}^3 Q(y)_{ij}K_iTK_j^\top
          =C\bigl(Q(y)\otimes T\bigr)C^\top.$$ Define the polynomial $$\label{eq:reduced-polynomial}
 q(X,Z,y)=\det\begin{pmatrix}
 I_3\otimes X&C^\top\\
 C\bigl(Q(y)\otimes I_4\bigr)&Z
 \end{pmatrix}.$$ For invertible $X$, taking a Schur complement yields $$q(X,Z,y)=(\det X)^3\det\bigl(Z-\Phi_y(X^{-1})\bigr).$$ Comparison with <a href="#eq:inverse-formula" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:inverse-formula">[eq:inverse-formula]</a> proves the polynomial identity $$\label{eq:reduced-factor}
 p(X,Z,y)=(\det X)q(X,Z,y)$$ first for invertible $X$ and then everywhere by continuity. Since $p$ and $\det X$ are homogeneous of degrees $20$ and $4$, this identity makes $q$ homogeneous of degree $16$; also $q(e)=1$. For each real $(X,Z,y)$, the polynomial $q(te-(X,Z,y))$ is a factor of the real-rooted polynomial $p(te-(X,Z,y))$. Hence $q$ is hyperbolic with respect to $e$.

<a id="eq:cone-intersection"></a>
Write $K_q=\Lambda_+(q,e)$. The factorization <a href="#eq:reduced-factor" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reduced-factor">[eq:reduced-factor]</a> gives $$\label{eq:cone-intersection}
 K=\{(X,Z,y):X\succeq0\}\cap K_q,$$ because the roots contributed by $\det(tI_4-X)$ are the eigenvalues of $X$. It remains to show that $K_q$ already forces $X\succeq0$. The polynomial $q$ is even in $y$, so $(X,Z,y)\in K_q$ implies $(X,Z,-y)\in K_q$. By convexity of hyperbolicity cones (Gårding 1959) <a id="pm-cite-24"></a>[[garding-1959](#ref-garding-1959)], their midpoint $(X,Z,0)$ also belongs to $K_q$. But <a href="#eq:reduced-polynomial" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reduced-polynomial">[eq:reduced-polynomial]</a> gives $$q(X,Z,0)=(\det X)^3\det Z,$$ whose closed hyperbolicity cone in this slice is exactly $\{(X,Z,0):X\succeq0,\ Z\succeq0\}$. Thus $X\succeq0$ throughout $K_q$, and <a href="#eq:cone-intersection" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:cone-intersection">[eq:cone-intersection]</a> implies $K_q=K$. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> now supplies nonspectrahedrality. ◻

</details>

<a id="app:opposition"></a>
### A path-containment assertion

Theorem 53 (pp. 16–17) of version 1 of González Nevado’s (González Nevado 2026) <a id="pm-cite-25"></a>[[gonzalez-nevado-2026](#ref-gonzalez-nevado-2026)] states a positive solution of the geometric Generalized Lax conjecture. Its proof invokes Theorem 52 (p. 16), which asserts containment of a fixed rigidly convex set throughout a smooth real-zero deformation from a product of normalized tangent linear factors to a multiple of the defining polynomial. The following example violates that intermediate assertion under its stated hypotheses.

A real polynomial $g$ with $g(0)=1$ is *real-zero* if the roots of $s\mapsto g(sx)$ are real for every real vector $x$. Its closed rigidly convex set is the closure of the component of $\{g\ne0\}$ containing the origin. Fix $a,b,\eta>0$ and, in one variable, put $$g(x)=(1-x/a)(1+x/b),\qquad
 c(t)=1+\eta\sin^2(\pi t),\qquad
 H_t(x)=g(c(t)x),\quad 0\le t\le1.$$ The zero set of $g$ is the compact smooth set $\{-b,a\}$. The normalized tangent linear factors at these two points are $1+x/b$ and $1-x/a$, whose product is $g$. Every $H_t$ has value $1$ at the origin and two distinct real roots, $$-\frac{b}{c(t)}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{a}{c(t)}.$$ Thus the path depends smoothly on $t$ and consists of real-zero polynomials with compact smooth zero sets. It even admits the smooth monic real symmetric determinantal representation $$H_t(x)=\det\begin{pmatrix}
 1-c(t)x/a&0\\
 0&1+c(t)x/b
 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Both endpoints equal $g$, so the final polynomial is $g$ times the constant cofactor $1$, as allowed by the stated hypotheses. Nevertheless the rigidly convex set of $H_t$ is $$\left[-\frac{b}{c(t)},\frac{a}{c(t)}\right],$$ which is strictly smaller than $[-b,a]$ whenever $0<t<1$. Consequently it does not contain the rigidly convex set of $g$.

The parameters $a,b>0$ describe the open family of normalized real quadratics with negative leading coefficient, so the failure is not confined to a symmetric or multiple-root example. The parameter $\eta$ may be arbitrarily small. The cited Theorem 52 states no restriction to two or more variables, no lower bound on pencil size, and no requirement that the endpoint cofactor be nonconstant.

This example refutes the all-intermediate-times conclusion of that theorem. Its endpoints coincide, so it does not refute an endpoint-only statement. The nonspectrahedrality result of this paper follows from the construction and obstruction proved above.
