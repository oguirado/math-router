## 1. Abstract and result contract

> For every integer <i>n</i> ≥ 2 and every origin-symmetric convex body $`K\subset\mathbb R^n`$, we prove that the Gromov width of $`\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits K\times\mathop{\mathrm{int}}\nolimits K^\circ`$ is 4. We construct smooth symplectic embeddings of standard balls of every capacity $`0\lt c\lt 4`$ into this polar product. Volume preservation then resolves the symmetric Mahler conjecture positively in every dimension.

- **C1** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): For every integer $n\ge2$ and every origin-symmetric convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$, $$c_G(U_K)=4.$$ More precisely, for every $0<c<4$ there is a smooth symplectic embedding $B^{2n}(c)\hookrightarrow U_K$.
  - Explanation: The polar product has Gromov width equal to the stated capacity, with smooth ball embeddings at every strictly smaller positive capacity.
- **C2** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-thm-holomorphic-ball)): Let $n,m\ge1$, let $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$ be an open set containing $0$, and let $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_m):\Omega\to\mathbb C^m$ be holomorphic. Suppose that, for some integer $k\ge1$,* 1. *$f^{-1}(0)=\{0\}$ and $\|f(z)\|=O(\|z\|^k)$ as $z\to0$;* 2. *for every $0<s<1$, the set $\{z\in\Omega:\|f(z)\|^2\le s\}$ is a compact subset of $\Omega$.* *Set $$\tau=\|f\|^2,\qquad V=\{z\in\Omega:\tau(z)<1\}, \qquad \omega=dd^c(\|z\|^2+\tau).$$ For every $0<c<\pi k$, there is a smooth symplectic embedding $$\bigl(B^{2n}(c),\omega_0\bigr)\longrightarrow(V,\omega).$$
  - Explanation: An isolated common holomorphic zero with the stated vanishing bound and compact sublevels yields ball embeddings for the specified symplectic form.
- **C3** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-planar-map)): The holomorphic function $$\label{eq:planar-F} F(w)=\frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^\ell w^{2\ell+1}}{(2\ell+1)^2}, \qquad w\in\mathbb D,$$ is a biholomorphism from $\mathbb D$ onto a bounded domain $D$. It is odd, commutes with conjugation, and satisfies $F(0)=0$. The imaginary coordinates of $D$ range over $(-1,1)$, and for each $t\in(-1,1)$ there is a finite $V(t)>0$ such that $$\label{eq:horizontal-slices} \{v\in\mathbb R:v+it\in D\}=(-V(t),V(t)).$$ Write $F(ir)=iT(r)$ for $0\le r<1$. The function $T$ increases strictly from $0$ to $1$, with $$\label{eq:planar-T} T'(r)=\frac{8}{\pi^2}\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}r}{r} \quad(0<r<1), \qquad \lim_{r\uparrow1}T'(r)=+\infty.$$ For fixed $t\in(-1,1)$, put $r_0=T^{-1}(\|t\|)$. The positive horizontal slice is parametrized by $$w(r,t)=re^{i\theta(r,t)},\qquad F(w(r,t))=v(r,t)+it,\qquad r_0<r<1,$$ where $-\pi/2<\theta(r,t)<\pi/2$ and $v(r,t)$ increases strictly from $0$ to $V(t)$.
  - Explanation: The holomorphic series gives a bounded conformal domain with centered horizontal slices and a monotone radial parametrization.
- **C4** (bound, [source](#stmt-prop-uniform-slice)): Each $J_k$ is smooth on $D$, is odd in $v$, and satisfies $$\label{eq:slice-derivative} \partial_v J_k(v,t) =k^2\|g(v+it)\|^{2k-2}\|g'(v+it)\|^2\ge0.$$ There is a sequence $\epsilon_k\ge0$, depending only on $F$, with $\epsilon_k\to0$ such that, simultaneously for every $v+it\in D$, $$\label{eq:uniform-slice} \frac{\|J_k(v,t)\|}{k} \le\frac{\pi}{4}\|g(v+it)\|^{2k}+\epsilon_k.$$
  - Explanation: The signed slice primitives have a uniform asymptotic bound across the entire conformal domain, including heights approaching the tips.
- **C5** (construction, [source](#stmt-prop-finite-strips)): Let $n\ge2$ be an integer, let $b_1,\ldots,b_m\in\mathbb R^n$ span $\mathbb R^n$, and set $$K=\{x\in\mathbb R^n: \|b_j\cdot x\|\le 1, \quad 1\le j\le m\}.$$ For every $0<c<4$ there is a smooth symplectic embedding $$B^{2n}(c)\longrightarrow \operatorname{int}(K)\times\operatorname{int}(K^\circ).$$
  - Explanation: A finite spanning system of symmetric strips admits smooth symplectic ball embeddings into its polar product below the stated capacity.
- **C6** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-polar-approximation)): Let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be an origin-symmetric convex body. For every $\alpha>0$, there are finitely many vectors $b_1,\ldots,b_m\in\mathbb R^n$ spanning $\mathbb R^n$ such that $$K'=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:\|\langle b_j,x\rangle\|\le1,\quad 1\le j\le m\}$$ satisfies $$\label{eq:polar-approximation} K\subset K'\subset(1+\alpha)K, \qquad (K')^\circ\subset K^\circ.$$
  - Explanation: Finite strip bodies approximate a symmetric convex body from outside while their polars remain contained in its polar; containment need not be strict.
- **C7** (construction, [source](#stmt-rem-parameter-order)): For each prescribed $0<c<4$, first choose $\alpha>0$ so that $\lambda c<4$, where $\lambda=1+\alpha$. Then fix the finite body $K'$ and its $m$ defining constraints. Choose $\eta>0$ so that $(1+\eta)\lambda c<4$, then choose $k$ large enough that $$\frac{C_{K'}}k+m\epsilon_k<\frac{\eta\pi}{4}.$$ This is possible because the constraint list is fixed before $k$ varies. Proposition <a href="#prop:finite-strips" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:finite-strips">5</a>, applied at capacity $\lambda c$, and the final rescaling then give the required ball. Each prescribed capacity is obtained by one finite construction; no limit of embeddings is required.
  - Explanation: The approximation body and its constraint list are fixed before the power is chosen; each prescribed capacity uses a finite construction.
- **C8** (bound, [source](#stmt-cor-mahler)): For every integer $n\ge1$ and every origin-symmetric convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$, $$\operatorname{vol}_n(K)\,\operatorname{vol}_n(K^\circ)\ge\frac{4^n}{n!}.$$
  - Explanation: Volume preservation of the ball embeddings implies the symmetric volume-product lower bound.
- **C9** (bound, [source](#stmt-auto-remark-1)): The cube $[-1,1]^n$ has volume $2^n$. Its polar is $\{p:\sum_{j=1}^n\|p_j\|\le1\}$, whose intersection with each coordinate orthant is a simplex of volume $1/n!$. These $2^n$ simplices meet only along faces, so the polar has volume $2^n/n!$. Their volume product is therefore $4^n/n!$, showing that the constant in Corollary <a href="#cor:mahler" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:mahler">8</a> is sharp.
  - Explanation: The cube and its polar crosspolytope attain the displayed volume product, establishing sharpness without classifying every equality case.
- **C10** (conditional_result, [source](#stmt-cor-polar-packings)): Let $n\ge3$ and $k\ge1$ be integers, and let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be an origin-symmetric convex body. Suppose that $R_1,\ldots,R_k>0$ satisfy $$\sum_{i=1}^k R_i^n<4^n, \qquad R_i+R_j<4\quad(1\le i<j\le k).$$ Then there are symplectic embeddings of open neighborhoods of the closed balls $\overline{B^{2n}(R_1)},\ldots,\overline{B^{2n}(R_k)}$ into $U_K$ with pairwise disjoint images. The pairwise condition is vacuous for $k=1$.
  - Explanation: The strict capacity inequalities suffice for disjoint embeddings of neighborhoods of the closed balls; the statement is a sufficient criterion.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Origin-symmetric convex body** ([source](#sec:introduction)): Let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be an origin-symmetric convex body: a compact convex set with nonempty interior and $K=-K$.
- **Polar body** ([source](#sec:introduction)): Its polar is $$K^\circ=\{p\in\mathbb R^n:\langle q,p\rangle\le1\text{ for every }q\in K\}.$$
- **Polar product and standard symplectic form** ([source](#sec:introduction)): We place $K$ in the position coordinates and $K^\circ$ in the conjugate momentum coordinates of $(\mathbb R^n_q\times\mathbb R^n_p,\omega_0)$, where $$\omega_0=\sum_{j=1}^n dq_j\wedge dp_j, \qquad U_K=\operatorname{int}K\times\operatorname{int}K^\circ.$$
- **Standard ball of capacity c** ([source](#sec:introduction)): For $c>0$, write $$B^{2n}(c)=\{(q,p):\pi(\|q\|^2+\|p\|^2)<c\}.$$
- **Gromov width** ([source](#sec:introduction)): The Gromov width $c_G(U)$ of an open set $U\subset\mathbb R^{2n}$ is the supremum of the capacities $c$ for which there is a smooth embedding $e:B^{2n}(c)\to U$ satisfying $e^*\omega_0=\omega_0$.
- **Signed slice primitive** ([source](#eq:slice-primitive)): For an integer $k\ge2$, define the signed slice integral $$\label{eq:slice-primitive} J_k(v,t)=k^2\int_0^v \|g(h+it)\|^{2k-2}\|g'(h+it)\|^2\,dh, \qquad v+it\in D.$$
- **Support function** ([source](#sec:realization)): Introduce the support function $$h_K(p)=\max_{y\in K}\langle p,y\rangle.$$

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **polar-width**: The polar product has Gromov width $4$, with smooth symplectic embeddings for $0<c<4$.
- **cube-sharpness**: The cube volume is $2^n$, its polar volume is $2^n/n!$, and their volume product is $4^n/n!$.
- **strict-packing**: Strict volume and pairwise-capacity inequalities give disjoint symplectic embeddings of neighborhoods of the closed balls into the polar product.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **endpoint**: Require a ball embedding at capacity $4$. -> The supremum does not supply an embedding at its endpoint.
- **arbitrary-domain**: Apply the width equality to an arbitrary convex phase-space domain. -> The domain may lack the symmetric polar-product structure.
- **nonstrict-packing**: Accept equality in the packing inequalities. -> The proof needs strictness to choose an intermediate target ball.
- **sharpness-as-general-proof**: Use the cube volume computation to certify embeddings for every symmetric convex body. -> Exact volumes of the cube and its polar establish a sharpness example, not the general analytic construction.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `constructive`. The cube sharpness computation is finite: its polar decomposes into coordinate-orthant simplices. Exact simplex volumes determine the polar volume and its product with the cube volume. This calculation does not construct or certify the general symplectic embeddings.

```python
# INPUTS: JSON object with integer n.
# PRECONDITIONS: 1 <= n <= 6; booleans and other inputs are rejected.
# OUTPUTS: Exact cube, polar, and product volumes as rational strings, or input_contract error.
# TIME: O(2**n * n**3) exact arithmetic operations.
# SPACE: O(n**2) matrix storage plus bounded sign enumeration.
# ARITHMETIC: Exact integer and rational arithmetic with python-flint.
# FAILURE: Invalid input returns input_contract; unexpected arithmetic failure aborts.
import sys, json, itertools
from flint import fmpz, fmpq, fmpq_mat

payload = json.load(sys.stdin)
dimension = payload.get("n") if isinstance(payload, dict) else None
if type(dimension) is not int or not 1 <= dimension <= 6:
    print(json.dumps({"error": "input_contract"}, sort_keys=True))
else:
    denominator = fmpz(1)
    for j in range(1, dimension + 1):
        denominator *= j
    polar_volume = fmpq(0)
    for signs in itertools.product((-1, 1), repeat=dimension):
        vertices = [[signs[row] if row == col else 0
                     for col in range(dimension)] for row in range(dimension)]
        simplex_volume = abs(fmpq_mat(vertices).det()) / denominator
        polar_volume += simplex_volume
    cube_volume = fmpq(2) ** dimension
    result = {"cube": str(cube_volume), "polar": str(polar_volume),
              "product": str(cube_volume * polar_volume)}
    print(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True))
```

import Mathlib

namespace OAI

open Set
open scoped ContDiff ENNReal

namespace SymmetricPolar

abbrev Position (n : ℕ) := EuclideanSpace ℝ (Fin n)
abbrev Phase (n : ℕ) := Position n × Position n

def IsSymmetricConvexBody {n : ℕ} (K : Set (Position n)) : Prop :=
  IsCompact K ∧ Convex ℝ K ∧ (interior K).Nonempty ∧
    ∀ x, x ∈ K ↔ -x ∈ K

def polar {n : ℕ} (K : Set (Position n)) : Set (Position n) :=
  {p | ∀ q ∈ K, inner (𝕜 := ℝ) q p ≤ 1}

def polarProduct {n : ℕ} (K : Set (Position n)) : Set (Phase n) :=
  interior K ×ˢ interior (polar K)

def capacityBall (n : ℕ) (c : ℝ) : Set (Phase n) :=
  {z | Real.pi * (‖z.1‖ ^ 2 + ‖z.2‖ ^ 2) < c}

noncomputable def omega0 {n : ℕ} (v w : Phase n) : ℝ :=
  inner (𝕜 := ℝ) v.1 w.2 - inner (𝕜 := ℝ) w.1 v.2

def HasSymplecticEmbedding {n : ℕ} (U V : Set (Phase n)) : Prop :=
  ∃ e : Phase n → Phase n,
    ContDiffOn ℝ ∞ e U ∧
    Topology.IsEmbedding (fun z : U => e z) ∧
    MapsTo e U V ∧
    ∀ z ∈ U, ∀ v w : Phase n,
      omega0 (fderiv ℝ e z v) (fderiv ℝ e z w) = omega0 v w

noncomputable def gromovWidth {n : ℕ} (U : Set (Phase n)) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  sSup (ENNReal.ofReal '' {c : ℝ | 0 < c ∧ HasSymplecticEmbedding (capacityBall n c) U})

    (K : Set (Position n)) (hK : IsSymmetricConvexBody K) :
    gromovWidth (polarProduct K) = 4 ∧
      ∀ c : ℝ, 0 < c → c < 4 →
        HasSymplecticEmbedding (capacityBall n c) (polarProduct K) := by
  sorry

end SymmetricPolar

end OAI

<a id="sec:introduction"></a>
### Introduction

Let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be an origin-symmetric convex body: a compact convex set with nonempty interior and $K=-K$. Its polar is $$K^\circ=\{p\in\mathbb R^n:\langle q,p\rangle\le1\text{ for every }q\in K\}.$$ We place $K$ in the position coordinates and $K^\circ$ in the conjugate momentum coordinates of $(\mathbb R^n_q\times\mathbb R^n_p,\omega_0)$, where $$\omega_0=\sum_{j=1}^n dq_j\wedge dp_j,
 \qquad U_K=\operatorname{int}K\times\operatorname{int}K^\circ.$$ For $c>0$, write $$B^{2n}(c)=\{(q,p):\pi(|q|^2+|p|^2)<c\}.$$ The Gromov width $c_G(U)$ of an open set $U\subset\mathbb R^{2n}$ is the supremum of the capacities $c$ for which there is a smooth embedding $e:B^{2n}(c)\to U$ satisfying $e^*\omega_0=\omega_0$.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1**. *For every integer $n\ge2$ and every origin-symmetric convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$, $$c_G(U_K)=4.$$ More precisely, for every $0<c<4$ there is a smooth symplectic embedding $B^{2n}(c)\hookrightarrow U_K$.*

</div>

The theorem imposes no smoothness, strict convexity, unconditional symmetry, or product structure on $K$. It concerns every capacity strictly below $4$; no embedding at the supremum is needed. Symplectic embeddings preserve volume, and $\operatorname{vol}_{2n}(B^{2n}(c))=c^n/n!$. Consequently Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> gives $$\operatorname{vol}_n(K)\operatorname{vol}_n(K^\circ)\ge\frac{4^n}{n!}.$$ Thus the theorem resolves the symmetric Mahler conjecture positively; the complete deduction, including the one-dimensional case, appears in Corollary <a href="#cor:mahler" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:mahler">8</a>. We make no claim here about the classification of equality cases.

For $n\ge3$, the width theorem also transports finite ball packings satisfying strict volume and pairwise-capacity inequalities into $U_K$; see Corollary <a href="#cor:polar-packings" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:polar-packings">10</a>.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
#### Background and prior constructions

The symmetric volume-product problem goes back to Mahler’s work on transference in the geometry of numbers (Mahler 1939) <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[Mahler1939](#ref-Mahler1939)]. Earlier sharp volume inequalities cover the plane (Böröczky et al. 2013, Theorem 13) <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[BoroczkyMakaiMeyerReisner2013](#ref-BoroczkyMakaiMeyerReisner2013)], unconditional bodies (those invariant under coordinate sign changes) (Reisner 1987, secs. 1–2) <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[Reisner1987](#ref-Reisner1987)], and dimension three (Iriyeh and Shibata 2020) <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[IriyehShibata](#ref-IriyehShibata)]. Bourgain and Milman’s asymptotic reverse Santaló inequality gives a lower bound $a^n/n!$ for some $a>0$ independent of dimension (Bourgain and Milman 1987) <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[BourgainMilman](#ref-BourgainMilman)]; the sharp target is $4^n/n!$. The companion paper (OpenAI 2026b) <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[SymmetricCompanion2026](#ref-SymmetricCompanion2026)] discusses the wider convex-geometric history and the equality problem. Our focus is the stronger geometric assertion that the polar product contains large symplectic balls.

Gromov’s nonsqueezing theorem established that ball embeddings detect symplectic restrictions beyond volume preservation (Gromov 1985) <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[Gromov1985](#ref-Gromov1985)]. Viterbo later proposed a sharp volume–capacity inequality for convex domains (Viterbo 2000) <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[Viterbo2000](#ref-Viterbo2000)]. Artstein-Avidan, Karasev, and Ostrover connected this proposed inequality to the symmetric Mahler conjecture and proved $$c_{\mathrm{HZ}}(K\times K^\circ)=4$$ for every origin-symmetric convex body (Artstein-Avidan, Karasev, and Ostrover 2014, Theorems 1.6–1.7 and Section 4) <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[AAKO2014](#ref-AAKO2014)]. Here $c_{\mathrm{HZ}}$ is the Hofer–Zehnder capacity, normalized to have value $\pi R^2$ on a ball of radius $R$ (Hofer and Zehnder 1994) <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[HoferZehnder1994](#ref-HoferZehnder1994)]. This computation implies the upper bound for Gromov width. We use the supporting-cylinder argument of (Artstein-Avidan, Karasev, and Ostrover 2014, Remark 4.2) <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[AAKO2014](#ref-AAKO2014)] and nonsqueezing to prove that bound directly. The matching lower bound requires actual ball embeddings; it does not follow from the Hofer–Zehnder capacity computation.

Earlier constructions establish this lower bound for particular families. Karasev proves it for $\ell_p$ unit balls, $1<p<\infty$, (Karasev 2021, Proposition 3.1), using coordinatewise area-preserving constructions related to those of Latschev, McDuff, and Schlenk (Latschev, McDuff, and Schlenk 2013) <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[LatschevMcDuffSchlenk2013](#ref-LatschevMcDuffSchlenk2013)]. Ramos and Sepe identify the interior of the cube–crosspolytope product with the open ball of capacity $4$ (Ramos and Sepe 2019, Theorem 7) <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[RamosSepe2019](#ref-RamosSepe2019)]. Vicente obtains capacity equalities for functional-dual products defined by Young functions and lower bounds for ordinary polar products (Vicente 2025, Theorems 1.3 and 1.5) <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[Vicente2025](#ref-Vicente2025)]. Functional duality and ordinary polarity give different partners, so these results require separate hypotheses. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> answers positively the unrestricted symmetric-polar-product width question formulated in (Vicente 2026) <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[VicenteQuestion2026](#ref-VicenteQuestion2026)].

The unrestricted Viterbo volume–capacity conjecture was disproved by Haim-Kislev and Ostrover (Haim-Kislev and Ostrover 2026) <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[HKO2025](#ref-HKO2025)]. Their four-dimensional counterexample uses a regular pentagon and a rotated partner (Haim-Kislev and Ostrover 2026, Theorem 1.3 and Proposition 1.4) <a id="pm-cite-17"></a>[[HKO2025](#ref-HKO2025)]; the pentagon is not centrally symmetric. The theorem here concerns the particular Lagrangian product of a symmetric body and its polar, and makes no assertion for arbitrary convex domains in phase space.

The planar coordinate is Gross’s uniform-distribution map in the conformal Skorokhod construction, rotated in source and target (Gross 2019, sec. 3.2) <a id="pm-cite-18"></a>[[Gross2019](#ref-Gross2019)]. The same lens and high powers of its inverse coordinate appear in the complete companion paper (OpenAI 2026b, sec. 3 and proof of Lemma 5.1) <a id="pm-cite-19"></a>[[SymmetricCompanion2026](#ref-SymmetricCompanion2026)]. Every lens estimate needed here, including the estimate uniform up to the tips, is proved locally. Neither the volume-product theorem nor the equality classification of the companion is used in the proof.

The holomorphic-zero argument belongs to the broader use of logarithmic poles to measure local positivity, as in Demailly (Demailly 1992, sec. 6) <a id="pm-cite-20"></a>[[Demailly1992](#ref-Demailly1992)]. Its replacement of a potential near the origin by a regularized maximum has a close analogue in Witt Nyström (Witt Nyström 2018, Proposition 3.3) <a id="pm-cite-21"></a>[[WittNystrom2018](#ref-WittNystrom2018)]. Those results concern projective Kähler geometry; here the replacement is proved directly for the specified form on a noncompact sublevel domain. Moser’s deformation method (Moser 1965, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-22"></a>[[Moser1965](#ref-Moser1965)], with compact support verified below, then transfers the ball to the original form. The radial coordinate change is a special case of the Kähler symplectic-coordinate formula of Loi and Zuddas (Loi and Zuddas 2008, proof of Theorem 1.1, equation (23)) <a id="pm-cite-23"></a>[[LoiZuddas2008](#ref-LoiZuddas2008)]; its pullback is also verified directly.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
#### The construction

The lower bound comes from comparing two scales. For a fixed body $K$ given by finitely many symmetric strips and a large integer $k$, a holomorphic construction produces balls of every capacity below $\pi k$ in a suitable symplectic domain. We realize that domain in $\operatorname{int}K\times S_k\operatorname{int}K^\circ$, where the momentum scale satisfies $S_k=(1+o(1))\pi k/4$ as $k\to\infty$. Dividing momentum by $S_k$ and compensating with a dilation of the source ball gives each prescribed capacity below $4$ once $k$ is sufficiently large. Two independent analytic ingredients supply these scales.

The first turns holomorphic vanishing into a ball (Theorem <a href="#thm:holomorphic-ball" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:holomorphic-ball">2</a>). Let $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_m)$ be holomorphic on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$ containing $0$, have the unique common zero $0$, and satisfy $|f(z)|=O(|z|^k)$ there. The domain $\{|f|^2<1\}$, equipped with a symplectic form constructed from the potential $|z|^2+|f(z)|^2$, contains balls of every capacity strictly below $\pi k$, provided the closed sublevels of $|f|^2$ below $1$ are compact in $\Omega$. The order of vanishing controls the slope of a logarithmic potential near zero. Replacing its singularity by a smooth radial potential exposes a ball, and a compactly supported Moser deformation transports that ball to the original form. Compact sublevels ensure the deformation is supported away from the boundary; no regularity of the level hypersurfaces is required.

The second ingredient controls the momentum scale (Proposition <a href="#prop:uniform-slice" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:uniform-slice">4</a>). A conformal map $F$ takes the unit disk $\mathbb D=\{w\in\mathbb C:|w|<1\}$ to a bounded domain $D$ with tips at $\pm i$ and centered horizontal slices at every height $-1<t<1$. Write $g=F^{-1}$. The horizontal primitives of the densities $|(g^k)'|^2$, taken from the imaginary axis, have absolute value at most $(\pi k/4)|g|^{2k}+o(k)$, uniformly on all of $D$. Uniformity near the tips is essential: the slice height may approach $1$ or $-1$ as $k$ increases. Section <a href="#sec:planar" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:planar">3</a> proves this estimate locally, including the moving-endpoint bound needed at those tips.

To combine the ingredients, write $K=\{x:|\langle b_j,x\rangle|\le1,\ 1\le j\le m\}$, where the real vectors $b_j$ span $\mathbb R^n$. Extend these pairings complex-linearly and set $f_j(z)=g(\langle b_j,z\rangle)^k$ on the domain where every $\langle b_j,z\rangle$ lies in $D$. These functions have the required unique common zero and compact sublevels. The phase-space realization sends the imaginary part of $z$ to position and uses the horizontal primitives for momentum. Their monotonicity makes the map globally injective, and their uniform estimate bounds the momentum in the polar body. Section <a href="#sec:realization" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:realization">4</a> carries out this construction. Finally, approximation in the polar body extends the lower bound to arbitrary $K$. A supporting-cylinder argument and nonsqueezing give the matching upper bound.

### Balls from holomorphic vanishing

The aim of this section is to turn the order of a holomorphic zero into the capacity of an embedded ball. We first fix the differential-form normalization, then smooth a logarithmic singularity and transport the resulting radial ball by Moser’s deformation method.

<a id="eq:ball-normalization"></a>
Identify $\mathbb C^n$ with $\mathbb R^{2n}$ by writing $z=u+ix$. For a smooth real function $\phi$, use the convention $$d^c\phi(X)=-\frac14\,d\phi(iX),
 \qquad dd^c\phi=d(d^c\phi).$$ In particular, the standard primitive and symplectic form are $$\lambda_0=d^c|z|^2
   =\frac12\sum_{j=1}^n(u_j\,dx_j-x_j\,du_j),
 \qquad
 \omega_0=dd^c|z|^2=\sum_{j=1}^n du_j\wedge dx_j.
 \label{eq:ball-normalization}$$ For every real tangent vector $X$, one has $$dd^c\phi(X,iX)
 =\frac14\left.\Delta_{\zeta}\phi(z+\zeta X)\right|_{\zeta=0},
 \qquad \zeta\in\mathbb C.$$ Thus a smooth function $\phi$ is plurisubharmonic precisely when these quantities are nonnegative. In that case $dd^c(|z|^2+\phi)$ is symplectic: it is closed and its value on $(X,iX)$ is at least $|X|^2$.

We will also use that a smooth convex, coordinatewise nondecreasing function of plurisubharmonic functions is plurisubharmonic. Indeed, on each complex line the ordinary chain rule gives $$\Delta\Gamma(h_1,\ldots,h_N)
 =\sum_{j=1}^N\Gamma_j\Delta h_j
   +\sum_{i,j=1}^N\Gamma_{ij}
        \langle \nabla h_i,\nabla h_j\rangle\ge0.$$ The first sum is nonnegative by monotonicity, and the second is nonnegative because the Hessian of $\Gamma$ is positive semidefinite.

<a id="stmt-thm-holomorphic-ball"></a>
<div id="thm:holomorphic-ball" class="theorem">

**Theorem 2** (A ball from a holomorphic zero). *Let $n,m\ge1$, let $\Omega\subset\mathbb C^n$ be an open set containing $0$, and let $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_m):\Omega\to\mathbb C^m$ be holomorphic. Suppose that, for some integer $k\ge1$,*

1.  *$f^{-1}(0)=\{0\}$ and $|f(z)|=O(|z|^k)$ as $z\to0$;*

2.  *for every $0<s<1$, the set $\{z\in\Omega:|f(z)|^2\le s\}$ is a compact subset of $\Omega$.*

*Set $$\tau=|f|^2,\qquad V=\{z\in\Omega:\tau(z)<1\},
 \qquad \omega=dd^c(|z|^2+\tau).$$ For every $0<c<\pi k$, there is a smooth symplectic embedding $$\bigl(B^{2n}(c),\omega_0\bigr)\longrightarrow(V,\omega).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-thm-holomorphic-ball"><summary>Proof of stmt-thm-holomorphic-ball</summary>

<a id="eq:ball-log-levi"></a>
*Proof.* *Step 1: a logarithmic potential with the required slope.* For a holomorphic tuple, differentiation along a complex line gives $$\begin{aligned}
 dd^c\tau(X,iX)&=|df(X)|^2,\notag\\
 dd^c\log\tau(X,iX)
 &=\frac{|f|^2|df(X)|^2
       -\left|\sum_{j=1}^m\overline{f_j}\,df_j(X)\right|^2}{|f|^4}
 \quad\text{on }\Omega\setminus\{0\}.
 \label{eq:ball-log-levi}
\end{aligned}$$ The second expression is nonnegative by Cauchy–Schwarz. Hence $\tau$ and, away from its zero, $\log\tau$ are plurisubharmonic.

Fix $0<a<k$; we will embed the ball of capacity $\pi a$. Choose $$\frac ak<b<1,\qquad a<\mu<bk.$$ Choose numbers $\log b<t_-<t_+<0$ and a smooth nondecreasing cutoff $\beta:\mathbb R\to[0,1]$ which is $0$ for $t\le t_-$ and $1$ for $t\ge t_+$. Define $\chi$ on $(-\infty,0)$ by $$\chi'(t)=b+\beta(t)(e^t-b),$$ choosing its additive constant so that $\chi(t)=e^t$ for $t\ge t_+$. This is possible since $\chi'=e^t$ there. Moreover, $\chi'>0$ and $$\chi''(t)=\beta'(t)(e^t-b)+\beta(t)e^t\ge0,$$ because the transition occurs where $e^t>b$. For $t\le t_-$, the function $\chi$ is affine with slope $b$.

<a id="eq:ball-singularity"></a>
It follows that $$H=\chi(\log\tau)$$ is smooth and plurisubharmonic on $V\setminus\{0\}$ and satisfies $H=\tau$ wherever $\tau\ge s_0:=e^{t_+}$. The vanishing assumption gives $\tau(z)\le C_0|z|^{2k}$ near zero. Since $\chi$ is eventually affine, there is a constant $C_1$ such that $$H(z)\le bk\log|z|^2+C_1
 \quad\text{for all sufficiently small }z\ne0.
 \label{eq:ball-singularity}$$

*Step 2: smooth the singularity without changing the form near the boundary.* The strict inequality $\mu<bk$ lets a smooth radial logarithm dominate $H$ close to zero while remaining below it on an outer sphere. Write $B_r=\{z\in\mathbb C^n:|z|<r\}$, and choose $r_2>0$ with $\overline{B}_{r_2}\subset V$. For $0<\delta\le1$, consider $$P_\delta(z)=\mu\log(|z|^2+\delta)-M.$$ For each fixed $r>0$, the radial calculation in Step 4 identifies $(B_r,dd^c(|z|^2+P_\delta))$ with a standard ball whose squared radius is $r^2+\mu r^2/(r^2+\delta)$. Since $\mu>a$, this exceeds $a$ for all sufficiently small $\delta$. We will therefore arrange that the modified potential agrees with $P_\delta$ on a ball whose radius is fixed before $\delta$ is chosen.

<a id="eq:ball-inner-domination"></a>
<a id="eq:ball-delta-choice"></a>
The functions $P_\delta$ are plurisubharmonic by Equation <a href="#eq:ball-log-levi" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:ball-log-levi">[eq:ball-log-levi]</a>, applied to the nowhere-zero holomorphic tuple $(\sqrt\delta,z_1,\ldots,z_n)$. Choose $M$ so that $$M>\mu\log(r_2^2+1)-\min_{|z|=r_2}H(z)+2.$$ The minimum is finite because $f$ has no zero on this sphere. Then $P_\delta<H-2$ on $|z|=r_2$ for every $0<\delta\le1$. On the other hand, Equation <a href="#eq:ball-singularity" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:ball-singularity">[eq:ball-singularity]</a> gives $$P_\delta(z)-H(z)
 \ge(\mu-bk)\log|z|^2-M-C_1.$$ As $z\to0$, this lower bound tends to $+\infty$, uniformly in $\delta$. We may therefore fix $0<r_1<r_2$, independently of $\delta$, such that $$P_\delta>H+2\quad\text{on }0<|z|\le r_1
 \quad\text{for every }0<\delta\le1.
 \label{eq:ball-inner-domination}$$ Fix from now on one positive $\delta$ satisfying $$0<\delta<\min\left\{1,\ r_1^2\left(\frac\mu a-1\right)\right\}.
 \label{eq:ball-delta-choice}$$ The upper bound is positive because $\mu>a$.

We next replace the singularity of $H$ by $P_\delta$ using a regularized maximum, the standard gluing device in Richberg’s smoothing method; see (Harvey, Lawson, and Pliś 2020, sec. 2) <a id="pm-cite-24"></a>[[HarveyLawsonPlis2020](#ref-HarveyLawsonPlis2020)]. A related replacement of Kähler potentials appears in (Witt Nyström 2018, Proposition 3.3) <a id="pm-cite-25"></a>[[WittNystrom2018](#ref-WittNystrom2018)] in a projective setting. We give the elementary two-function construction explicitly. Let $\vartheta\in C_c^\infty((-1,1))$ be nonnegative and even, with $\int_{\mathbb R}\vartheta=1$, and set $$\rho(s)=\int_{\mathbb R}|s-t|\vartheta(t)\,dt,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{rmax}(h,p)=\frac{h+p+\rho(h-p)}2.$$ The function $\rho$ is smooth and convex, with $|\rho'|\le1$, and equals $|s|$ for $|s|\ge1$. Thus $\operatorname{rmax}$ is smooth, convex, nondecreasing in both arguments, and equals $\max(h,p)$ when $|h-p|\ge1$. Define $\widetilde H$ to be $\operatorname{rmax}(H,P_\delta)$ on $B_{r_2}\setminus\{0\}$, to be $H$ outside $B_{r_2}$, and to take the value $P_\delta(0)$ at zero. The outer comparison gives equality with $H$ in a neighborhood of $\partial B_{r_2}$, and Equation <a href="#eq:ball-inner-domination" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:ball-inner-domination">[eq:ball-inner-domination]</a> gives equality with $P_\delta$ on $B_{r_1}$. Consequently $\widetilde H$ is smooth and plurisubharmonic on all of $V$.

The difference $\widetilde H-\tau$ has compact support in $V$. Indeed, set $s_2=\max_{\overline B_{r_2}}\tau<1$, choose $\max(s_0,s_2)<\sigma<1$, and put <a id="eq:ball-support"></a>$$C=\{z\in\Omega:\tau(z)\le\sigma\}.
 \label{eq:ball-support}$$ This is a compact subset of $V$ by hypothesis. Outside $C$ a point lies outside $\overline B_{r_2}$ and has $H=\tau$, so that $\widetilde H-\tau$ vanishes there.

*Step 3: transport the smoothed form.* We now use Moser’s deformation argument (Moser 1965, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-26"></a>[[Moser1965](#ref-Moser1965)]. The support estimate above supplies a complete flow even though $V$ need not be compact. Consider the symplectic forms $$\omega_s=dd^c\bigl(|z|^2+(1-s)\tau+s\widetilde H\bigr),
 \qquad 0\le s\le1,$$ and write $\widetilde\omega=\omega_1$. Each form is symplectic because the two non-Euclidean potentials are plurisubharmonic. Define the smooth time-dependent vector field $Y_s$ by $$\iota_{Y_s}\omega_s=-\gamma,
 \qquad \gamma=d^c(\widetilde H-\tau).$$ Both $\gamma$ and every $Y_s$ are supported in the fixed compact set $C$. Extending $Y_s$ by zero outside $V$ gives a smooth vector field on $\mathbb C^n$, with its coefficients and first derivatives bounded uniformly for $s\in[0,1]$. The ordinary differential equation for this field has a flow $\varphi_s$ throughout this time interval. The ambient flow fixes $\mathbb C^n\setminus V$ pointwise and hence restricts to diffeomorphisms of $V$. This argument applies even if $V$ is disconnected and places no regularity assumption on its boundary.

<a id="eq:ball-moser"></a>
Since $d\omega_s=0$, differentiation of pullbacks gives $$\frac{d}{ds}\varphi_s^*\omega_s
 =\varphi_s^*\bigl(d\gamma+d\iota_{Y_s}\omega_s\bigr)=0.$$ Therefore $$\varphi_1^*\widetilde\omega=\omega,
 \qquad
 \varphi_1^{-1}:(V,\widetilde\omega)\longrightarrow(V,\omega)
 \text{ is symplectic}.
 \label{eq:ball-moser}$$

*Step 4: read off the ball in the radial model.* The deformation has reduced the problem to an explicit radial form. On $B_{r_1}$ the new potential, up to the additive constant $-M$, is $\Psi(|z|^2)$, where $$\Psi(t)=t+\mu\log(t+\delta).$$ Define the radial map $$R(z)=\sqrt{\Psi'(|z|^2)}\,z
     =\sqrt{1+\frac\mu{|z|^2+\delta}}\,z.$$ This is the radial case of the symplectic-coordinate construction in (Loi and Zuddas 2008, proof of Theorem 1.1, Equation (23)) <a id="pm-cite-27"></a>[[LoiZuddas2008](#ref-LoiZuddas2008)]. In the pullback of $\lambda_0$ from Equation <a href="#eq:ball-normalization" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:ball-normalization">[eq:ball-normalization]</a>, the terms differentiating the radial factor cancel. Hence $$R^*\lambda_0=\Psi'(|z|^2)\lambda_0=d^c\Psi(|z|^2),
 \qquad R^*\omega_0=\widetilde\omega\big|_{B_{r_1}}.$$ The squared radial coordinate is strictly increasing, because $$\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(t\Psi'(t)\bigr)
 =\frac{d}{dt}\left(t+\frac{\mu t}{t+\delta}\right)
 =1+\frac{\mu\delta}{(t+\delta)^2}>0.$$ At zero the map is smooth and has derivative $DR(0)=\sqrt{1+\mu/\delta}\,I$, so its inverse is smooth there as well. Thus $R$ is a diffeomorphism from $B_{r_1}$ onto the standard ball $B^{2n}(\pi Q_\delta)$, where $$Q_\delta=r_1^2+\frac{\mu r_1^2}{r_1^2+\delta}>a$$ by Equation <a href="#eq:ball-delta-choice" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:ball-delta-choice">[eq:ball-delta-choice]</a>. If $\iota:B_{r_1}\hookrightarrow V$ denotes inclusion, the map $$\varphi_1^{-1}\circ\iota\circ
       R^{-1}\big|_{B^{2n}(\pi a)}:
 B^{2n}(\pi a)\longrightarrow V$$ is a smooth embedding, and its pullback of $\omega$ is $\omega_0$ by Equation <a href="#eq:ball-moser" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:ball-moser">[eq:ball-moser]</a> and the radial pullback identity. Since $a$ was arbitrary in $(0,k)$, the theorem follows. ◻

</details>

### A planar coordinate and a uniform slice estimate

We use Gross’s conformal map $\psi$ for the uniform distribution (Gross 2019, sec. 3.2) <a id="pm-cite-28"></a>[[Gross2019](#ref-Gross2019)], with the rotation $F(w)=i\psi(iw)$. There is no scaling. The same lens appears in (OpenAI 2026b, sec. 3) <a id="pm-cite-29"></a>[[SymmetricCompanion2026](#ref-SymmetricCompanion2026)]; its half-width $\lambda(t)$ is the function $V(t)$ used here. We establish its needed properties directly and then prove a uniform slice estimate that controls the momentum coordinates in the polar product.

<a id="stmt-lem-planar-map"></a>
<div id="lem:planar-map" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:planar-F"></a>
<a id="eq:horizontal-slices"></a>
<a id="eq:planar-T"></a>
**Lemma 3**. *The holomorphic function $$\label{eq:planar-F}
 F(w)=\frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
       \frac{(-1)^\ell w^{2\ell+1}}{(2\ell+1)^2},
 \qquad w\in\mathbb D,$$ is a biholomorphism from $\mathbb D$ onto a bounded domain $D$. It is odd, commutes with conjugation, and satisfies $F(0)=0$. The imaginary coordinates of $D$ range over $(-1,1)$, and for each $t\in(-1,1)$ there is a finite $V(t)>0$ such that $$\label{eq:horizontal-slices}
 \{v\in\mathbb R:v+it\in D\}=(-V(t),V(t)).$$ Write $F(ir)=iT(r)$ for $0\le r<1$. The function $T$ increases strictly from $0$ to $1$, with $$\label{eq:planar-T}
 T'(r)=\frac{8}{\pi^2}\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}r}{r}
 \quad(0<r<1),
 \qquad \lim_{r\uparrow1}T'(r)=+\infty.$$ For fixed $t\in(-1,1)$, put $r_0=T^{-1}(|t|)$. The positive horizontal slice is parametrized by $$w(r,t)=re^{i\theta(r,t)},\qquad
 F(w(r,t))=v(r,t)+it,\qquad r_0<r<1,$$ where $-\pi/2<\theta(r,t)<\pi/2$ and $v(r,t)$ increases strictly from $0$ to $V(t)$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-planar-map"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-planar-map</summary>

<a id="eq:planar-arctan"></a>
*Proof.* *The map and its angular derivative.* The series in Equation <a href="#eq:planar-F" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-F">[eq:planar-F]</a> is absolutely summable on the closed disk. Hence $F$ is bounded, odd, and commutes with conjugation. Termwise differentiation in the open disk gives $$\label{eq:planar-arctan}
 wF'(w)=\frac{8}{\pi^2}\arctan w,
 \qquad
 \arctan w=\frac{1}{2i}\log\frac{1+iw}{1-iw}.$$ Here the logarithm is the branch on the right half-plane that is real on the positive real axis. Indeed, $$\Re\frac{1+iw}{1-iw}
   =\frac{1-|w|^2}{|1-iw|^2}>0
 \qquad (w\in\mathbb D).$$ This also shows that $\arctan w=0$ only at $w=0$. Thus $F'$ has no zeros away from the origin, and the series gives $F'(0)=8/\pi^2\ne0$.

<a id="eq:planar-polar-derivatives"></a>
For $w=re^{i\theta}$, define <a id="eq:planar-A"></a>$$\label{eq:planar-A}
 A(r,\theta)=\Re\bigl(wF'(w)\bigr)
   =\frac{4}{\pi^2}
       \arctan\frac{2r\cos\theta}{1-r^2}.$$ To obtain the last identity, the imaginary part of the fraction in Equation <a href="#eq:planar-arctan" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-arctan">[eq:planar-arctan]</a>, divided by its real part, is $2r\cos\theta/(1-r^2)$, and its argument lies in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. On the right semicircle, $0<A(r,\theta)<2/\pi$. The identities $$\label{eq:planar-polar-derivatives}
 \partial_r F(re^{i\theta})=\frac{wF'(w)}{r},
 \qquad
 \partial_\theta F(re^{i\theta})=iwF'(w)$$ therefore show that $\Re F(w)>0$ in the right half-disk: its radial derivative is positive, and its value tends to zero at the origin. They also show that $\Im F(re^{i\theta})$ increases strictly with $\theta$ on each right semicircle.

Oddness and conjugation symmetry give $F(ir)=iT(r)$ with real $T$. Differentiating Equation <a href="#eq:planar-arctan" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-arctan">[eq:planar-arctan]</a> on the imaginary axis gives Equation <a href="#eq:planar-T" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-T">[eq:planar-T]</a>. In particular, $T$ is strictly increasing, $T(0)=0$, and $T'(r)$ diverges as $r\uparrow1$. Since $F(r)$ is real, Equation <a href="#eq:planar-polar-derivatives" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-polar-derivatives">[eq:planar-polar-derivatives]</a> also gives $$T(r)=\int_0^{\pi/2}A(r,\theta)\,d\theta.$$ The integrand is bounded by $2/\pi$ and tends to $2/\pi$ at every $\theta\in[0,\pi/2)$ as $r\uparrow1$. Dominated convergence therefore gives $T(r)\to1$.

<a id="eq:horizontal-radius-derivative"></a>
*Horizontal slices and global invertibility.* Fix $|t|<1$ and let $T(r_0)=|t|$. For every $r\in(r_0,1)$, the strictly increasing imaginary coordinate on the right semicircle ranges from $-T(r)$ to $T(r)$. There is consequently exactly one angle $\theta(r,t)$ there with imaginary coordinate $t$. It depends smoothly on $r$, since its angular derivative is $A>0$. Writing $wF'(w)=A+iB$, implicit differentiation at fixed $t$ gives $$\partial_r\theta=-\frac{B}{rA},
 \qquad
 \partial_r v=\frac{A}{r}-B\,\partial_r\theta
             =\frac{A^2+B^2}{rA}.$$ Thus $$\label{eq:horizontal-radius-derivative}
 \frac{\partial v}{\partial r}(r,t)
   =\frac{|wF'(w)|^2}{rA(r,\theta(r,t))}>0.$$ As $r\downarrow r_0>0$, the angle tends to the appropriate imaginary-axis endpoint of the semicircle, so $v(r,t)\to0$. If $r_0=0$, the same limit follows from $F(0)=0$. Boundedness of $F$ now shows that $v(r,t)$ maps $(r_0,1)$ continuously and strictly increasingly onto an interval $(0,V(t))$, where $0<V(t)<\infty$.

This parametrization proves injectivity on each positive horizontal slice. The reflection identity $F(-\overline w)=-\overline{F(w)}$ gives the negative slices, and the strict monotonicity of $T$ gives injectivity on the imaginary axis. The right half-disk, imaginary axis, and left half-disk have images with positive, zero, and negative real parts, respectively. Hence $F$ is globally injective. Every point of its image has imaginary coordinate in $(-1,1)$, and every such coordinate occurs on the imaginary axis. This proves Equation <a href="#eq:horizontal-slices" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:horizontal-slices">[eq:horizontal-slices]</a>. Since $F'\ne0$, the local holomorphic inverses combine into a holomorphic inverse $g:D\to\mathbb D$. ◻

</details>

<a id="eq:slice-primitive"></a>
For an integer $k\ge2$, define the signed slice integral $$\label{eq:slice-primitive}
 J_k(v,t)=k^2\int_0^v
       |g(h+it)|^{2k-2}|g'(h+it)|^2\,dh,
 \qquad v+it\in D.$$ The segment of integration lies in $D$ by Lemma <a href="#lem:planar-map" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:planar-map">3</a>.

<a id="stmt-prop-uniform-slice"></a>
<div id="prop:uniform-slice" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:slice-derivative"></a>
<a id="eq:uniform-slice"></a>
**Proposition 4**. *Each $J_k$ is smooth on $D$, is odd in $v$, and satisfies $$\label{eq:slice-derivative}
 \partial_v J_k(v,t)
    =k^2|g(v+it)|^{2k-2}|g'(v+it)|^2\ge0.$$ There is a sequence $\epsilon_k\ge0$, depending only on $F$, with $\epsilon_k\to0$ such that, simultaneously for every $v+it\in D$, $$\label{eq:uniform-slice}
 \frac{|J_k(v,t)|}{k}
    \le\frac{\pi}{4}|g(v+it)|^{2k}+\epsilon_k.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-uniform-slice"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-uniform-slice</summary>

*Proof.* *Step 1: express the slice integral in the disk radius.* For any fixed $v+it\in D$, the horizontal segment from $it$ to $v+it$ is a compact subset of the open set $D$; the same is true for all sufficiently nearby endpoints and heights. The formula $$J_k(v,t)=k^2v\int_0^1
       |g(av+it)|^{2k-2}|g'(av+it)|^2\,da$$ then proves smoothness by differentiation under the integral. The integrand is smooth even at the zero of $g$, since $|g|^{2k-2}=(|g|^2)^{k-1}$ and $k$ is an integer. Equation <a href="#eq:slice-derivative" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-derivative">[eq:slice-derivative]</a> follows from the fundamental theorem of calculus. The symmetry $g(-\overline\zeta)=-\overline{g(\zeta)}$ makes the density in Equation <a href="#eq:slice-primitive" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-primitive">[eq:slice-primitive]</a> even in its real coordinate. Thus $J_k(-v,t)=-J_k(v,t)$, and it suffices to estimate $v>0$.

<a id="eq:slice-radial-integral"></a>
Fix such a point and put $$R=|g(v+it)|,\qquad r_0=T^{-1}(|t|).$$ Along its positive horizontal slice, the radius increases from $r_0$ to $R$. Since $g'(F(w))=1/F'(w)$, Equation <a href="#eq:horizontal-radius-derivative" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:horizontal-radius-derivative">[eq:horizontal-radius-derivative]</a> gives $$\label{eq:slice-radial-integral}
 \frac{J_k(v,t)}{k}
    =k\int_{r_0}^{R}
       \frac{r^{2k-1}}{A(r,\theta(r,t))}\,dr.$$ Indeed, $|g'|^2\,dv=(r/A)\,dr$. At the lower endpoint this is understood as an improper integral: perform the change of variables first on a segment beginning at a positive real coordinate, and then let that coordinate decrease to zero. The original integrand is smooth on the full compact segment, so its integral is finite; the nonnegative transformed integrals converge to the same value. This argument also covers $t=0$, when $r_0=0$.

The leading constant comes from splitting $1/A$ into $\pi/2$ and the nonnegative excess $1/A-\pi/2$, since $0<A<2/\pi$. The constant part in Equation <a href="#eq:slice-radial-integral" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-radial-integral">[eq:slice-radial-integral]</a> contributes $\frac{\pi}{4}(R^{2k}-r_0^{2k})$. It therefore suffices to bound the remaining weighted integral uniformly over all heights and endpoints by a quantity tending to zero as $k\to\infty$.

<a id="eq:reciprocal-arctan"></a>
<a id="eq:A-angular-bound"></a>
<a id="eq:A-slice-bound"></a>
*Step 2: estimate the loss near the two tips.* We next control the possible small denominator near the ends of the right semicircle. Suppose $1/2\le r<1$ and set $$s=\frac{\pi}{2}-|\theta|\in(0,\pi/2],
 \qquad
 X=\frac{2r\sin s}{1-r^2}.$$ For every $X>0$, $$\label{eq:reciprocal-arctan}
 \frac{1}{\arctan X}-\frac{2}{\pi}\le\frac{2}{X}.$$ For $X\le1$, this follows from $\arctan X\ge X/2$; for $X\ge1$, use $\pi/2-\arctan X=\arctan(1/X)\le1/X$ and $\arctan X\ge\pi/4$. Combining Equation <a href="#eq:reciprocal-arctan" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:reciprocal-arctan">[eq:reciprocal-arctan]</a> with $\sin s\ge2s/\pi$ and $(1+r)/(2r)\le3/2$, we obtain $$\label{eq:A-angular-bound}
 \frac1{A(r,\theta)}
    \le\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{3\pi^3}{8}\frac{1-r}{s}.$$ On the other hand, the vertical gap to the semicircle endpoint is <a id="eq:vertical-gap"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
 \Delta(r,\theta)
   &:=T(r)-|\Im F(re^{i\theta})|
     =\int_0^s\frac{4}{\pi^2}
          \arctan\frac{2r\sin\xi}{1-r^2}\,d\xi \notag\\
   &\le\frac{4r}{\pi^2(1-r^2)}s^2
     \le\frac{2}{\pi^2}\frac{s^2}{1-r}.
 \label{eq:vertical-gap}
\end{aligned}$$ Here we used $\arctan y\le y$ and $\sin\xi\le\xi$. The last inequality supplies the lower bound $s\ge(\pi/\sqrt2)\sqrt{\Delta(r,\theta)(1-r)}$. Consequently, with the absolute constant $C_0=3\sqrt2\pi^2/8$, Equation <a href="#eq:A-angular-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:A-angular-bound">[eq:A-angular-bound]</a> implies $$\label{eq:A-slice-bound}
 \frac1{A(r,\theta(r,t))}
   \le\frac{\pi}{2}
        +C_0\sqrt{\frac{1-r}{T(r)-|t|}}
 \qquad (r\ge1/2,\ r>r_0).$$

<a id="eq:slice-inner-bound"></a>
*Step 3: separate a compact part from the tip region.* Fix a radius $r_*\in(1/2,1)$. The part of the original horizontal integral for which $|g|\le r_*$ is uniformly bounded, after division by $k$, by $$\label{eq:slice-inner-bound}
 C_*k r_*^{2k-2},
 \qquad
 C_*:=\operatorname{diam}(D)
          \max_{|w|\le r_*}|F'(w)|^{-2}<\infty.$$ To see this, the radius is strictly increasing along the positive slice, so the relevant portion is exactly its initial segment ending at radius $\min(R,r_*)$, when $r_0\le r_*$, and is empty otherwise. Every point of this segment lies in $F(\{|w|\le r_*\})$, and its horizontal length is at most $\operatorname{diam}(D)$. This includes the case $R\le r_*$, when it is the whole integral.

<a id="eq:slice-outer-bound"></a>
If an outer portion remains, put $$r_a=\max(r_0,r_*),\qquad
 L_*:=\inf_{r_*\le r<1}T'(r)>0.$$ Since $T(r_a)\ge T(r_0)=|t|$, for $r>r_a$ we have <a id="eq:gap-tail-bound"></a>$$\label{eq:gap-tail-bound}
 T(r)-|t|\ge T(r)-T(r_a)\ge L_*(r-r_a).$$ Equations <a href="#eq:slice-radial-integral" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-radial-integral">[eq:slice-radial-integral]</a> and <a href="#eq:A-slice-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:A-slice-bound">[eq:A-slice-bound]</a> therefore bound the outer contribution by $$\label{eq:slice-outer-bound}
 \frac{\pi}{4}\bigl(R^{2k}-r_a^{2k}\bigr)
  +\frac{C_0}{\sqrt{L_*}}\,
       k\int_{r_a}^{1}r^{2k-1}
                 \sqrt{\frac{1-r}{r-r_a}}\,dr.$$ The extension of the error integral from $R$ to $1$ uses its nonnegativity and removes any dependence on the distance $R-r_a$.

<a id="eq:uniform-endpoint-integral"></a>
The last integral has an absolute bound, including when its lower endpoint depends on $k$. Write $$I(k,r_a)=k\int_{r_a}^{1}r^{2k-1}
                    \sqrt{\frac{1-r}{r-r_a}}\,dr,
 \qquad H=k(1-r_a).$$ The substitution $y=k(1-r)$ gives $$\begin{aligned}
 I(k,r_a)
   &=\int_0^H(1-y/k)^{2k-1}\sqrt{\frac{y}{H-y}}\,dy\\
   &\le\int_0^H e^{-y}\sqrt{\frac{y}{H-y}}\,dy,
\end{aligned}$$ since $\log r\le-(1-r)$ and $2k-1\ge k$. On $[0,H/2]$ the square-root factor is at most one. On $[H/2,H]$, bound the exponential by $e^{-H/2}$ and use $$\int_0^H\sqrt{\frac{y}{H-y}}\,dy=\frac{\pi H}{2}.$$ It follows that $$\label{eq:uniform-endpoint-integral}
 I(k,r_a)\le 1+\frac{\pi H}{2}e^{-H/2}
           \le 1+\frac{\pi}{e}.$$ The same integral without its exponential factor also gives $I(k,r_a)\le\pi H/2$. These estimates hold for every $H>0$, covering both $H\downarrow0$ and $H\to\infty$.

<a id="eq:slice-uniform-error-bound"></a>
Combining Equations <a href="#eq:slice-inner-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-inner-bound">[eq:slice-inner-bound]</a>, <a href="#eq:slice-outer-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-outer-bound">[eq:slice-outer-bound]</a>, and <a href="#eq:uniform-endpoint-integral" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:uniform-endpoint-integral">[eq:uniform-endpoint-integral]</a>, and using symmetry when $v<0$, we have, for every $v+it\in D$, $$\label{eq:slice-uniform-error-bound}
 \frac{|J_k(v,t)|}{k}-\frac{\pi}{4}|g(v+it)|^{2k}
   \le C_*k r_*^{2k-2}
          +\frac{C_0(1+\pi/e)}{\sqrt{L_*}}.$$ When there is no outer portion, the first term alone bounds the excess, so the same displayed inequality remains valid. It is also valid at $v=0$.

*Step 4: make the error uniform.* The cutoff radius will be chosen before the power $k$, so the estimates above allow the slice height and both radial endpoints to move with $k$. Define explicitly <a id="eq:slice-error-sequence"></a>$$\label{eq:slice-error-sequence}
 \epsilon_k=
 \max\left\{0,\ \sup_{v+it\in D}
     \left(\frac{|J_k(v,t)|}{k}
                  -\frac{\pi}{4}|g(v+it)|^{2k}\right)\right\}.$$ Equation <a href="#eq:slice-uniform-error-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-uniform-error-bound">[eq:slice-uniform-error-bound]</a> makes this supremum finite for each $k$. For fixed $r_*$, its first error term tends to zero as $k\to\infty$. Moreover, Equation <a href="#eq:planar-T" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:planar-T">[eq:planar-T]</a> gives $$L_*\ge\frac{8}{\pi^2}\operatorname{arctanh}r_*
       \longrightarrow\infty
 \qquad\text{as }r_*\uparrow1.$$ Given $\varepsilon>0$, first choose $r_*$ so that the second term in Equation <a href="#eq:slice-uniform-error-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-uniform-error-bound">[eq:slice-uniform-error-bound]</a> is less than $\varepsilon/2$. For this fixed $r_*$, choose $N$ such that $C_*k r_*^{2k-2}<\varepsilon/2$ for every $k\ge N$. The resulting bound $\epsilon_k<\varepsilon$ holds simultaneously on all of $D$. In particular it allows the height $t$, the initial radius $r_0$, and the final radius $R$ all to vary with $k$, with no separation from either endpoint. This proves $\epsilon_k\to0$ and Equation <a href="#eq:uniform-slice" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:uniform-slice">[eq:uniform-slice]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:realization"></a>
### Realization in a polar product

We now combine the ball construction with the uniform slice estimate. Writing $z=u+ix$, the imaginary coordinates $x$ will give the position in a convex body. The signed slice integrals will correct the standard momentum $-u$ to account for the holomorphic part of the symplectic form.

<a id="stmt-prop-finite-strips"></a>
<div id="prop:finite-strips" class="proposition">

**Proposition 5**. *Let $n\ge2$ be an integer, let $b_1,\ldots,b_m\in\mathbb R^n$ span $\mathbb R^n$, and set $$K=\{x\in\mathbb R^n: |b_j\cdot x|\le 1,
                         \quad 1\le j\le m\}.$$ For every $0<c<4$ there is a smooth symplectic embedding $$B^{2n}(c)\longrightarrow
 \operatorname{int}(K)\times\operatorname{int}(K^\circ).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-finite-strips"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-finite-strips</summary>

*Proof.* *Step 1: encode the strips by holomorphic functions.* The body $K$ and its finite list of defining vectors are fixed throughout the proof. Extend each real functional $b_j\cdot x$ complex-linearly to $\ell_j(z)=b_j\cdot z$. Let $F:\mathbb D\to D$ and $g=F^{-1}$ be the maps of Lemma <a href="#lem:planar-map" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:planar-map">3</a>, and define <a id="eq:strip-domain"></a>$$\label{eq:strip-domain}
 \Omega=\{z\in\mathbb C^n:\ell_j(z)\in D\text{ for every }j\}.$$ This is an open neighborhood of the origin. The spanning condition makes the linear map $z\mapsto(\ell_1(z),\ldots,\ell_m(z))$ injective, so there is a constant $C_b$ such that $$|z|\le C_b\max_j|\ell_j(z)|.$$ Since $D$ is bounded, $\Omega$ is bounded as well; neither set depends on the integer $k$ chosen below. The same linear bound makes $K$ compact, and a sufficiently small Euclidean ball lies in all its defining strips. In particular, $0\in\operatorname{int}(K)$.

For each integer $k\ge2$, put $$f_j(z)=g(\ell_j(z))^k,\qquad
 \tau(z)=\sum_{j=1}^m|f_j(z)|^2,\qquad
 V=\{z\in\Omega:\tau(z)<1\},\qquad
 \omega=dd^c\bigl(|z|^2+\tau\bigr).$$ The tuple $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_m)$ is holomorphic on $\Omega$. Since $g$ has its only zero at the origin, the common zero set of $f$ is $$\{z\in\Omega:\ell_j(z)=0\text{ for every }j\}=\{0\}.$$ Holomorphicity at the origin gives $|f(z)|=O(|z|^k)$. To verify the compactness hypothesis of Theorem <a href="#thm:holomorphic-ball" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:holomorphic-ball">2</a>, fix $0<s<1$. On $\{\tau\le s\}$, for every $j$ we have $$\ell_j(z)\in
 F\bigl(\{w\in\mathbb C:|w|\le s^{1/(2k)}\}\bigr),$$ a compact subset of $D$. The linear bound above makes this sublevel bounded. Moreover, the limit of any convergent sequence in it still has all its images under the functionals $\ell_j$ in that compact subset of $D$, hence belongs to $\Omega$ and satisfies $\tau\le s$ by continuity. Thus the sublevel is compact in $\Omega$. Theorem <a href="#thm:holomorphic-ball" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:holomorphic-ball">2</a> now supplies symplectic embeddings into $(V,\omega)$ of every standard ball of capacity less than $\pi k$.

*Step 2: realize the form by a globally injective map.* Write $z=u+ix$ and use the signed integrals $J_k$ of Proposition <a href="#prop:uniform-slice" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:uniform-slice">4</a> to define <a id="eq:strip-phase-map"></a>$$\label{eq:strip-phase-map}
 \Phi_k:V\longrightarrow\mathbb R^n_q\times\mathbb R^n_p,
 \qquad
 q=x,\qquad
 p=-u-\sum_{j=1}^m
       J_k(b_j\cdot u,b_j\cdot x)b_j.$$ The signed integrals $J_k$ are smooth on $D$ by Proposition <a href="#prop:uniform-slice" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:uniform-slice">4</a>, so $\Phi_k$ is smooth.

<a id="eq:strip-pullback"></a>
Set $v_j=b_j\cdot u$ and $t_j=b_j\cdot x$. Pulling back the target form gives $$\begin{aligned}
 \Phi_k^*\omega_0
 &=\sum_{\ell=1}^n dx_\ell\wedge(-du_\ell)
       -\sum_{j=1}^m dt_j\wedge dJ_k(v_j,t_j)\notag\\
 &=\sum_{\ell=1}^n du_\ell\wedge dx_\ell
       +\sum_{j=1}^m
          \partial_vJ_k(v_j,t_j)\,dv_j\wedge dt_j\notag\\
 &=dd^c\bigl(|z|^2+\tau\bigr)=\omega.
 \label{eq:strip-pullback}
\end{aligned}$$ Indeed, the terms containing $\partial_tJ_k$ vanish because $dt_j\wedge dt_j=0$, while $$\partial_vJ_k(v,t)=k^2|g(v+it)|^{2k-2}|g'(v+it)|^2.$$ This is precisely the coefficient of $dd^c|g(v+it)^k|^2$ in front of $dv\wedge dt$, with the convention for $d^c$ fixed above.

The map $\Phi_k$ is globally injective. To see this, equality of its $q$ coordinates first fixes $x$. For two possible real coordinates $u_1,u_2$ at that $x$, write $$\Delta u=u_2-u_1,\qquad
 v_{ji}=b_j\cdot u_i,\qquad t_j=b_j\cdot x,$$ and denote the corresponding momenta by $p_1,p_2$. Both $v_{j1}$ and $v_{j2}$ belong to the same horizontal interval of $D$ at height $t_j$. Since $\partial_vJ_k\ge0$ on this entire interval, $$\begin{aligned}
 (p_2-p_1)\cdot\Delta u
 &=-|\Delta u|^2
   -\sum_{j=1}^m
     \bigl(J_k(v_{j2},t_j)-J_k(v_{j1},t_j)\bigr)
     (v_{j2}-v_{j1})\\
 &\le -|\Delta u|^2.
\end{aligned}$$ Thus equal momenta force $u_1=u_2$. This comparison applies to any two points of the fiber, even if that fiber of $V$ is disconnected. Equation <a href="#eq:strip-pullback" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:strip-pullback">[eq:strip-pullback]</a> and nondegeneracy of $\omega$ show that $\Phi_k$ is a local diffeomorphism. It is therefore open, and its injectivity gives a smooth inverse onto its image. Consequently $\Phi_k$ is a symplectic embedding.

<a id="eq:strip-support"></a>
<a id="eq:strip-scaled-image"></a>
*Step 3: control the momentum by the polar body.* We next bound its image. All imaginary parts of points of $D$ lie in $(-1,1)$, so $|b_j\cdot x|<1$ for every $j$. The finite system of strict inequalities implies $q=x\in\operatorname{int}(K)$. Introduce the support function $$h_K(p)=\max_{y\in K}\langle p,y\rangle.$$ It satisfies $h_K(\pm b_j)\le1$, and $\operatorname{int}(K^\circ)=\{p:h_K(p)<1\}$. Because $\Omega$ is bounded, there is a finite constant $C_K$, independent of $k$, such that $h_K(-u)\le C_K$ on $\Omega$. Subadditivity and positive homogeneity of $h_K$, followed by Proposition <a href="#prop:uniform-slice" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:uniform-slice">4</a>, give $$\begin{aligned}
 \frac{h_K(p)}{k}
 &\le \frac{C_K}{k}
       +\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{|J_k(v_j,t_j)|}{k}\notag\\
 &\le \frac{C_K}{k}
       +\frac{\pi}{4}\sum_{j=1}^m|g(\ell_j(z))|^{2k}
       +m\epsilon_k
  =\frac{C_K}{k}+\frac{\pi}{4}\tau(z)+m\epsilon_k.
 \label{eq:strip-support}
\end{aligned}$$ Here $\epsilon_k\ge0$ tends to zero, uniformly in the planar argument. The number $m$ and the constant $C_K$ are fixed before $k$ varies. It follows that, for any $\eta>0$ and all sufficiently large $k$, $$\frac{C_K}{k}+m\epsilon_k<\frac{\eta\pi}{4}.$$ Since $\tau<1$ on $V$, Equation <a href="#eq:strip-support" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:strip-support">[eq:strip-support]</a> then gives the strict inclusion $$\label{eq:strip-scaled-image}
 \Phi_k(V)\subset
 \operatorname{int}(K)\times S\operatorname{int}(K^\circ),
 \qquad S=(1+\eta)\frac{\pi k}{4}.$$

*Step 4: rescale the ball and the momentum together.* Finally, fix $0<c<4$. Choose $\eta>0$ with $(1+\eta)c<4$, and then fix an integer $k$ large enough for Equation <a href="#eq:strip-scaled-image" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:strip-scaled-image">[eq:strip-scaled-image]</a>. The resulting constant $S$ satisfies $Sc<\pi k$, so there is a symplectic embedding $$E:B^{2n}(Sc)\longrightarrow(V,\omega).$$ Let $D_{\sqrt S}(z)=\sqrt S\,z$ and $L_S(q,p)=(q,p/S)$. The composition <a id="eq:strip-rescaling"></a>$$\label{eq:strip-rescaling}
 L_S\circ\Phi_k\circ E\circ D_{\sqrt S}:
 B^{2n}(c)\longrightarrow
 \operatorname{int}(K)\times\operatorname{int}(K^\circ)$$ is a smooth embedding. Since $D_{\sqrt S}$ maps $B^{2n}(c)$ onto $B^{2n}(Sc)$ and $$D_{\sqrt S}^*\omega_0=S\omega_0,
 \qquad L_S^*\omega_0=S^{-1}\omega_0,$$ its pullback of $\omega_0$ is exactly $\omega_0$. This proves the proposition. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:approximation"></a>
### Arbitrary convex bodies and consequences

The passage from finite strip bodies to arbitrary convex bodies uses an approximation in the polar. This keeps the momentum factor inside the desired polar and enlarges the position factor by an arbitrarily small amount.

<a id="stmt-lem-polar-approximation"></a>
<div id="lem:polar-approximation" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:polar-approximation"></a>
**Lemma 6** (Finite polar approximation). *Let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be an origin-symmetric convex body. For every $\alpha>0$, there are finitely many vectors $b_1,\ldots,b_m\in\mathbb R^n$ spanning $\mathbb R^n$ such that $$K'=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:|\langle b_j,x\rangle|\le1,\quad 1\le j\le m\}$$ satisfies $$\label{eq:polar-approximation}
 K\subset K'\subset(1+\alpha)K,
 \qquad (K')^\circ\subset K^\circ.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-polar-approximation"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-polar-approximation</summary>

*Proof.* First, $0\in\operatorname{int}K$. Indeed, if the Euclidean ball $B(y,r)$ is contained in $K$, then so is $B(-y,r)$, and their midpoints contain $B(0,r)$. Since $K$ is also bounded, there are $r,R>0$ with $B(0,r)\subset K\subset B(0,R)$. The definition of the polar gives $$B(0,R^{-1})\subset K^\circ\subset\overline{B(0,r^{-1})}.$$ Thus $K^\circ$ is a compact, origin-symmetric convex body with nonempty interior.

We will use the bipolar identity $K^{\circ\circ}=K$. To recall its justification here, if $x\notin K$, separation gives a nonzero vector $v$ such that $$\langle v,x\rangle>h_K(v),
 \qquad h_K(v)=\max_{y\in K}\langle v,y\rangle>0.$$ The normalized vector $v/h_K(v)$ belongs to $K^\circ$ and pairs with $x$ to a value greater than $1$, so $x\notin K^{\circ\circ}$. The reverse inclusion follows directly from the definition. The same argument applies to every convex body containing the origin in its interior.

Put $\lambda=1+\alpha$ and $$\rho=\min_{|v|=1}h_{K^\circ}(v)>0.$$ Choose $d>0$ with $d\le\alpha\rho/(1+\alpha)$, and choose a finite $d$-net $\{b_1,\ldots,b_m\}$ in the compact set $K^\circ$, with all net points in $K^\circ$. Let $$P=\operatorname{conv}\{\pm b_1,\ldots,\pm b_m\}.$$ Symmetry of $K^\circ$ gives $P\subset K^\circ$. For every unit vector $v$, a maximizer of $\langle v,p\rangle$ over $p\in K^\circ$ lies within distance $d$ of a net point. Consequently $$h_P(v)\ge h_{K^\circ}(v)-d
       \ge\lambda^{-1}h_{K^\circ}(v).$$ The characterization of inclusion by support functions, which follows from convex separation, now gives $$\lambda^{-1}K^\circ\subset P\subset K^\circ.$$ In particular, $P$ has interior, so its generating vectors span $\mathbb R^n$. Taking polars and using the bipolar identity yields $$K\subset P^\circ\subset\lambda K,
 \qquad (P^\circ)^\circ=P\subset K^\circ.$$ Finally, the definition of $P$ shows that $P^\circ$ is exactly the finite strip body $K'$ in the statement. ◻

</details>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-polar-approximation-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-polar-approximation</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.* Fix $0<c<4$. Choose $\alpha>0$ such that $\lambda c<4$, where $\lambda=1+\alpha$, and choose one body $K'$ from Lemma <a href="#lem:polar-approximation" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:polar-approximation">6</a>. Proposition <a href="#prop:finite-strips" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:finite-strips">5</a> gives a smooth symplectic embedding $$e:B^{2n}(\lambda c)\longrightarrow
 \operatorname{int}K'\times\operatorname{int}\bigl((K')^\circ\bigr).$$ If one body is contained in another, every interior ball in the first is contained in the second. Thus Equation <a href="#eq:polar-approximation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polar-approximation">[eq:polar-approximation]</a> implies $$\operatorname{int}K'\subset\lambda\operatorname{int}K,
 \qquad \operatorname{int}\bigl((K')^\circ\bigr)\subset\operatorname{int}K^\circ,$$ even when their boundaries meet. Regard $e$ as an embedding into $\lambda\operatorname{int}K\times\operatorname{int}K^\circ$. Define $$D_{\sqrt\lambda}(z)=\sqrt\lambda\,z,
 \qquad Q_\lambda(q,p)=(q/\lambda,p).$$ The composition <a id="eq:general-rescaling"></a>$$\label{eq:general-rescaling}
 Q_\lambda\circ e\circ D_{\sqrt\lambda}:
 B^{2n}(c)\longrightarrow U_K$$ is a smooth embedding. Since $$D_{\sqrt\lambda}^*\omega_0=\lambda\omega_0,
 \qquad Q_\lambda^*\omega_0=\lambda^{-1}\omega_0,$$ its pullback of $\omega_0$ equals $\omega_0$. This proves $c_G(U_K)\ge4$.

For the upper bound, we use the supporting-slab construction of Artstein-Avidan, Karasev, and Ostrover (Artstein-Avidan, Karasev, and Ostrover 2014, Remark 4.2) <a id="pm-cite-30"></a>[[AAKO2014](#ref-AAKO2014)]. Choose $q_0\in K$ and $p_0\in K^\circ$ with $\langle q_0,p_0\rangle=1$. Such a pair is obtained by maximizing any nonzero linear functional on $K$ and normalizing its maximum to $1$. Complete $q_0$ to a basis of $\mathbb R^n$ by vectors in $\ker p_0$, and let $A$ be the matrix of this basis. The linear change $$Q=A^{-1}q,\qquad P=A^Tp$$ is symplectic and has first coordinates $Q_1=\langle p_0,q\rangle$ and $P_1=\langle q_0,p\rangle$. Both functionals are nonzero, so symmetry, polarity, and the interior condition give $|Q_1|<1$ and $|P_1|<1$ on $U_K$. Thus this change embeds $U_K$ into $(-1,1)^2\times\mathbb R^{2n-2}$, with the square in its first conjugate coordinate pair.

For completeness, this open square is area-preservingly diffeomorphic to the disk of area $4$. Put $R=2/\sqrt\pi$, $h(X)=\sqrt{R^2-X^2}$ for $-R<X<R$, and $$H(X)=-1+\int_{-R}^{X}h(s)\,ds.$$ Since $\int_{-R}^{R}h(s)\,ds=2$ and $H'=h>0$, $H$ is a smooth diffeomorphism from $(-R,R)$ onto $(-1,1)$. The map $$(u,v)\longmapsto
 \bigl(X=H^{-1}(u),\ Y=v\,h(H^{-1}(u))\bigr)$$ has image $B^2(4)$ and Jacobian $X'(u)h(X)=1$. Applying it to the first conjugate pair gives a symplectic embedding $U_K\hookrightarrow B^2(4)\times\mathbb R^{2n-2}$. Gromov’s nonsqueezing theorem (Gromov 1985) <a id="pm-cite-31"></a>[[Gromov1985](#ref-Gromov1985)] therefore bounds the capacity of every ball in $U_K$ by $4$. Combined with the lower bound, this proves $c_G(U_K)=4$. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-rem-parameter-order"></a>
<div id="rem:parameter-order" class="remark">

*Remark 7* (Order of choices). For each prescribed $0<c<4$, first choose $\alpha>0$ so that $\lambda c<4$, where $\lambda=1+\alpha$. Then fix the finite body $K'$ and its $m$ defining constraints. Choose $\eta>0$ so that $(1+\eta)\lambda c<4$, then choose $k$ large enough that $$\frac{C_{K'}}k+m\epsilon_k<\frac{\eta\pi}{4}.$$ This is possible because the constraint list is fixed before $k$ varies. Proposition <a href="#prop:finite-strips" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:finite-strips">5</a>, applied at capacity $\lambda c$, and the final rescaling then give the required ball. Each prescribed capacity is obtained by one finite construction; no limit of embeddings is required.

</div>

<a id="stmt-cor-mahler"></a>
<div id="cor:mahler" class="corollary">

**Corollary 8** (Symmetric Mahler inequality). *For every integer $n\ge1$ and every origin-symmetric convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^n$, $$\operatorname{vol}_n(K)\,\operatorname{vol}_n(K^\circ)\ge\frac{4^n}{n!}.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-cor-mahler"><summary>Proof of stmt-cor-mahler</summary>

*Proof.* For $n=1$, write $K=[-a,a]$ with $a>0$. Then $K^\circ=[-a^{-1},a^{-1}]$, and the product of their lengths is $4$. Assume henceforth that $n\ge2$.

<a id="eq:polar-product-volume"></a>
We first justify that taking interiors does not change the volumes in the statement. If a convex body $C\subset\mathbb R^n$ contains $B(0,r)$, then convexity gives, for $0<t<1$, $$tC+B(0,(1-t)r)\subset C.$$ Hence $tC\subset\operatorname{int}C$, and $$t^n\operatorname{vol}_n(C)\le\operatorname{vol}_n(\operatorname{int}C)\le\operatorname{vol}_n(C).$$ Letting $t\uparrow1$ proves $\operatorname{vol}_n(\operatorname{int}C)=\operatorname{vol}_n(C)$. Apply this to $K$ and $K^\circ$, both of which contain a neighborhood of the origin. The product formula for Lebesgue measure then gives $$\label{eq:polar-product-volume}
 \operatorname{vol}_{2n}(U_K)=\operatorname{vol}_n(K)\,\operatorname{vol}_n(K^\circ).$$

For every $0<c<4$, Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> supplies a smooth symplectic embedding $f:B^{2n}(c)\to U_K$. Taking the $n$th exterior power of $f^*\omega_0=\omega_0$ shows that $\det Df=1$, so the change of variables formula gives $$\frac{c^n}{n!}
 =\operatorname{vol}_{2n}\bigl(B^{2n}(c)\bigr)
 =\operatorname{vol}_{2n}\bigl(f(B^{2n}(c))\bigr)
 \le\operatorname{vol}_{2n}(U_K).$$ Here the first equality follows from the Euclidean ball volume $\pi^nR^{2n}/n!$ at radius $R=\sqrt{c/\pi}$. Combining this with Equation <a href="#eq:polar-product-volume" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:polar-product-volume">[eq:polar-product-volume]</a> and letting $c\uparrow4$ proves the claimed inequality. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-auto-remark-1"></a>
<div class="remark">

*Remark 9* (Sharpness). The cube $[-1,1]^n$ has volume $2^n$. Its polar is $\{p:\sum_{j=1}^n|p_j|\le1\}$, whose intersection with each coordinate orthant is a simplex of volume $1/n!$. These $2^n$ simplices meet only along faces, so the polar has volume $2^n/n!$. Their volume product is therefore $4^n/n!$, showing that the constant in Corollary <a href="#cor:mahler" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:mahler">8</a> is sharp.

</div>

<a id="stmt-cor-polar-packings"></a>
<div id="cor:polar-packings" class="corollary">

**Corollary 10** (A sufficient packing criterion). *Let $n\ge3$ and $k\ge1$ be integers, and let $K\subset\mathbb R^n$ be an origin-symmetric convex body. Suppose that $R_1,\ldots,R_k>0$ satisfy $$\sum_{i=1}^k R_i^n<4^n,
 \qquad R_i+R_j<4\quad(1\le i<j\le k).$$ Then there are symplectic embeddings of open neighborhoods of the closed balls $\overline{B^{2n}(R_1)},\ldots,\overline{B^{2n}(R_k)}$ into $U_K$ with pairwise disjoint images. The pairwise condition is vacuous for $k=1$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-cor-polar-packings"><summary>Proof of stmt-cor-polar-packings</summary>

*Proof.* Strictness and finiteness permit a choice of $0<c<4$ such that $\sum_i R_i^n<c^n$ and $R_i+R_j<c$ for every $i<j$. The ball-packing theorem (OpenAI 2026a, Theorem 1.1) <a id="pm-cite-32"></a>[[OpenAIBallPackings2026](#ref-OpenAIBallPackings2026)] embeds these closed balls into $B^{2n}(c)$, with embeddings defined on neighborhoods. Their compact images are pairwise disjoint and lie in the open target, so the neighborhoods can be shrunk to have pairwise disjoint images contained in $B^{2n}(c)$. Composing with the embedding in Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> proves the assertion. ◻

</details>
