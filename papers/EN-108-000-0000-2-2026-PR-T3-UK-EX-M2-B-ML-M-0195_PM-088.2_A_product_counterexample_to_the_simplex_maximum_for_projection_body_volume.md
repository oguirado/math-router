## 1. Abstract and result contract

> The product of two ten-dimensional simplices has larger normalized projection-body volume than a twenty-dimensional simplex. This gives a counterexample to Brannen's proposed simplex maximum.

- **C1** (counterexample, [source](#stmt-thm-counterexample)): Let $T_{10}=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(0,e_1,\ldots,e_{10})\subset\mathbb R^{10}$, where the $e_i$ are the coordinate unit vectors, and let $K=T_{10}\times T_{10}\subset\mathbb R^{20}$. Then $$\frac{R_{20}(K)}{c_{20}} =\frac{121\binom{20}{10}}{21\cdot2^{20}} =\frac{22\,355\,476}{22\,020\,096}>1.$$ Thus $K$ violates <a href="#eq:proposed-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:proposed-bound">[eq:proposed-bound]</a> in dimension twenty.
  - Explanation: The Cartesian product of two ten-dimensional simplices strictly exceeds the simplex benchmark in dimension twenty.
- **C2** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-facets)): Let $P\subset\mathbb R^d$, $d\ge2$, be a full-dimensional convex polytope. For each facet $F$, let $s_F$ be its $(d-1)$-dimensional volume and $\nu_F$ its outward unit normal. Then $$\label{eq:facet} h_{\Pi P}(u)=\frac12\sum_Fs_F\|\langle\nu_F,u\rangle\|, \qquad u\in\mathbb R^d,$$ and therefore $$\label{eq:zonotope} \Pi P=\sum_F \left[-\frac{s_F\nu_F}{2},\frac{s_F\nu_F}{2}\right].$$
  - Explanation: Facet areas and outward normals express the projection body as a Minkowski sum of centered segments.
- **C3** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-product)): Let $A\subset\mathbb R^r$ and $B\subset\mathbb R^s$ be full-dimensional convex polytopes, where $r,s\ge2$. In the orthogonal product space $\mathbb R^{r+s}=\mathbb R^r\times\mathbb R^s$, $$\label{eq:product-body} \Pi(A\times B)=(\|B\|\Pi A)\times(\|A\|\Pi B).$$ In particular, $$\label{eq:multiplicative} R_{r+s}(A\times B)=R_r(A)R_s(B).$$
  - Explanation: The projection body of an orthogonal product separates into scaled factors, and the normalized volume is multiplicative.
- **C4** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-affine)): Let $P\subset\mathbb R^d$, $d\ge2$, be a convex body. For every invertible linear map $L\colon\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, $$\Pi(LP)=\|\det L\|L^{-t}\Pi P,\qquad R_d(LP)=R_d(P),$$ where $L^{-t}$ denotes the inverse transpose. Translations also preserve $R_d$.
  - Explanation: Invertible affine changes preserve the normalized projection-body volume, while the projection body transforms covariantly.
- **C5** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-simplex)): For $d\ge2$, the standard simplex $T_d=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(0,e_1,\ldots,e_d)\subset\mathbb R^d$ satisfies $$\|T_d\|=\frac1{d!},\qquad \|\Pi T_d\|=\frac{d+1}{((d-1)!)^d},\qquad R_d(T_d)=c_d.$$ Every full-dimensional $d$-simplex has the same value $c_d$.
  - Explanation: The simplex projection body is a translated, scaled cube plus a segment; its volume gives the simplex benchmark.
- **C6** (bound, [source](#stmt-cor-exponential-excess)): There exist a constant $\lambda>1$ and an integer $N\ge20$ such that, for every integer $n\ge N$, some full-dimensional convex polytope $K_n\subset\mathbb R^n$ satisfies $$\frac{R_n(K_n)}{R_n(T_n)}\ge\lambda^n.$$ The polytopes $K_n$ may be chosen as Cartesian products of simplices.
  - Explanation: Repeating ten-dimensional simplex factors with an additional simplex yields exponential excess over the simplex benchmark in every sufficiently large dimension.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Convex body** ([source](#pm-h-2)): Throughout, a convex body in $\mathbb R^d$ is compact and convex with nonempty interior, and $d\ge2$.
- **Volume and support function** ([source](#pm-h-2)): Write $\|K\|$ for its $d$-dimensional volume and $h_C(x)=\max_{y\in C}\langle x,y\rangle$ for the support function of a compact convex set $C$.
- **Projection body** ([source](#pm-h-2)): The projection body $\Pi_d K$, also written $\Pi K$ when the dimension is evident, is defined by $$h_{\Pi K}(u)=\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}_{d-1}\bigl(\mathop{\mathrm{proj}}_{u^\perp}K\bigr), \qquad u\in S^{d-1},$$ extended homogeneously to all of $\mathbb R^d$.
- **Normalized projection-body volume and simplex benchmark** ([source](#pm-h-2)): The functional under consideration is $$\label{eq:functional} R_d(K)=\frac{\|\Pi K\|}{\|K\|^{d-1}}, \qquad c_d=\frac{(d+1)d^d}{d!}.$$
- **Minkowski addition** ([source](#sec:facets)): We use Minkowski addition $C+D=\{c+d:c\in C,\ d\in D\}$.
- **Standard simplex** ([source](#stmt-prop-simplex)): For $d\ge2$, the standard simplex $T_d=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(0,e_1,\ldots,e_d)\subset\mathbb R^d$ satisfies $$\|T_d\|=\frac1{d!},\qquad \|\Pi T_d\|=\frac{d+1}{((d-1)!)^d},\qquad R_d(T_d)=c_d.$$

## 3. Proof roadmap

- **S1** [claim](#stmt-thm-counterexample) / [proof](#proof-stmt-prop-simplex-2): Exact integer comparison after applying product and simplex identities. -> The Cartesian product of two ten-dimensional simplices strictly exceeds the simplex benchmark in dimension twenty.
- **S2** [claim](#stmt-lem-facets) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-facets): Integrate the entry and exit facet multiplicity under orthogonal projection. -> Facet areas and outward normals express the projection body as a Minkowski sum of centered segments.
- **S3** [claim](#stmt-prop-product) / [proof](#proof-stmt-prop-product): Separate product facets into orthogonal families and cancel volume factors. -> The projection body of an orthogonal product separates into scaled factors, and the normalized volume is multiplicative.
- **S4** [claim](#stmt-lem-affine) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-affine): Transform facet area-normal vectors, then pass from inscribed polytopes to a convex body. -> Invertible affine changes preserve the normalized projection-body volume, while the projection body transforms covariantly.
- **S5** [claim](#stmt-prop-simplex) / [proof](#proof-stmt-prop-simplex): Extrude a coordinate cube by the diagonal segment and normalize its volume. -> The simplex projection body is a translated, scaled cube plus a segment; its volume gives the simplex benchmark.
- **S6** [claim](#stmt-cor-exponential-excess) / [proof](#proof-stmt-cor-exponential-excess): Repeat simplex factors and compare exponential growth with the simplex benchmark. -> Repeating ten-dimensional simplex factors with an additional simplex yields exponential excess over the simplex benchmark in every sufficiently large dimension.

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I1**: The normalized projection-body functional is multiplicative for orthogonal Cartesian products of full-dimensional polytopes. Simplex factors therefore reduce comparison with the simplex benchmark to exact rational arithmetic.
- **I2**: The Cartesian product of the two specified standard simplices violates the simplex upper bound through an exact strictly positive rational excess.
- **I3**: The functional is preserved by invertible affine transformations, including translations.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **A1**: Use multiplicativity for degenerate factors or an arbitrary coupled geometric composition. -> The proof separates facet families in an orthogonal Cartesian product of full-dimensional factors; the stated hypotheses do not cover that composition.
- **A2**: Apply the same result to the volume of the polar projection body. -> The source explicitly identifies polar-volume inequalities as a different functional.
- **A3**: Convert the counterexample into a hardware concurrency, queue throughput, or optimal-constant certificate. -> Engineering correspondences are ABSENT; the source leaves the optimal upper constant and maximizing bodies unresolved.
- **A4**: Apply affine invariance under a singular map. -> A singular transformation can destroy full dimensionality, and the inverse transpose is undefined.

### Introduction

Throughout, a convex body in $\mathbb R^d$ is compact and convex with nonempty interior, and $d\ge2$. Write $|K|$ for its $d$-dimensional volume and $h_C(x)=\max_{y\in C}\langle x,y\rangle$ for the support function of a compact convex set $C$. The projection body $\Pi_d K$, also written $\Pi K$ when the dimension is evident, is defined by $$h_{\Pi K}(u)=\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}_{d-1}\bigl(\mathop{\mathrm{proj}}_{u^\perp}K\bigr),
 \qquad u\in S^{d-1},$$ extended homogeneously to all of $\mathbb R^d$. Here $S^{d-1}$ is the Euclidean unit sphere and $\mathop{\mathrm{proj}}_{u^\perp}$ is orthogonal projection. We write $B_2^j$ for the Euclidean unit ball in $\mathbb R^j$ and $\kappa_j=|B_2^j|$. The functional under consideration is <a id="eq:functional"></a>$$\label{eq:functional}
 R_d(K)=\frac{|\Pi K|}{|K|^{d-1}},
 \qquad c_d=\frac{(d+1)d^d}{d!}.$$

<a id="eq:proposed-bound"></a>
The extremal values of this affine-invariant functional are classical questions in convex geometry. Petty established the affine covariance of the projection-body operator <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[Petty](#ref-Petty)]; see also <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[Ludwig](#ref-Ludwig), Equation (1)]. Brannen conjectured in 1996 that simplices maximize $R_d$ among all convex bodies <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[Brannen](#ref-Brannen)]. The simplex value is $c_d$, as we verify below. Thus the proposed simplex upper bound is $$\label{eq:proposed-bound}
 |\Pi K|\le c_d|K|^{d-1}.$$ It is the volume of $\Pi K$ itself that occurs here, not the volume of its polar: the classical projection inequalities involving the polar concern a different functional. For $d\ge3$, Petty’s separate minimum problem asks whether ellipsoids are the only minimizers of the same functional $R_d$; see <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[Saroglou](#ref-Saroglou), Section 1] for these distinctions.

In dimension three, Chen, Feng, Li, Xi, and Xu settle the minimum problem and also prove the bound $R_3(K)\le18=c_3$ for all three-dimensional convex bodies, with tetrahedra attaining equality <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[ChenEtAl](#ref-ChenEtAl), Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]. Feng, Hu, Liu, and Xu give counterexamples to <a href="#eq:proposed-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:proposed-bound">[eq:proposed-bound]</a> in every dimension $d\ge9$, using polytopes with at most $d+2$ facets <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[FengEtAl](#ref-FengEtAl), Theorem 1.3]. Their result already gives a negative answer to the unrestricted-dimensional simplex-maximum question. Their construction uses perturbed projections of a cube and Minkowski’s existence theorem <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[FengEtAl](#ref-FengEtAl), Section 4]. The present paper supplies a direct Cartesian-product construction and its exact value: two ten-dimensional simplices suffice.

<a id="stmt-thm-counterexample"></a>
<div id="thm:counterexample" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1**. *Let $T_{10}=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(0,e_1,\ldots,e_{10})\subset\mathbb R^{10}$, where the $e_i$ are the coordinate unit vectors, and let $K=T_{10}\times T_{10}\subset\mathbb R^{20}$. Then $$\frac{R_{20}(K)}{c_{20}}
 =\frac{121\binom{20}{10}}{21\cdot2^{20}}
 =\frac{22\,355\,476}{22\,020\,096}>1.$$ Thus $K$ violates <a href="#eq:proposed-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:proposed-bound">[eq:proposed-bound]</a> in dimension twenty.*

</div>

The exact witness in Theorem <a href="#thm:counterexample" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:counterexample">1</a> provides an elementary illustration of the failure of the simplex maximum. The optimal upper constant and its maximizing bodies remain separate questions.

The useful mechanism is a product test: for full-dimensional polytopes $A\subset\mathbb R^r$ and $B\subset\mathbb R^s$, with $r,s\ge2$, $$R_{r+s}(A\times B)=R_r(A)R_s(B).$$ Consequently, the product $c_rc_s$ of the simplex values is a necessary lower bound for any universal upper constant in dimension $r+s$. The dimension-twenty counterexample comes from comparing two copies of the ten-dimensional simplex value with the twenty-dimensional simplex value. All identities used in that comparison are proved below.

The growth of upper constants with dimension is a further quantitative question. For centrally symmetric bodies, Henk gives the lower bound $2^d(9/8)^{\lfloor d/3\rfloor}$ for the optimal upper constant <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[Henk](#ref-Henk)]. Iterating our product identity gives an exponential excess over the simplex benchmark: for some fixed $\lambda>1$ and every sufficiently large $n$, a product of simplices $K_n\subset\mathbb R^n$ satisfies $R_n(K_n)\ge\lambda^n c_n$. Corollary <a href="#cor:exponential-excess" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="cor:exponential-excess">6</a> proves this consequence by repeating ten-dimensional factors and using one additional simplex to reach each dimension.

Section <a href="#sec:facets" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:facets">2</a> derives the facet formula, the product identity, and affine covariance. Section <a href="#sec:simplex" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:simplex">3</a> computes the simplex value by expressing its projection body as a cube plus one segment. Section <a href="#sec:counterexample" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:counterexample">4</a> combines the identities and gives the exact integer comparison, then proves the exponential excess in all sufficiently large dimensions.

<a id="sec:facets"></a>
### Facet vectors and Cartesian products

We first express projection bodies of polytopes as sums of segments. The facets of a Cartesian product then separate into two families, which will make its normalized projection-body volume multiplicative. The facet formula is the polytopal case of Cauchy’s projection formula; see <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[Saroglou](#ref-Saroglou), Section 1]. We include its proof to fix the factor $1/2$ on which the subsequent constants depend.

We use Minkowski addition $C+D=\{c+d:c\in C,\ d\in D\}$. Support functions add under this operation and determine compact convex sets uniquely. For the latter assertion, if $x\notin C$ and $y\in C$ is nearest to $x$, minimization along each segment $[y,z]\subset C$ gives $\langle x-y,z-y\rangle\le0$ for every $z\in C$. The linear functional with vector $x-y$ therefore separates $x$ from $C$.

<div class="samepage">

<a id="stmt-lem-facets"></a>
<div id="lem:facets" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:facet"></a>
<a id="eq:zonotope"></a>
**Lemma 2**. *Let $P\subset\mathbb R^d$, $d\ge2$, be a full-dimensional convex polytope. For each facet $F$, let $s_F$ be its $(d-1)$-dimensional volume and $\nu_F$ its outward unit normal. Then $$\label{eq:facet}
 h_{\Pi P}(u)=\frac12\sum_Fs_F|\langle\nu_F,u\rangle|,
 \qquad u\in\mathbb R^d,$$ and therefore $$\label{eq:zonotope}
 \Pi P=\sum_F
 \left[-\frac{s_F\nu_F}{2},\frac{s_F\nu_F}{2}\right].$$*

</div>

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-facets"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-facets</summary>

*Proof.* First take $u$ to be a unit vector. Orthogonal projection from the hyperplane of $F$ to $u^\perp$ has absolute Jacobian $|\langle\nu_F,u\rangle|$. Indeed, for an orthonormal tangent basis $v_1,\ldots,v_{d-1}$, that Jacobian is $|\det(v_1,\ldots,v_{d-1},u)|$, which equals the stated inner product in absolute value.

Apart from a set of $(d-1)$-dimensional measure zero, a point in the projection of $P$ lies on the projections of exactly two facet interiors: the entry and exit points of its line parallel to $u$. To see that the exceptions are null, discard the boundary of the projection and the projections of all faces of dimension at most $d-2$. Also discard projections of facets whose hyperplanes are parallel to $u$; these projections have dimension at most $d-2$. Every remaining line meets $P$ in a nondegenerate interval with its two endpoints in facet interiors. Integrating this multiplicity gives <a href="#eq:facet" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:facet">[eq:facet]</a> for unit $u$, and homogeneity gives the general case.

The support function of $[-v/2,v/2]$ is $\tfrac12|\langle v,u\rangle|$. Adding these support functions proves <a href="#eq:zonotope" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:zonotope">[eq:zonotope]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="stmt-prop-product"></a>
<div id="prop:product" class="proposition">

<a id="eq:product-body"></a>
**Proposition 3**. *Let $A\subset\mathbb R^r$ and $B\subset\mathbb R^s$ be full-dimensional convex polytopes, where $r,s\ge2$. In the orthogonal product space $\mathbb R^{r+s}=\mathbb R^r\times\mathbb R^s$, $$\label{eq:product-body}
 \Pi(A\times B)=(|B|\Pi A)\times(|A|\Pi B).$$ In particular, <a id="eq:multiplicative"></a>$$\label{eq:multiplicative}
 R_{r+s}(A\times B)=R_r(A)R_s(B).$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-product"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-product</summary>

*Proof.* The face exposed by a linear functional $(u,v)$ on $A\times B$ is the product of the faces exposed by $u$ and $v$. Dimensions add under Cartesian products. A nonzero linear functional exposes a proper face of a full-dimensional body, so if both $u$ and $v$ are nonzero, the product face has codimension at least two. A facet therefore has one facet factor and one whole factor. Thus the facets are $F\times B$ and $A\times G$, with area-normal vectors $$(|B|s_F\nu_F,0)
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 (0,|A|s_G\nu_G),$$ respectively. The area factors are products because the two coordinate spaces are orthogonal. Lemma <a href="#lem:facets" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:facets">2</a> now gives $$h_{\Pi(A\times B)}(u,v)
 =|B|h_{\Pi A}(u)+|A|h_{\Pi B}(v).$$ This is the support function of the right side of <a href="#eq:product-body" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:product-body">[eq:product-body]</a>, proving that identity. Hence $$\begin{split}
 R_{r+s}(A\times B)
 &=\frac{|B|^r|A|^s|\Pi A||\Pi B|}
         {(|A||B|)^{r+s-1}}\\
 &=\frac{|\Pi A|}{|A|^{r-1}}
   \frac{|\Pi B|}{|B|^{s-1}},
 \end{split}$$ as required. ◻

</details>

The product identity reduces our comparison to the values of its factors. To use simplices as a dimension-dependent benchmark, we also need to know that all full-dimensional simplices of a fixed dimension have the same value. The following affine covariance formula supplies that fact.

<a id="stmt-lem-affine"></a>
<div id="lem:affine" class="lemma">

**Lemma 4**. *Let $P\subset\mathbb R^d$, $d\ge2$, be a convex body. For every invertible linear map $L\colon\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, $$\Pi(LP)=|\det L|L^{-t}\Pi P,\qquad R_d(LP)=R_d(P),$$ where $L^{-t}$ denotes the inverse transpose. Translations also preserve $R_d$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-affine"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-affine</summary>

*Proof.* First suppose that $P$ is a polytope. The transformed outward unit normal of $F$ is $L^{-t}\nu_F/\|L^{-t}\nu_F\|$. Its area is $$s_{LF}=|\det L|\,\|L^{-t}\nu_F\|\,s_F.$$ For the area formula, take a prism of height one over $F$, extending in direction $\nu_F$. Its image has volume $|\det L|s_F$ and height $1/\|L^{-t}\nu_F\|$ over $LF$; dividing volume by height gives the formula. Thus each area-normal vector transforms by $|\det L|L^{-t}$, including when $\det L<0$. Apply <a href="#eq:zonotope" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:zonotope">[eq:zonotope]</a>. The absolute determinant of this map is $|\det L|^{d-1}$, exactly the scaling of $|LP|^{d-1}$. Translation invariance follows directly from projection volumes.

For completeness, we justify the passage to an arbitrary convex body and the existence of its projection body. Translate an interior point of $P$ to the origin, choose $R>0$ with $P\subset RB_2^d$, and take increasing inscribed polytopes $P_m$ containing a fixed ball $aB_2^d$, $a>0$, such that $$P_m\subset P\subset P_m+\varepsilon_mB_2^d
 \subset(1+\varepsilon_m/a)P_m,
 \qquad \varepsilon_m\longrightarrow0.$$ Take, for example, convex hulls of cumulative finite nets in $P$ and a fixed inscribed polytope containing $aB_2^d$. Put $\lambda_m=1+\varepsilon_m/a$, and for unit $u$ write $F(u)=\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}_{d-1}(\mathop{\mathrm{proj}}_{u^\perp}P)$ and $f_m(u)=h_{\Pi P_m}(u)$. Projection and volume monotonicity give $$0\le F(u)-f_m(u)
 \le (\lambda_m^{d-1}-1)\kappa_{d-1}R^{d-1}.$$ Thus $f_m\to F$ uniformly. The increasing bodies $\Pi P_m$ lie in a fixed ball and contain $\kappa_{d-1}a^{d-1}B_2^d$, so their union closure is a convex body with support function $F$, namely $\Pi P$. The support inequalities also give $$\Pi P_m\subset\Pi P\subset\lambda_m^{d-1}\Pi P_m,
 \qquad
 |\Pi P_m|\le|\Pi P|\le\lambda_m^{d(d-1)}|\Pi P_m|.$$ Likewise $|P_m|\le|P|\le\lambda_m^d|P_m|$. Applying $L$ to the original sandwich gives the same convergence for $LP_m$. Passing to the limit in the polytope identity proves covariance and, by these volume inequalities, the asserted invariance of $R_d$. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:simplex"></a>
### The simplex value

We now compute the value to which the product will be compared. Only one extra segment beyond a coordinate cube is needed to describe the projection body of a simplex.

<a id="stmt-prop-simplex"></a>
<div id="prop:simplex" class="proposition">

**Proposition 5**. *For $d\ge2$, the standard simplex $T_d=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(0,e_1,\ldots,e_d)\subset\mathbb R^d$ satisfies $$|T_d|=\frac1{d!},\qquad
 |\Pi T_d|=\frac{d+1}{((d-1)!)^d},\qquad
 R_d(T_d)=c_d.$$ Every full-dimensional $d$-simplex has the same value $c_d$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-simplex"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-simplex</summary>

*Proof.* The simplex volume follows by iterated base and height. Put $w=e_1+\cdots+e_d$. The coordinate facets have area-normal vectors $-e_i/(d-1)!$. The remaining facet has unit normal $w/\sqrt d$. Dropping its last coordinate projects it onto the standard $(d-1)$-simplex with Jacobian $1/\sqrt d$; its area-normal vector is therefore $w/(d-1)!$. In the centered segments of Lemma <a href="#lem:facets" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:facets">2</a>, changing the sign of a generator changes no segment. Translating each segment to start at the origin therefore shows that $\Pi T_d$ is a translate of $$\frac{[0,1]^d+[0,w]}{(d-1)!}.$$

To compute the numerator’s volume, let $Q=[0,1]^d$. Every nonempty fiber of $Q$ parallel to $w$ is an interval, and adding $[0,w]$ increases its length by $\|w\|$. The base projection is unchanged. Fubini therefore gives $$|Q+[0,w]|
 =|Q|+\|w\|\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}_{d-1}(\mathop{\mathrm{proj}}_{w^\perp}Q)
 =1+h_{\Pi Q}(w).$$ The cube has two facets of area one in each coordinate direction, so <a href="#eq:facet" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:facet">[eq:facet]</a> gives $h_{\Pi Q}(w)=d$. Thus $|Q+[0,w]|=d+1$; Figure <a href="#fig:extrusion" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:extrusion">1</a> illustrates the two-dimensional case. Dilation by $1/(d-1)!$ and division by $|T_d|^{d-1}$ now yield $$R_d(T_d)=\frac{(d+1)(d!)^{d-1}}{((d-1)!)^d}
         =\frac{(d+1)d^d}{d!}.$$ Every full-dimensional simplex is an invertible affine image of $T_d$, so Lemma <a href="#lem:affine" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:affine">4</a> proves the final assertion. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:counterexample"></a>
### The counterexample and exponential excess

The geometric work is complete. The product formula turns the comparison in dimension twenty into a calculation with two simplex constants.

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-simplex-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-simplex</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:counterexample" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:counterexample">1</a>.* The product $K=T_{10}\times T_{10}$ is compact and convex with nonempty interior in $\mathbb R^{20}$. Propositions <a href="#prop:product" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:product">3</a> and <a href="#prop:simplex" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:simplex">5</a> give $$R_{20}(K)=R_{10}(T_{10})^2
         =\left(\frac{11\cdot10^{10}}{10!}\right)^2.$$ Consequently, $$\frac{R_{20}(K)}{c_{20}}
 =\frac{121\cdot10^{20}\cdot20!}
        {(10!)^2\cdot21\cdot20^{20}}
 =\frac{121\binom{20}{10}}{21\cdot2^{20}}
 =\frac{22\,355\,476}{22\,020\,096}>1.$$ Here $\binom{20}{10}=184\,756$, and the exact integer certificate is $$121\cdot184\,756-21\cdot1\,048\,576=335\,380>0.$$ This is an exact strict violation of <a href="#eq:proposed-bound" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:proposed-bound">[eq:proposed-bound]</a>. ◻

</details>

More generally, for $r,s\ge2$, the same two propositions give the explicit product test $$\frac{R_{r+s}(T_r\times T_s)}{c_{r+s}}
 =\frac{(r+1)(s+1)}{r+s+1}
   \binom{r+s}{r}\frac{r^rs^s}{(r+s)^{r+s}}.$$ Thus any universal upper constant in dimension $r+s$ must be at least $c_rc_s$.

Iterating the product test gives the following quantitative consequence.

<a id="stmt-cor-exponential-excess"></a>
<div id="cor:exponential-excess" class="corollary">

**Corollary 6**. *There exist a constant $\lambda>1$ and an integer $N\ge20$ such that, for every integer $n\ge N$, some full-dimensional convex polytope $K_n\subset\mathbb R^n$ satisfies $$\frac{R_n(K_n)}{R_n(T_n)}\ge\lambda^n.$$ The polytopes $K_n$ may be chosen as Cartesian products of simplices.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-cor-exponential-excess"><summary>Proof of stmt-cor-exponential-excess</summary>

*Proof.* We use repeated ten-dimensional factors to obtain exponential growth, and one factor of dimension between ten and nineteen to reach every sufficiently large dimension. For $r\in\{0,\ldots,9\}$, put $d_r=10+r$. Every integer $n\ge20$ has a unique representation $n=10k+d_r$ with $k\ge1$. In the orthogonal coordinate product space, take $$K_n=T_{10}^{\,k}\times T_{d_r}.$$ Here $T_{10}^{\,k}$ denotes the $k$-fold Cartesian product. All factors and their successive products are full-dimensional convex polytopes, with each factor dimension at least ten. Thus Propositions <a href="#prop:product" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:product">3</a> and <a href="#prop:simplex" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:simplex">5</a> apply iteratively and give $$R_n(K_n)=c_{10}^k c_{d_r}.$$

To compare this value with $c_n$, we bound the simplex constants by $n+1$ times a fixed exponential, whose rate is smaller than that supplied by the ten-dimensional factors. Put $\beta=11/4$. The exponential series gives $$e=\frac52+\sum_{j=3}^{\infty}\frac1{j!}
 <\frac52+\frac16\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}3^{-j}
 =\beta,$$ since the successive ratios in the tail are strictly less than $1/3$. The positive term $n^n/n!$ in the series for $e^n$ is strictly smaller than the full sum, so $$c_n<(n+1)e^n<(n+1)\beta^n\qquad(n\ge2).$$ For the fixed ten-dimensional block, exact arithmetic yields $$c_{10}=\frac{17\,187\,500}{567},\qquad
 A:=\frac{c_{10}}{\beta^{10}}
 =\frac{1\,638\,400\,000\,000}{1\,336\,956\,340\,797}>1.$$ Choose a fixed $\lambda$ with $1<\lambda<A^{1/10}$. Combining these estimates with the formula for $R_n(K_n)$ gives $$\frac{R_n(K_n)}{R_n(T_n)\lambda^n}
 >c_{d_r}\beta^{-d_r}\lambda^{-d_r}
   \frac{(A/\lambda^{10})^k}{10k+d_r+1}.$$ For each fixed $r$, the right side tends to infinity as $k\to\infty$, because $A/\lambda^{10}>1$. A threshold can therefore be chosen in each of the ten residue classes; their maximum, enlarged to be at least twenty, gives one $N$ for all $n\ge N$. ◻

</details>
