## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We construct an exact semidefinite lift of the explicit nonspectrahedral hyperbolicity cone in twenty-three variables defined in the companion paper. The lift is a homogeneous real symmetric pencil of size 100 with 307 auxiliary variables and represents the entire closed cone, including every point with singular <i>X</i>. Thus, although this cone has no semidefinite representation in its original coordinates, it admits one when auxiliary variables are allowed. The stated sizes are not claimed to be minimal.

- **C1** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): There exist fixed matrices $A_1,\ldots,A_{23},B_1,\ldots,B_{307} \in\mathbb S^{100}$ such that, for every $x=(X,Z,y)\in\mathcal V$, $$x\in K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{there exists }v\in\mathbb R^{307}\text{ with } \sum_{\nu=1}^{23}x_\nu A_\nu+ \sum_{j=1}^{307}v_jB_j\succeq0.$$ The equivalence holds on the entire closed cone, including points with singular $X$.
  - Explanation: An exact homogeneous semidefinite lift represents the entire closed cone, including singular matrix inputs.
- **C2** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-gram)): For every $\theta,\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:gram} \widetilde F_\theta(\eta,\xi) \widetilde F_\theta(\eta,\xi)^T =Q\bigl(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)\bigr).$$ In particular, $Q(y)\succeq0$ for every $y\in\mathbb R^3$.
  - Explanation: Angular Gram factors prove positive semidefiniteness of the Choi–Lam matrix for every real input.
- **C3** (theorem, [source](#stmt-lem-slice)): For every $\theta,\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R$ and $\mathcal X,\mathcal Z\in\mathbb S^4$, $$\label{eq:slice-equivalence} (\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,Y_\theta(\eta,\xi))\in K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_\theta(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)\succeq0.$$ In particular, $p$ is hyperbolic with respect to $e$. If $\mathcal X\succ0$, the conditions in <a href="#eq:slice-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-equivalence">[eq:slice-equivalence]</a> are also equivalent to $\mathcal Z\succeq\Phi_{Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)}(\mathcal X^{-1})$.
  - Explanation: Each angular slice is exactly a positive semidefinite pencil locus; the determinant identity establishes hyperbolicity.
- **C4** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-matching)): Let $S\in\mathbb S^3$ and let $z\in\mathbb R^3$ satisfy $(z_1,z_2)\ne(0,0)$. There is a matrix $C(\theta)\in\mathbb C^{3\times2}$ whose entries are trigonometric polynomials of degree at most two such that, for all $\theta,\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:matching} \begin{aligned} \widetilde C(\theta)\widetilde C(\theta)^T&=Q(z),\\ \langle F_\theta(\eta,\xi),C(\theta)\rangle_S &=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(SQ(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi),z)\bigr). \end{aligned}$$
  - Explanation: A unitary rotation constructs bounded-degree matching test matrices, including for indefinite pairing matrices.
- **C5** (theorem, [source](#stmt-prop-lift)): For $(X,Z,y)\in\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3$, the following are equivalent:* 1. *$(X,Z,y)\in K$;* 2. *there is a real linear functional $\Lambda:\mathcal E\to\mathbb R$ satisfying <a href="#eq:output" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:output">[eq:output]</a> and $H(\Lambda)\succeq0$.
  - Explanation: Output constraints and moment-matrix positivity characterize cone membership for arbitrary feasible linear functionals.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Real symmetric matrices** ([source](#sec:introduction)): Let $\mathbb S^n$ denote the real symmetric $n\times n$ matrices.
- **Spectrahedron and spectrahedral shadow** ([source](#sec:introduction)): A *spectrahedron* is a set defined by a finite affine linear matrix inequality; a *spectrahedral shadow* is a linear projection of such a set.
- **Hyperbolicity** ([source](#sec:introduction)): For a homogeneous real polynomial $p$ with $p(e)>0$, hyperbolicity with respect to $e$ means that $t\mapsto p(te-x)$ has only real roots for every $x$.
- **Closed hyperbolicity cone** ([source](#sec:introduction)): We use the closed-cone convention $$K(p,e)=\{x:\text{every root of }t\mapsto p(te-x) \text{ is nonnegative}\}.$$
- **Choi–Lam matrix Q** ([source](#eq:p)): For $y=(y_1,y_2,y_3)\in\mathbb R^3$, define $$\label{eq:Q} Q(y)=\begin{pmatrix} y_1^2+y_2^2&-y_1y_2&-y_1y_3\\ -y_1y_2&y_2^2+y_3^2&-y_2y_3\\ -y_1y_3&-y_2y_3&y_3^2+y_1^2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
- **Linear map Phi** ([source](#eq:p)): For $a\in\mathbb R$, $r\in\mathbb R^3$, and $T\in\mathbb S^3$, set $$\label{eq:Phi} \Phi_y\!\begin{pmatrix}a&r^T\\r&T\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(Q(y)T)&-r^TQ(y)\\ -Q(y)r&aQ(y) \end{pmatrix}.$$
- **Polynomial p and direction e** ([source](#eq:p)): On the space $\mathcal V=\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3$, define $$\label{eq:p} p(X,Z,y)=\det\!\left((\det X)Z-\Phi_y(\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X)\right), \qquad e=(I_4,I_4,0),$$ where $\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X$ denotes the classical adjugate.
- **Realification** ([source](#sec:slices)): For a complex matrix $F=(f_1,f_2)\in\mathbb C^{3\times2}$, define its realification by $$\widetilde F=(\operatorname{Re}f_1,\operatorname{Im}f_1, \operatorname{Re}f_2,\operatorname{Im}f_2).$$

- **Pairing** ([source](#sec:matching)): For $S\in\mathbb S^3$ and $F,H\in\mathbb C^{3\times2}$, put $$\langle F,H\rangle_S =\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}(F^*SH) =\operatorname{tr}(\widetilde F^T S\widetilde H).$$
- **Trigonometric polynomial space** ([source](#eq:moment)): Let $\mathcal T_j$ be the real vector space of trigonometric polynomials of degree at most $j$, with basis $$1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\ldots,\cos(j\theta),\sin(j\theta).$$
- **Function space E** ([source](#eq:moment)): Let $\mathcal E$ be the space of real functions of $(\theta,\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)$ that are linear homogeneous in the $22$ coordinates of $(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)\in \mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^2$, with coefficients in $\mathcal T_7$.
- **Lifting functional** ([source](#eq:moment)): Our lifting variable is a real linear functional $\Lambda:\mathcal E\to\mathbb R$; on vectors and matrices we apply it entrywise.
- **Moment matrix H** ([source](#eq:moment-matrix)): Indeed, set $(f_1,\ldots,f_5)= (1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos2\theta,\sin2\theta)$ and define $H(\Lambda)\in\mathbb S^{100}$ by $$\label{eq:moment-matrix} H(\Lambda)_{(i,a),(j,b)} =\Lambda\bigl(f_a f_b (L_\theta)_{ij}\bigr), \qquad 1\leq i,j\leq20,\quad 1\leq a,b\leq5.$$

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I-Q-PSD**: The explicitly defined Choi–Lam matrix Q is positive semidefinite for every real input.
- **I-closed-lift**: The finite homogeneous lift represents the entire closed cone, including singular X.
- **I-matching-domain**: The degree-two matching construction allows indefinite S but assumes a nonzero first-two-coordinate part of z.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **A-inverse**: Use an ordinary inverse for singular X. -> X inverse is undefined and drops closed boundary inputs.
- **A-minimal**: Treat the displayed lift as the smallest SDP model. -> No optimality of matrix size or auxiliary count is proved.
- **A-full-certificate**: Treat finite Q evaluation tests as a proof of the complete lift theorem. -> The test harness checks finitely many rational triples and does not construct H or solve for Lambda.
- **A-matching-axis**: Apply the stated matching lemma on the z3 axis without another argument. -> Its nonzero transverse-coordinate precondition fails.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `constructive`. Finite constructive component at eq:Q and Lemma 2: evaluate the explicit rational matrix and its exact principal-minor PSD certificate. This implements neither the full semidefinite lift nor a general cone-membership solver; finite tests give PARTIAL coverage of the all-real invariant.

```python
# INPUTS: JSON list of rational triples; each coordinate is an integer or rational string.
# PRECONDITIONS: At most 256 triples, each rational token at most 128 characters.
# OUTPUTS: Q entries, all nonempty principal minors, and exact PSD verdict for each triple.
# TIME: O(n) exact rational operations; bit cost depends on input numerators and denominators.
# SPACE: O(n) output storage; constant working matrix size.
# ARITHMETIC: python-flint fmpq exact rational arithmetic; no floating point.
# FAILURE: Invalid shape, token, denominator, or batch size raises and exits nonzero.
import json, sys
from itertools import combinations, permutations
from flint import fmpq
batch=json.load(sys.stdin)
if not isinstance(batch,list) or not 1 <= len(batch) <= 256:
    raise ValueError('batch size')
answer=[]
for triple in batch:
    if not isinstance(triple,list) or len(triple)!=3:
        raise ValueError('triple')
    for token in triple:
        if type(token) not in (str,int) or len(str(token))>128:
            raise ValueError('rational token')
    a,b,c=(fmpq(str(t)) for t in triple)
    Q=[[a*a+b*b,-a*b,-a*c],[-a*b,b*b+c*c,-b*c],[-a*c,-b*c,c*c+a*a]]
    minors=[]
    for size in (1,2,3):
        for indices in combinations(range(3),size):
            det=fmpq(0)
            for perm in permutations(range(size)):
                inversions=sum(perm[i]>perm[j] for i in range(size) for j in range(i+1,size))
                term=fmpq((-1)**inversions)
                for i in range(size):
                    term *= Q[indices[i]][indices[perm[i]]]
                det += term
            minors.append(det)
    answer.append({'Q':[str(q) for row in Q for q in row],
                   'minors':[str(m) for m in minors], 'psd':all(m>=0 for m in minors)})
print(json.dumps(answer,sort_keys=True,separators=(',',':')))
```

<a id="sec:introduction"></a>
### Introduction

Let $\mathbb S^n$ denote the real symmetric $n\times n$ matrices. A *spectrahedron* is a set defined by a finite affine linear matrix inequality; a *spectrahedral shadow* is a linear projection of such a set. Thus auxiliary variables may make a semidefinite representation possible even when no representation exists in the original variables. We study this distinction for one explicit hyperbolicity cone.

For a homogeneous real polynomial $p$ with $p(e)>0$, hyperbolicity with respect to $e$ means that $t\mapsto p(te-x)$ has only real roots for every $x$. We use the closed-cone convention $$K(p,e)=\{x:\text{every root of }t\mapsto p(te-x)
                    \text{ is nonnegative}\}.$$ In particular, zero roots are allowed.

The convexity of hyperbolicity cones goes back to Gårding (Gårding 1959) <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[garding1959](#ref-garding1959)]. In three variables, the Helton–Vinnikov theorem yields definite symmetric determinantal representations, and hence spectrahedral cones (Helton and Vinnikov 2007; Lewis, Parrilo, and Ramana 2005) <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[helton-vinnikov2007](#ref-helton-vinnikov2007), [lewis-parrilo-ramana2005](#ref-lewis-parrilo-ramana2005)]. Allowing auxiliary variables leads to a broader representation question. Netzer and Sanyal proved semidefinite liftability when every nonzero boundary point is a smooth point of the defining hyperbolic polynomial (Netzer and Sanyal 2015) <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[netzer-sanyal2015](#ref-netzer-sanyal2015)]. More recently, Scheiderer obtained second-order cone lifts under a Nash-smooth boundary hypothesis (Scheiderer 2025) <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[scheiderer2025](#ref-scheiderer2025)]. Our result instead concerns an explicit cone and gives its lift by a bounded-degree algebraic construction.

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
#### The cone and the main result

<a id="eq:p"></a>
For $y=(y_1,y_2,y_3)\in\mathbb R^3$, define <a id="eq:Q"></a>$$\label{eq:Q}
 Q(y)=\begin{pmatrix}
 y_1^2+y_2^2&-y_1y_2&-y_1y_3\\
 -y_1y_2&y_2^2+y_3^2&-y_2y_3\\
 -y_1y_3&-y_2y_3&y_3^2+y_1^2
 \end{pmatrix}.$$ The associated biquadratic form $z^TQ(y)z$ is the coefficient-one Choi–Lam form (Choi and Lam 1977, sec. 4) <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[choi-lam1977](#ref-choi-lam1977)]. For $a\in\mathbb R$, $r\in\mathbb R^3$, and $T\in\mathbb S^3$, set <a id="eq:Phi"></a>$$\label{eq:Phi}
 \Phi_y\!\begin{pmatrix}a&r^T\\r&T\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(Q(y)T)&-r^TQ(y)\\
 -Q(y)r&aQ(y)
 \end{pmatrix}.$$ This is a linear map from $\mathbb S^4$ to itself for each fixed $y$. On the space $\mathcal V=\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3$, define $$\label{eq:p}
 p(X,Z,y)=\det\!\left((\det X)Z-\Phi_y(\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X)\right),
 \qquad e=(I_4,I_4,0),$$ where $\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}X$ denotes the classical adjugate. Each matrix entry inside this determinant is homogeneous of degree five, and $p(e)=1$; hence $p$ is homogeneous of degree twenty. We write $K=K(p,e)$. The slice representation in Section <a href="#sec:slices" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:slices">2</a> will in particular verify that $p$ is hyperbolic with respect to $e$.

Identify $\mathcal V$ with $\mathbb R^{23}$ by listing the upper triangular entries of $X$, then those of $Z$, each in lexicographic order of $(i,j)$, followed by $y_1,y_2,y_3$.

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>
<div id="thm:main" class="theorem">

**Theorem 1**. *There exist fixed matrices $A_1,\ldots,A_{23},B_1,\ldots,B_{307}
\in\mathbb S^{100}$ such that, for every $x=(X,Z,y)\in\mathcal V$, $$x\in K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \text{there exists }v\in\mathbb R^{307}\text{ with }
 \sum_{\nu=1}^{23}x_\nu A_\nu+
 \sum_{j=1}^{307}v_jB_j\succeq0.$$ The equivalence holds on the entire closed cone, including points with singular $X$.*

</div>

The sizes in Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> are explicit bounds, with no minimality assertion. The matrices are specified by a finite coefficient rule in Section <a href="#sec:lift" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:lift">4</a>.

The cone in <a href="#eq:p" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:p">[eq:p]</a> was constructed in the companion manuscript (OpenAI 2026) <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[parent](#ref-parent)], whose Theorem 1.1 proves that it has no homogeneous semidefinite representation in its original twenty-three coordinates. This also excludes affine representations. Indeed, if a cone containing $0$ equals $\{x:A_0+L(x)\succeq0\}$, where $L$ is linear, then $A_0\succeq0$. For each point $x$ of the cone and $t>0$, we have $A_0/t+L(x)\succeq0$; letting $t\to\infty$ gives $L(x)\succeq0$. Conversely, $L(x)\succeq0$ implies $A_0+L(x)\succeq0$; thus the same cone has the homogeneous representation $L(x)\succeq0$. We use the companion result only for this comparison: the proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> below is self-contained. The companion also establishes hyperbolicity and, in Proposition 3.2, describes the part of $K$ with $X\succ0$. Our slice calculation recovers those two facts as part of the lift construction.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
#### Proof strategy

The starting point is a family of planes in $\mathbb R^3$: $$y=Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)
   =\eta(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)+\xi(0,0,1).$$ They cover all values of $y$. On each plane, the matrix $Q(y)$ has a Gram factorization linear in $(\eta,\xi)$ and trigonometric of degree three in $\theta$. This gives a $20\times20$ linear pencil $L_\theta$ whose positive semidefinite locus is exactly the corresponding slice of $K$, including its boundary.

The angular parameter does not itself furnish a finite linear matrix inequality. We instead introduce a real linear functional on a finite-dimensional space of trigonometric polynomials with coefficients linear in the slice variables. We require it to be nonnegative on $q^TL_\theta q$ for every vector $q$ of trigonometric polynomials of degree at most two. These requirements form one finite matrix inequality. Evaluation at a single angle provides a feasible functional for every point of $K$.

This use of a finite matrix to encode positivity against a space of test polynomials belongs to the moment and sum-of-squares approach to semidefinite representations (Lasserre 2001, 2009) <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[lasserre2001](#ref-lasserre2001), [lasserre2009](#ref-lasserre2009)]. Here exactness must hold at one fixed degree.

The main issue is the converse: an arbitrary feasible functional need not be evaluation at an angle, or integration against a measure. The degree-two matching identity in Section <a href="#sec:matching" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:matching">3</a> supplies enough test vectors to recover the matrix inequalities defining each slice. Its construction rotates a fixed complex Gram factor by a $2\times2$ unitary matrix. The rotation preserves the Gram matrix and reduces a cubic trigonometric pairing to a first harmonic. Matching its value and first derivative then identifies the pairing exactly. This bounded-degree construction is the principal additional algebraic ingredient of the lift. Section <a href="#sec:lift" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:lift">4</a> applies it to prove exactness for every feasible functional and completes the proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.

<a id="sec:slices"></a>
### Exact semidefinite representations on angular slices

We first construct a matrix pencil on each plane $y=Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)$. Its determinant will agree with $p$ on that plane. This will identify cone membership with positive semidefiniteness even when $X$ is singular.

For $\theta\in\mathbb R$, set $$c=\cos\theta,\qquad s=\sin\theta,\qquad w=e^{i\theta},
 \qquad d(\theta)=(c,s,0),\qquad k=(0,0,1),$$ and write $$Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)=\eta d(\theta)+\xi k
 \qquad (\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R).$$ These planes cover $\mathbb R^3$. We first factor $Q$ on each plane, using complex notation to keep the formulas short. For a complex matrix $F=(f_1,f_2)\in\mathbb C^{3\times2}$, define its realification by $$\widetilde F=(\operatorname{Re}f_1,\operatorname{Im}f_1,
                 \operatorname{Re}f_2,\operatorname{Im}f_2).$$ Thus $\widetilde F\widetilde F^T=\operatorname{Re}(FF^*)$, where $*$ denotes conjugate transpose. Define <a id="eq:U"></a>$$\label{eq:U}
 U(\theta)=\frac1{\sqrt2}
 \begin{pmatrix}
 -w^{-1}&-w\\
 sw^{-2}&sw^2\\
 cw^{-2}&-cw^2
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad
 V_0=\frac1{\sqrt2}
 \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\ i&i\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad F_\theta(\eta,\xi)=\eta U(\theta)+\xi V_0.$$

<a id="stmt-lem-gram"></a>
<div id="lem:gram" class="lemma">

**Lemma 2** (Angular Gram factorization). *For every $\theta,\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R$, <a id="eq:gram"></a>$$\label{eq:gram}
 \widetilde F_\theta(\eta,\xi)
 \widetilde F_\theta(\eta,\xi)^T
 =Q\bigl(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)\bigr).$$ In particular, $Q(y)\succeq0$ for every $y\in\mathbb R^3$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-gram"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-gram</summary>

*Proof.* The three coefficients in the quadratic expansion of $\operatorname{Re}(F_\theta F_\theta^*)$ are $$\begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{Re}(UU^*)&=
 \begin{pmatrix}1&-sc&0\\-sc&s^2&0\\0&0&c^2\end{pmatrix},
 &\operatorname{Re}(V_0V_0^*)&=
 \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\\
 \operatorname{Re}(UV_0^*+V_0U^*)&=
 \begin{pmatrix}0&0&-c\\0&0&-s\\-c&-s&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}$$ For the $(2,3)$ entry of the last matrix, the multiplication gives $s\cos(2\theta)-c\sin(2\theta)=-s$. The sum of these matrices with coefficients $\eta^2,\xi^2,\eta\xi$ is exactly $Q(\eta c,\eta s,\xi)$. Every real $y$ has this form, so the factorization also proves positive semidefiniteness. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-6"></a>
#### The slice pencil

<a id="eq:Phi-factor"></a>
<a id="eq:Phi-quadratic"></a>
For a real column $r\in\mathbb R^3$ and a real vector $u=(u_0,u')\in\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^3$, define <a id="eq:BJ"></a>$$\label{eq:BJ}
 B(r)=\begin{pmatrix}0&r^T\\-r&0_{3\times3}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad J_u=\begin{pmatrix}u'&-u_0I_3\end{pmatrix}.$$ Then $B(r)u=J_u^Tr$. If $r_1,\ldots,r_4$ are the columns of $\widetilde F_\theta(\eta,\xi)$, direct multiplication and Lemma <a href="#lem:gram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:gram">2</a> give $$\label{eq:Phi-factor}
 \sum_{j=1}^4 B(r_j)^TWB(r_j)
       =\Phi_{Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)}(W)
       \qquad(W\in\mathbb S^4).$$ Indeed, for $W=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&v^T\\v&T\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, the individual summands have blocks $r_j^TTr_j$, $-r_j^Tv\,r_j^T$, $-r_jr_j^Tv$, and $a r_jr_j^T$. Summing uses $\sum_jr_jr_j^T=Q(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi))$. Consequently, for every $y\in\mathbb R^3$, $$\label{eq:Phi-quadratic}
 u^T\Phi_y(W)u=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(J_uWJ_u^TQ(y)).$$ Here we used that the angular planes cover $\mathbb R^3$.

<a id="eq:slice-pencil"></a>
Write $\mathcal X,\mathcal Z\in\mathbb S^4$ for the matrix variables on a slice, and define $$\label{eq:slice-pencil}
 L_\theta(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)
 =\begin{pmatrix}
 I_4\otimes\mathcal X&\mathcal B_\theta(\eta,\xi)\\
 \mathcal B_\theta(\eta,\xi)^T&\mathcal Z
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \mathcal B_\theta(\eta,\xi)=
 \begin{pmatrix}B(r_1)\\B(r_2)\\B(r_3)\\B(r_4)\end{pmatrix}.$$ For fixed $\theta$, this is a homogeneous real symmetric linear pencil of size twenty. Its coefficients are trigonometric polynomials in $\theta$ of degree at most three.

<a id="stmt-lem-slice"></a>
<div id="lem:slice" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:slice-equivalence"></a>
**Lemma 3**. *For every $\theta,\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R$ and $\mathcal X,\mathcal Z\in\mathbb S^4$, $$\label{eq:slice-equivalence}
 (\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,Y_\theta(\eta,\xi))\in K
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 L_\theta(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)\succeq0.$$ In particular, $p$ is hyperbolic with respect to $e$. If $\mathcal X\succ0$, the conditions in <a href="#eq:slice-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-equivalence">[eq:slice-equivalence]</a> are also equivalent to $\mathcal Z\succeq\Phi_{Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)}(\mathcal X^{-1})$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-slice"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-slice</summary>

*Proof.* For invertible $\mathcal X$, the block determinant formula and <a href="#eq:Phi-factor" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:Phi-factor">[eq:Phi-factor]</a> show that $$\begin{aligned}
 \det L_\theta
 &= (\det\mathcal X)^4
    \det\!\left(\mathcal Z-
        \Phi_{Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)}(\mathcal X^{-1})\right)\\
 &=p(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)).
\end{aligned}$$ The second equality uses $\mathop{\mathrm{adj}}\mathcal X=(\det\mathcal X)\mathcal X^{-1}$ and linearity of $\Phi_y$ in its matrix argument. Both sides are polynomials in the slice variables, so the identity also holds for singular $\mathcal X$. Moreover, $L_\theta(I_4,I_4,0,0)=I_{20}$. Thus <a id="eq:roots"></a>$$\label{eq:roots}
 p\!\left(te-(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,Y_\theta(\eta,\xi))\right)
 =\det\!\left(tI_{20}-L_\theta(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)\right).$$ The roots are the real eigenvalues of this symmetric pencil evaluated at the specified point. Since every $y$ lies on some angular plane, this proves hyperbolicity. Nonnegativity of all these eigenvalues, including zero eigenvalues, proves <a href="#eq:slice-equivalence" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-equivalence">[eq:slice-equivalence]</a>. For $\mathcal X\succ0$, the final assertion is the Schur complement criterion applied to <a href="#eq:slice-pencil" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:slice-pencil">[eq:slice-pencil]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:matching"></a>
### A degree-two matching identity

Write $$Q(y,z)=\tfrac12\bigl(Q(y+z)-Q(y)-Q(z)\bigr)$$ for the symmetric bilinear polarization of $Q$, so that $Q(y,y)=Q(y)$.

For $S\in\mathbb S^3$ and $F,H\in\mathbb C^{3\times2}$, put $$\langle F,H\rangle_S
 =\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}(F^*SH)
 =\operatorname{tr}(\widetilde F^T S\widetilde H).$$ This is a symmetric real bilinear form. A complex trigonometric polynomial of degree at most $j$ is a linear combination of $e^{i\ell\theta}$ for $-j\leq\ell\leq j$.

The finite lift will test $L_\theta$ against trigonometric polynomial vectors built from the columns of a matrix $\widetilde C(\theta)$. A constant Gram matrix for $\widetilde C$ makes the coefficient of $\mathcal X$ in the quadratic contribution of the top blocks independent of $\theta$. Requiring $\langle F_\theta,C\rangle_S=\operatorname{tr}(SQ(Y_\theta,z))$ makes the mixed contribution linear in $Y_\theta$. The next lemma achieves both properties with entries of degree at most two. The matrix $S$ in the pairing may be indefinite.

<a id="stmt-lem-matching"></a>
<div id="lem:matching" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:matching"></a>
**Lemma 4** (Degree-two matching). *Let $S\in\mathbb S^3$ and let $z\in\mathbb R^3$ satisfy $(z_1,z_2)\ne(0,0)$. There is a matrix $C(\theta)\in\mathbb C^{3\times2}$ whose entries are trigonometric polynomials of degree at most two such that, for all $\theta,\eta,\xi\in\mathbb R$, $$\label{eq:matching}
 \begin{aligned}
 \widetilde C(\theta)\widetilde C(\theta)^T&=Q(z),\\
 \langle F_\theta(\eta,\xi),C(\theta)\rangle_S
 &=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(SQ(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi),z)\bigr).
 \end{aligned}$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-matching"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-matching</summary>

*Proof.* Write $z=\tau d(\alpha)+\rho k$, where $\tau>0$, and set $$w_0=e^{i\alpha},\qquad R_0=\tau U(\alpha)+\rho V_0,
 \qquad N=SR_0.$$ By Lemma <a href="#lem:gram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:gram">2</a>, $\widetilde R_0\widetilde R_0^T=Q(z)$. We will set $C(\theta)=R_0M(\theta)$ with $M(\theta)$ unitary, so that this Gram matrix remains fixed. We choose the rotation to make $\langle V_0,C\rangle_S$ constant and $\langle U(\theta),C(\theta)\rangle_S$ a linear combination of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$. After those frequency bounds are established, values and a derivative at $\alpha$ will identify the pairings.

<a id="eq:rotation-pairings"></a>
*Choosing the unitary rotation.* Write $N_j$ for the $j$th row of $N$ and introduce the two-component row $$v=N_3-iN_2.$$ Subscripts $+$ and $-$ denote the first and second components of a row. Set $$g=v_++\overline{v_-},\qquad
 h=v_+-\overline{v_-},\qquad
 \kappa(\theta)=(w_0/w)^2,\qquad
 \delta=|g|^2+|h|^2.$$ We seek a matrix of the form $$M(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-\overline b&\overline a\end{pmatrix}.$$ Writing $v^M=vM$, we impose the two conditions $$\label{eq:rotation-pairings}
 v^M_++\overline{v^M_-}=g,
 \qquad
 v^M_+-\overline{v^M_-}=h\kappa.$$ The sum on the left controls the pairing with $V_0$, while the difference multiplies $w^3$ in the pairing with $U$. These conditions therefore keep the first pairing constant and lower the cubic term of the second to frequency one, since $w^3\kappa=w_0^2w$. The expansion below will also bound its remaining frequencies.

<a id="eq:rotation-system"></a>
The two left sides of <a href="#eq:rotation-pairings" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:rotation-pairings">[eq:rotation-pairings]</a> are $ga+\overline h\,\overline b$ and $ha-\overline g\,\overline b$, respectively. Thus, for $\delta>0$, we choose $a(\theta)$ and $b(\theta)$ by solving $$\label{eq:rotation-system}
 \begin{pmatrix}g&\overline h\\h&-\overline g\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}a\\\overline b\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}g\\h\kappa\end{pmatrix}.$$ The coefficient matrix $A$ satisfies $A^*A=\delta I_2$, so the system has a unique solution. Explicitly, <a id="eq:rotation-coefficients"></a>$$\label{eq:rotation-coefficients}
 a=\frac{|g|^2+|h|^2\kappa}{\delta},\qquad
 \overline b=\frac{gh(1-\kappa)}{\delta}.$$ Since the right side of <a href="#eq:rotation-system" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:rotation-system">[eq:rotation-system]</a> has squared norm $\delta$, we have $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$. For $\delta=0$, take $a=1$ and $b=0$; then $g=h=0$ and the same system is satisfied. In both cases $M$ is unitary, $M(\alpha)=I_2$, and $a,\overline b$ involve only the frequencies $0$ and $-2$. Thus $C=R_0M$ has degree at most two and $C(\alpha)=R_0$. Moreover, $$\widetilde C\widetilde C^T
 =\operatorname{Re}(R_0MM^*R_0^*)
 =\operatorname{Re}(R_0R_0^*)=Q(z),$$ which proves the first identity in <a href="#eq:matching" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:matching">[eq:matching]</a>.

*Reducing the frequencies of the pairings.* The formula for $V_0$ and <a href="#eq:rotation-pairings" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:rotation-pairings">[eq:rotation-pairings]</a> yield $$\sqrt2\,\langle V_0,C\rangle_S
 =\operatorname{Re}(v^M_++v^M_-)=\operatorname{Re}g,$$ so this pairing is constant.

<a id="eq:frequency-reduction"></a>
Set $m=N_3+iN_2$, $n=N_1$, and write $m^M=mM$, $n^M=nM$. Expanding $c$ and $s$ in powers of $w$, the formula for $U$ gives $$\begin{aligned}
 \sqrt2\,\langle U(\theta),C(\theta)\rangle_S
 =\operatorname{Re}\bigl[{}
 &\tfrac12w^3v^M_+
 +w(\tfrac12m^M_+-n^M_+)\\
 &-\tfrac12w^{-3}v^M_-
 +w^{-1}(-\tfrac12m^M_--n^M_-)
 \bigr].
\end{aligned}$$ Inside the real part, we may replace each negative-power term by its complex conjugate. The resulting expression is $$\label{eq:frequency-reduction}
 \operatorname{Re}\left[
 \frac{w^3}{2}(v^M_+-\overline{v^M_-})
 +w\left(\frac{m^M_+}{2}-n^M_+
          -\frac{\overline{m^M_-}}{2}-\overline{n^M_-}\right)
 \right].$$ The first term has frequency one because $w^3\kappa=w_0^2w$. In the second term, the expression in parentheses has only frequencies $0$ and $-2$: for any fixed row $r$, $$(rM)_+=r_+a-r_-\overline b,
 \qquad
 \overline{(rM)_-}=\overline{r_+}\,\overline b+\overline{r_-}a.$$ Consequently <a href="#eq:frequency-reduction" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:frequency-reduction">[eq:frequency-reduction]</a> is a real linear combination of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$, as required.

<a id="eq:matching-value-v"></a>
<a id="eq:matching-value-u"></a>
*Identifying the pairings.* At $\theta=\alpha$, polarization of Lemma <a href="#lem:gram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:gram">2</a> within the plane spanned by $d(\alpha)$ and $k$ gives $$\begin{aligned}
 \langle V_0,R_0\rangle_S
 &=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(SQ(k,z)\bigr),\label{eq:matching-value-v}\\
 \langle U(\alpha),R_0\rangle_S
 &=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(SQ(d(\alpha),z)\bigr).
 \label{eq:matching-value-u}
\end{aligned}$$ Both sides of the desired identity $\langle V_0,C(\theta)\rangle_S=\operatorname{tr}(SQ(k,z))$ are constant, so <a href="#eq:matching-value-v" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:matching-value-v">[eq:matching-value-v]</a> proves it for every $\theta$.

<a id="eq:matching-moving-factor"></a>
For the pairing with $U$, both $\langle U(\theta),C(\theta)\rangle_S$ and $\operatorname{tr}(SQ(d(\theta),z))$ are linear combinations of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$. They agree at $\alpha$ by <a href="#eq:matching-value-u" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:matching-value-u">[eq:matching-value-u]</a>; it remains to compare their derivatives there. The fixed Gram matrix of $C$ gives $$\langle C(\theta),C(\theta)\rangle_S
 =\operatorname{tr}(SQ(z)),
 \qquad
 \langle R_0,C'(\alpha)\rangle_S=0.$$ Constancy of the pairing with $V_0$ also gives $\langle V_0,C'(\alpha)\rangle_S=0$. Since $R_0=\tau U(\alpha)+\rho V_0$ and $\tau>0$, it follows that $$\label{eq:matching-moving-factor}
 \langle U(\alpha),C'(\alpha)\rangle_S=0.$$ Next differentiate the identity $$\langle\tau U(\theta)+\rho V_0,
          \tau U(\theta)+\rho V_0\rangle_S
 =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(SQ(\tau d(\theta)+\rho k)\bigr),$$ which follows from Lemma <a href="#lem:gram" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:gram">2</a>. Evaluating at $\alpha$ and dividing by $2\tau$ gives $$\langle U'(\alpha),R_0\rangle_S
 =\operatorname{tr}\bigl(SQ(d'(\alpha),z)\bigr).$$ Together with <a href="#eq:matching-moving-factor" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:matching-moving-factor">[eq:matching-moving-factor]</a>, this is the required derivative identity. A real linear combination of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ is determined by its value and derivative at a single angle. Hence the pairing with $U$ has the desired value for all $\theta$. Linearity in $\eta,\xi$ completes the proof. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:lift"></a>
### A finite semidefinite lift

We now replace the angular family of pencils by one finite matrix condition. Evaluation on an angular slice will provide a feasible lift for each point of $K$. Conversely, the functions supplied by Lemma <a href="#lem:matching" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:matching">4</a> will turn positivity of the finite matrix into the inequalities that characterize the slice, including its singular boundary.

<a id="pm-h-9"></a>
#### The matrix condition

<a id="eq:output"></a>
<a id="eq:moment"></a>
Let $\mathcal T_j$ be the real vector space of trigonometric polynomials of degree at most $j$, with basis $$1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\ldots,\cos(j\theta),\sin(j\theta).$$ Let $\mathcal E$ be the space of real functions of $(\theta,\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)$ that are linear homogeneous in the $22$ coordinates of $(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)\in
\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^2$, with coefficients in $\mathcal T_7$. Thus $\dim\mathcal E=22\cdot15=330$. Our lifting variable is a real linear functional $\Lambda:\mathcal E\to\mathbb R$; on vectors and matrices we apply it entrywise. For an output point $(X,Z,y)$, impose $$\label{eq:output}
  \Lambda(\mathcal X)=X,\qquad
  \Lambda(\mathcal Z)=Z,\qquad
  \Lambda\bigl(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)\bigr)=y.$$ We also require $$\label{eq:moment}
  \Lambda\bigl(q(\theta)^T
  L_\theta(\mathcal X,\mathcal Z,\eta,\xi)q(\theta)\bigr)\geq0
  \qquad\text{for every }q\in(\mathcal T_2)^{20}.$$ The expression to which $\Lambda$ is applied lies in $\mathcal E$: the entries of $L_\theta$ have trigonometric degree at most three, so its product with two entries of $q$ has degree at most seven.

<a id="eq:moment-matrix"></a>
Condition <a href="#eq:moment" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment">[eq:moment]</a> is a single finite semidefinite condition, using the finite test-space construction familiar from moment relaxations (Lasserre 2001, 2009) <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[lasserre2001](#ref-lasserre2001), [lasserre2009](#ref-lasserre2009)]. Indeed, set $(f_1,\ldots,f_5)=
(1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos2\theta,\sin2\theta)$ and define $H(\Lambda)\in\mathbb S^{100}$ by $$\label{eq:moment-matrix}
  H(\Lambda)_{(i,a),(j,b)}
    =\Lambda\bigl(f_a f_b (L_\theta)_{ij}\bigr),
  \qquad 1\leq i,j\leq20,\quad 1\leq a,b\leq5.$$ If $q_i=\sum_a t_{i,a}f_a$, the left side of <a href="#eq:moment" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment">[eq:moment]</a> is $t^TH(\Lambda)t$. Hence <a href="#eq:moment" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment">[eq:moment]</a> is equivalent to $H(\Lambda)\succeq0$. Every entry of this real symmetric matrix is linear homogeneous in the $330$ coordinates of $\Lambda$.

<div class="samepage">

<a id="stmt-prop-lift"></a>
<div id="prop:lift" class="proposition">

**Proposition 5**. *For $(X,Z,y)\in\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb S^4\times\mathbb R^3$, the following are equivalent:*

1.  *$(X,Z,y)\in K$;*

2.  *there is a real linear functional $\Lambda:\mathcal E\to\mathbb R$ satisfying <a href="#eq:output" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:output">[eq:output]</a> and $H(\Lambda)\succeq0$.*

</div>

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-lift"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-lift</summary>

*Proof.* Suppose first that $(X,Z,y)\in K$. Choose $\theta_0,\eta_0,\xi_0$ with $y=Y_{\theta_0}(\eta_0,\xi_0)$, and let $\Lambda$ be evaluation at $(\theta_0,X,Z,\eta_0,\xi_0)$. The output equations hold, and Lemma <a href="#lem:slice" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:slice">3</a> gives $L_{\theta_0}(X,Z,\eta_0,\xi_0)\succeq0$, which implies <a href="#eq:moment" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment">[eq:moment]</a>. This evaluation functional is a finite feasible witness even when $X$ is singular.

For the converse, suppose that $\Lambda$ satisfies the stated conditions. Taking constant $q$ supported on one of the four top blocks of $L_\theta$ in <a href="#eq:moment" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment">[eq:moment]</a> gives $X\succeq0$. We next obtain a family of scalar inequalities from which the remaining slice conditions will follow.

<a id="eq:certificate"></a>
Fix $u\in\mathbb R^4$, $D\in\mathbb S^4$, and $z\in\mathbb R^3$ with $(z_1,z_2)\neq(0,0)$, and apply Lemma <a href="#lem:matching" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:matching">4</a> with $S=J_uDJ_u^T\in\mathbb S^3$. Neither $D$ nor $S$ is required to be positive semidefinite. Let $b_1(\theta),\ldots,b_4(\theta)$ be the columns of the resulting real matrix $\widetilde C(\theta)$. Choose the five four-dimensional blocks of $q$ to be $$q(\theta)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    -DJ_u^Tb_1(\theta)\\
    \vdots\\
    -DJ_u^Tb_4(\theta)\\
    u
  \end{pmatrix}.$$ The degree bound in Lemma <a href="#lem:matching" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:matching">4</a> puts this vector in $(\mathcal T_2)^{20}$. Write $r_j$ for the columns of $\widetilde F_\theta(\eta,\xi)$, as in the definition of $L_\theta$. Using $B(r_j)u=J_u^Tr_j$, the top blocks and the cross terms of $q^TL_\theta q$ are respectively $$\begin{aligned}
  \sum_{j=1}^4 b_j^T J_uD\mathcal X DJ_u^Tb_j
    &=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(J_uD\mathcal X DJ_u^TQ(z)\bigr),\\
  -2\sum_{j=1}^4 b_j^TJ_uDJ_u^Tr_j
    &=-2\langle F_\theta(\eta,\xi),C(\theta)\rangle_S\\
    &=-2\operatorname{tr}\bigl(J_uDJ_u^T
        Q(Y_\theta(\eta,\xi),z)\bigr).
\end{aligned}$$ These identities hold before applying $\Lambda$. The first identity removes the angular dependence from the coefficient of $\mathcal X$; the last expression is linear in $Y_\theta(\eta,\xi)$. Consequently, applying <a href="#eq:output" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:output">[eq:output]</a> and <a href="#eq:moment" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment">[eq:moment]</a> gives $$\label{eq:certificate}
  u^TZu+
  \operatorname{tr}\bigl(J_uDXDJ_u^TQ(z)\bigr)
  -2\operatorname{tr}\bigl(J_uDJ_u^TQ(y,z)\bigr)\geq0.$$ For fixed $u,D$, the left side is a polynomial in $z$, so the inequality extends by continuity to every $z\in\mathbb R^3$. Only this scalar inequality is extended; no continuity of the functions $C$ is needed. Thus <a href="#eq:certificate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:certificate">[eq:certificate]</a> holds for all $u\in\mathbb R^4$, $D\in\mathbb S^4$, and $z\in\mathbb R^3$.

<a id="eq:range"></a>
If $X$ were positive definite, setting $z=y$ and $D=X^{-1}$ in <a href="#eq:certificate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:certificate">[eq:certificate]</a> would give $Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^{-1})$. For singular $X$, the same family of tests also supplies the necessary range condition. Fix slice parameters $y=Y_{\theta_0}(\eta_0,\xi_0)$ and, for the rest of the proof, let $r_j$ denote the columns of $\widetilde F_{\theta_0}(\eta_0,\xi_0)$ and set $B_j=B(r_j)$. For $v\in\ker X$, take $D=t vv^T$ and $z=y$ in <a href="#eq:certificate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:certificate">[eq:certificate]</a>, where $t\in\mathbb R$ is arbitrary. Since $DXD=0$, this gives $$u^TZu-2t\,(J_uv)^TQ(y)(J_uv)\geq0
  \qquad(t\in\mathbb R).$$ Its coefficient of $t$ must vanish. The Gram factorization of $Q(y)$ therefore yields $$0=(J_uv)^TQ(y)(J_uv)
    =\sum_{j=1}^4(v^TB_ju)^2.$$ As $u$ and $v\in\ker X$ are arbitrary, it follows that $$\label{eq:range}
  \operatorname{range}B_j\subseteq(\ker X)^\perp
    =\operatorname{range}X\qquad(1\leq j\leq4).$$

<a id="eq:singular-schur"></a>
Let $X^\dagger$ be the symmetric matrix that inverts the positive eigenvalues of $X$ and is zero on $\ker X$. In particular, $X^\dagger XX^\dagger=X^\dagger$. Taking $D=X^\dagger$ and $z=y$ in <a href="#eq:certificate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:certificate">[eq:certificate]</a>, and using <a href="#eq:Phi-quadratic" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:Phi-quadratic">[eq:Phi-quadratic]</a> and <a href="#eq:Phi-factor" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:Phi-factor">[eq:Phi-factor]</a>, gives $$\label{eq:singular-schur}
  Z\succeq\Phi_y(X^\dagger)
    =\sum_{j=1}^4B_j^TX^\dagger B_j.$$ We now verify positivity of the slice pencil directly by completing a square using <a href="#eq:range" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:range">[eq:range]</a> and <a href="#eq:singular-schur" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:singular-schur">[eq:singular-schur]</a>. For $a_1,\ldots,a_4,u\in\mathbb R^4$, its quadratic form equals $$\begin{aligned}
  &\sum_{j=1}^4a_j^TXa_j
    +2\sum_{j=1}^4a_j^TB_ju+u^TZu\\
  &\quad=\sum_{j=1}^4
    (a_j+X^\dagger B_ju)^TX(a_j+X^\dagger B_ju)
    +u^T\left(Z-\sum_{j=1}^4B_j^TX^\dagger B_j\right)u
    \geq0.
\end{aligned}$$ Here the cross terms agree because $XX^\dagger B_j=B_j$ by <a href="#eq:range" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:range">[eq:range]</a>. Thus $L_{\theta_0}(X,Z,\eta_0,\xi_0)\succeq0$, and Lemma <a href="#lem:slice" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:slice">3</a> gives $(X,Z,y)\in K$. ◻

</details>

The converse has used an arbitrary feasible functional $\Lambda$. In particular, the proof requires no representation of $\Lambda$ by a measure and no closure operation on the projected feasible set.

<a id="pm-h-10"></a>
#### The size of the lift

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-lift-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-lift</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.* Choose the displayed trigonometric basis of $\mathcal T_7$ and the upper-triangular coordinate functions of $\mathcal X$ and $\mathcal Z$, together with $\eta,\xi$, to specify the $330$ coordinates of $\Lambda$. The output equations <a href="#eq:output" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:output">[eq:output]</a> prescribe precisely the following $23$ distinct coordinates: $$\begin{gathered}
  \Lambda(\mathcal X_{ij}),\quad
  \Lambda(\mathcal Z_{ij})\qquad(1\leq i\leq j\leq4),\\
  \Lambda(\eta\cos\theta),\quad
  \Lambda(\eta\sin\theta),\quad
  \Lambda(\xi).
\end{gathered}$$ They are independent members of the chosen coordinate system on $\mathcal E^*$. Substitute the corresponding entries of $X,Z,y$ for these coordinates in <a href="#eq:moment-matrix" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:moment-matrix">[eq:moment-matrix]</a>, and use the remaining $330-23=307$ coordinates as auxiliary variables. The result is a fixed real symmetric $100\times100$ matrix pencil, homogeneous in the output and auxiliary variables. Proposition <a href="#prop:lift" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:lift">5</a> proves that its projection is exactly $K$. Hence the requested representation has $N=100$, $m=307$, and zero constant term. ◻

</details>
