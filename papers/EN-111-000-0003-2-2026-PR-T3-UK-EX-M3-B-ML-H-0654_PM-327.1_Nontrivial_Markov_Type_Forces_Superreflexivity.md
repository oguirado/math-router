## 1. Abstract and result contract

> We prove that every real Banach space with Markov type <i>p</i> &gt; 1 is superreflexive. Together with the known converse, this characterizes superreflexivity by nontrivial Markov type. This answers Naor's question: every real Banach space with nontrivial Markov type admits an equivalent uniformly smooth norm.

- **thm:main** (theorem, [source](#stmt-thm-main)): Every real Banach space with Markov type $p$ for at least one $p>1$ is superreflexive.
  - Explanation: Nontrivial Markov type forces an equivalent uniformly convex norm for real Banach spaces.
- **lem:ordered-norm** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-ordered-norm)): If a real Banach space $X$ is not superreflexive, there is a norm $N$ on $c_{00}$ such that:* 1. *$N$ is spreading invariant;* 2. *merging two adjacent entries is contractive: $$\label{eq:merge} N(a_1,\ldots,a_{i-1},a_i+a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_m) \leq N(a_1,\ldots,a_m);$$* 3. *every initial coordinate span, equipped with $N$, admits linear embeddings into $X$ with distortion arbitrarily close to $1$.
  - Explanation: Failure of superreflexivity supplies a spreading norm with contractive adjacent merging and almost isometric finite-dimensional embeddings.
- **prop:walks** (construction, [source](#stmt-prop-walks)): Let $N$ be a spreading invariant norm on $c_{00}$ for which merging adjacent entries is contractive. For every integer $n\geq1$, there are a finite stationary reversible marked chain and an odd labeling $g$ in a finite coordinate span such that $$N(g(e))=1\qquad\text{for every edge }e,$$ and, for the first $n$ edges $E_1,\ldots,E_n$ of the stationary chain, $$\label{eq:unit-walk} \mathbb P\left\{N\left(\sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right) \geq\frac n{24}\right\}\geq\frac3{20}.$$
  - Explanation: A reversible marked walk has normalized odd labels and linear displacement with a fixed positive probability.
- **lem:edge-labels** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-edge-labels)): Suppose $V$ is a normed space of Markov type $p$ with constant $K$. For a finite marked chain satisfying <a href="#eq:edge-balance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:edge-balance">[eq:edge-balance]</a>, let $g:\mathcal E\to V$ satisfy $g(e^*)=-g(e)$. If $E_1,\ldots,E_n$ are its first $n$ edges, with initial state distributed as $\pi$, then $$\label{eq:odd-markov} \mathbb E\left\lVert \sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right\rVert^p \leq K^p n\,\mathbb E\left\lVert g(E_1)\right\rVert^p.$$ The same conclusion holds if the finite span of the labels is finitely representable in a space of Markov type $p$ with constant $K$.
  - Explanation: A finite lattice lift transfers Markov type from potential differences to sums of odd edge labels, including finitely representable label spans.
- **lem:order-averaging** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-order-averaging)): For every finite ordered set $\Lambda$ and every $\epsilon>0$, there are an integer $B$ and a probability distribution $\nu$ on charts such that, whenever $A,A'$ are increasing subtuples of $\Lambda$ of the same length, $$\label{eq:subtuple-TV} \operatorname{TV}\bigl(\operatorname{Law}_\nu(C(A)), \operatorname{Law}_\nu(C(A'))\bigr)<\epsilon.$$
  - Explanation: Ramsey averaging gives chart laws whose equal-length ordered subtuples have arbitrarily close marginal distributions.
- **prop:chart-transport** (construction, [source](#stmt-prop-chart-transport)): Let $\Lambda$ be a finite ordered set, and let $T_s:D_s\to R_s$ be order-preserving bijections between its subsets, indexed by a nonempty finite set $\mathcal S$ with a fixed-point-free involution $s\mapsto s^{-1}$ and $T_{s^{-1}}=T_s^{-1}$. For every $\epsilon>0$, there are an integer $B$ and a finite stationary reversible marked chain on charts $C:\Lambda\to\{1,\ldots,B\}$ whose successive edge letters are independent and uniform in $\mathcal S$. At every stationary step $(C,s,C')$, $$C'(a)=C(T_s(a))\quad(a\in D_s)$$ holds simultaneously with probability at least $1-\epsilon$.
  - Explanation: Coupled charts yield a stationary reversible marked chain with independent uniform letters and high-probability rank transport.
- **lem:action** (construction, [source](#stmt-lem-action)): There are increasing homeomorphisms $h_s:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ such that $$\begin{aligned} h_s(y+1)&=h_s(y)+1, & h_{s^{-1}}&=h_s^{-1},\label{eq:periodic-action}\\ h_s(\mathbb R\setminus\mathcal A_{s^{-1}})&=\mathcal A_s, & \|h_s(y)-y\|&<2.\label{eq:pingpong} \end{aligned}$$ For a word $w=s_1\cdots s_k$, write $h_w=h_{s_1}\circ\cdots\circ h_{s_k}$, with $h_\varnothing$ the identity. If $w$ is nonempty and reduced, then $$\label{eq:interior} h_w(0)\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal A_{s_1});$$ in particular, $h_w(0)\notin\mathbb Z$.
  - Explanation: Periodic increasing homeomorphisms have inverse pairing, bounded displacement and a ping-pong action on disjoint intervals.
- **lem:two-levels** (bound, [source](#stmt-lem-two-levels)): Let the reduced form of $w$ have length $\ell>0$, with $n_+$ positive letters and $n_-$ negative letters. For every $j\in\mathbb Z$, $$\label{eq:two-levels} c_w(j)=-n_-,\qquad c_w(h_w(0)+j)=n_+.$$
  - Explanation: The word cocycle has prescribed values on integer points and integer translates of the image of the origin, separated by the reduced word length.

## 2. Definitions, validity and bounds

- **Stationary reversible finite Markov chain** ([source](#eq:markov-type)): A finite Markov chain $(Z_t)_{t\geq0}$ on a set $S$, with transition matrix $P$ and initial distribution $\pi$, is *stationary and reversible* if $$\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}\qquad(i,j\in S).$$
- **Markov type** ([source](#eq:markov-type)): A normed space $X$ has *Markov type $p>0$ with constant $0<K<\infty$* if, for every such chain, every $f:S\to X$, and every integer $t\geq1$, $$\label{eq:markov-type} \mathbb E\left\lVert f(Z_t)-f(Z_0)\right\rVert^p \leq K^p t\,\mathbb E\left\lVert f(Z_1)-f(Z_0)\right\rVert^p.$$
- **Superreflexivity** ([source](#eq:markov-type)): A Banach space is *superreflexive* if it admits an equivalent uniformly convex norm.
- **Equivalent norm and uniform convexity** ([source](#eq:markov-type)): Here a norm $\|\cdot\|$ is equivalent to $\\|\cdot\\|$ if $a\\|x\\|\leq\|x\|\leq b\\|x\\|$ for some $a,b>0$; it is uniformly convex if, for every $\epsilon\in(0,2]$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $$\|x\|,\|y\|\leq1,\quad \|x-y\|\geq\epsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left\|\frac{x+y}{2}\right\|\leq1-\delta.$$
- **Finite representability** ([source](#eq:superreflexive-characterization)): Here $Y$ is *finitely representable* in $X$ if, for every finite-dimensional $E\subseteq Y$ and every $\eta>0$, there is an injective linear $T:E\to X$ with $\left\lVert T\right\rVert\left\lVert T^{-1}\right\rVert<1+\eta$.
- **Spreading invariant norm** ([source](#eq:spreading)): A norm $N$ on $c_{00}$ is *spreading invariant* if $$\label{eq:spreading} N\left(\sum_{i=1}^m a_i b_i\right) =N\left(\sum_{i=1}^m a_i b_{k_i}\right) \qquad(k_1<\cdots<k_m).$$
- **Finite marked chain** ([source](#eq:edge-balance)): A *finite marked chain* has a finite state set $S$ and a finite set of oriented edges $\mathcal E$. An edge $e$ has source $e^-$, target $e^+$, and reverse $e^*$, where star is an involution and $(e^*)^-=e^+$, $(e^*)^+=e^-$. The probability of choosing $e$ from its source is $q(e)$, with $\sum_{e:e^-=z}q(e)=1$ for every state $z$. We require a probability distribution $\pi$ satisfying $$\label{eq:edge-balance} \pi(e^-)q(e)=\pi(e^+)q(e^*).$$ The state chain is then stationary and reversible when started in $\pi$.
- **Odd labeling** ([source](#pm-h-7)): A labeling $g$ is *odd* if $g(e^*)=-g(e)$ for every edge.
- **Chart** ([source](#pm-h-11)): For a finite ordered set $\Lambda$, call a strictly increasing map $C:\Lambda\to\{1,\ldots,B\}$ a *chart*.
- **Reduced word** ([source](#sec:action)): A word is *reduced* if it has no adjacent inverse letters.
- **Periodic signed interval function** ([source](#eq:chi-cancellation)): Define the periodic integer-valued functions $$\label{eq:chi} \chi_s(y)=\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_s}(y)-\mathbf 1_{\{s\text{ is negative}\}}.$$
- **Subdivision limit norm** ([source](#app:norm)): It follows that $$\label{eq:N-definition} N(a)=\lim_{J\to\infty}N_0(F_Ja)$$ exists.

## 3. Proof roadmap

- **norm** [claim](#stmt-lem-ordered-norm) / [proof](#app:norm): Triangular arrays, Ramsey stabilization, diagonal limits and subdivision averaging. -> A spreading norm with contractive adjacent merging and finite-dimensional representability.
- **averaging** [claim](#stmt-lem-order-averaging) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-order-averaging): Finite Ramsey homogeneity and finite-dimensional separation. -> A chart distribution with close equal-length subtuple marginals.
- **transport** [claim](#stmt-prop-chart-transport) / [proof](#proof-stmt-prop-chart-transport): Maximal diagonal matching of marginals followed by inverse-symmetric coupling. -> A reversible stationary marked chain with independent uniform letters.
- **action** [claim](#stmt-lem-action) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-action): Affine interval maps extended periodically, with inverse maps for negative letters. -> An increasing periodic ping-pong action.
- **levels** [claim](#stmt-lem-two-levels) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-two-levels): Inverse cancellation and evaluation along reduced-word orbit points. -> Interlaced prefix levels separated by reduced length.
- **walk** [claim](#stmt-prop-walks) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-two-levels-2): Transport landmark ranks, compress consecutive coefficients, bound reduced length and normalize. -> Unit odd labels with linear displacement at uniform positive probability.
- **lift** [claim](#stmt-lem-edge-labels) / [proof](#proof-stmt-lem-edge-labels): Lift marked edges to lattice coordinates; reject boundary moves and let the cube grow. -> The powered-displacement inequality for label sums.
- **contradiction** [claim](#stmt-thm-main) / [proof](#pm-h-9): Combine the linear-displacement lower bound with the uniform Markov type upper bound. -> Superreflexivity for real Banach spaces with nontrivial Markov type.

## 4. Engineering Triptych

### Panel 1 - epistemic contract

- **I-main**: Every real Banach space with Markov type $p$ for at least one $p>1$ is superreflexive.
- **I-length**: The unconditioned reduced length has expected value bounded below by a constant fraction of the total length and exceeds a positive fraction of the length with probability bounded away from zero.
- **I-odd**: The edge-label inequality requires odd labels and stationary reversible edge balance.

### Panel 2 - negative knowledge

- **A-sample**: Infer superreflexivity from agreement on a finite collection of walks. -> Finite agreement does not establish the universal Markov type hypothesis.
- **A-condition**: Apply the uniform-letter reduced-length calculation after conditioning on good chart transitions. -> Conditioning can change the letter law and destroy the independence used in the calculation.
- **A-label**: Treat each marked edge label as the difference of a function on chart states. -> A marked transition can carry a nonzero label without changing its chart state.

### Panel 3 - reference implementation and oracle

Applicability: `algorithmic`. The finite reduced-word calculation in the walk proof is executable. Its probability and expectation bounds can be checked exactly on bounded word lengths; this does not certify the chart construction or the superreflexivity theorem.

```python
# INPUTS: A JSON integer n.
# PRECONDITIONS: n is an integer, not a Boolean, and 0 <= n <= 6.
# OUTPUTS: Exact counts of reduced lengths and a Boolean for the source bounds.
# TIME: O(n squared) exact integer operations.
# SPACE: O(n) exact integers.
# ARITHMETIC: Exact fmpz counts and fmpq probabilities; no floating point.
# FAILURE: Invalid or out-of-range input prints INVALID.
import json, sys
from flint import fmpz, fmpq
try:
    n = json.loads(sys.stdin.read())
except (ValueError, TypeError):
    n = None
if type(n) is not int or not 0 <= n <= 6:
    print("INVALID")
    sys.exit(0)
counts = [fmpz(1)]
for step in range(n):
    nxt = [fmpz(0) for _ in range(len(counts) + 1)]
    for length, mass in enumerate(counts):
        if length == 0:
            nxt[1] += 4 * mass
        else:
            nxt[length - 1] += mass
            nxt[length + 1] += 3 * mass
    counts = nxt
total = fmpz(4) ** n
mean = fmpq(sum(i * c for i, c in enumerate(counts)), total)
tail = fmpq(sum(c for i, c in enumerate(counts) if 4 * i >= n), total)
bounds = bool(mean >= fmpq(n, 2) and tail >= fmpq(1, 4))
print(json.dumps({"counts": [int(c) for c in counts], "bounds": bounds}, sort_keys=True))
```

  π : S → ℝ
  P : S → S → ℝ
  π_nonneg : ∀ s, 0 ≤ π s
  sum_π : ∑ s, π s = 1
  P_nonneg : ∀ s t, 0 ≤ P s t
  sum_P : ∀ s, ∑ t, P s t = 1
  balance : ∀ s t, π s * P s t = π t * P t s

abbrev StatePath (S : Type*) (n : ℕ) := Fin (n + 1) → S

    (C : FiniteReversibleChain S) (n : ℕ) (ω : StatePath S n) : ℝ :=
  C.π (ω 0) * ∏ i : Fin n, C.P (ω i.castSucc) (ω i.succ)

### Introduction

<a id="eq:markov-type"></a>
A finite Markov chain $(Z_t)_{t\geq0}$ on a set $S$, with transition matrix $P$ and initial distribution $\pi$, is *stationary and reversible* if $$\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}\qquad(i,j\in S).$$ A normed space $X$ has *Markov type $p>0$ with constant $0<K<\infty$* if, for every such chain, every $f:S\to X$, and every integer $t\geq1$, $$\label{eq:markov-type}
 \mathbb E\left\lVert f(Z_t)-f(Z_0)\right\rVert^p
 \leq K^p t\,\mathbb E\left\lVert f(Z_1)-f(Z_0)\right\rVert^p.$$ A Banach space is *superreflexive* if it admits an equivalent uniformly convex norm. Here a norm $|\cdot|$ is equivalent to $\|\cdot\|$ if $a\|x\|\leq|x|\leq b\|x\|$ for some $a,b>0$; it is uniformly convex if, for every $\epsilon\in(0,2]$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $$|x|,|y|\leq1,\quad |x-y|\geq\epsilon
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \left|\frac{x+y}{2}\right|\leq1-\delta.$$

<a id="stmt-thm-main"></a>

**Theorem 1**. *Every real Banach space with Markov type $p$ for at least one $p>1$ is superreflexive.*

</div>

<a id="pm-h-3"></a>
###### Context and result.

Ball introduced Markov type to study Lipschitz extension from metric spaces <a id="pm-cite-1"></a>[[Ball](#ref-Ball)]. The inequality tests every finite reversible walk, so it is a metric property even when the target is a Banach space. Superreflexivity, by contrast, is defined through an equivalent norm; its metric characterizations are central examples in the Ribe program, which seeks metric descriptions of linear-geometric properties. Bourgain characterized it by the distortion of finite complete binary trees <a id="pm-cite-2"></a>[[Bourgain](#ref-Bourgain)]; finite diamonds provide another metric test <a id="pm-cite-3"></a>[[JohnsonSchechtman](#ref-JohnsonSchechtman)]. Lee, Naor, and Peres introduced Markov convexity in their study of tree distortion <a id="pm-cite-4"></a>[[LeeNaorPeres](#ref-LeeNaorPeres)]. Mendel and Naor later characterized superreflexivity through *Markov convexity*, an inequality comparing walks that share their past and then evolve independently <a id="pm-cite-5"></a>[[MendelNaor](#ref-MendelNaor)]. Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> identifies what ordinary Markov type alone detects for Banach spaces. Ordinary Rademacher type, which controls sums with independent random signs, does not suffice for this conclusion: James constructed nonreflexive spaces of Rademacher type $2$ <a id="pm-cite-6"></a>[[JamesType2](#ref-JamesType2)].

Naor, Peres, Schramm, and Sheffield showed that uniform smoothness of power type $p$ implies Markov type $p$ for $1<p\leq2$ <a id="pm-cite-7"></a>[[NPSS](#ref-NPSS)]. Pisier’s power-type renorming theorem <a id="pm-cite-8"></a>[[Pisier](#ref-Pisier)] therefore gives nontrivial Markov type to every superreflexive space; Markov type is invariant under equivalent norms. Naor asked whether a Banach space could have nontrivial Markov type without an equivalent uniformly smooth norm <a id="pm-cite-9"></a>[[Naor](#ref-Naor), Question 4]. Since equivalent uniformly smooth and uniformly convex renormings exist for precisely the superreflexive spaces <a id="pm-cite-10"></a>[[Pisier](#ref-Pisier)], Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> answers this question negatively and completes the characterization for real Banach spaces.

<a id="eq:superreflexive-characterization"></a>
The classical structural input is the James–Enflo characterization: $$\label{eq:superreflexive-characterization}
 \begin{gathered}
 X\text{ admits an equivalent uniformly convex norm}\\
 \Longleftrightarrow\\
 \text{every Banach space finitely representable in }X
 \text{ is reflexive}.
 \end{gathered}$$ Here $Y$ is *finitely representable* in $X$ if, for every finite-dimensional $E\subseteq Y$ and every $\eta>0$, there is an injective linear $T:E\to X$ with $\left\lVert T\right\rVert\left\lVert T^{-1}\right\rVert<1+\eta$. We use <a href="#eq:superreflexive-characterization" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:superreflexive-characterization">[eq:superreflexive-characterization]</a> in its standard form <a id="pm-cite-11"></a>[[James](#ref-James), [Enflo](#ref-Enflo)]; the other background tools are Hahn–Banach and the finite Ramsey theorem. The finite-dimensional form of Goldstine’s theorem needed below is included in the proof.

<a id="pm-h-4"></a>
###### Proof strategy.

We argue by contradiction. The James–Enflo characterization supplies a nonreflexive space finitely representable in a nonsuperreflexive one. From it we construct a spreading norm $N$ on finite sequences for which summing consecutive entries is contractive. This is a concrete version of the equal-sign-additive construction of Brunel and Sucheston <a id="pm-cite-12"></a>[[BrunelSucheston1974](#ref-BrunelSucheston1974), [BrunelSucheston1975](#ref-BrunelSucheston1975), [BrunelSucheston](#ref-BrunelSucheston)]. Equal-sign additivity means that merging adjacent coefficients of the same sign preserves the norm. The finite-representability formulation and related graph-embedding applications appear in <a id="pm-cite-13"></a>[[Ostrovskii](#ref-Ostrovskii), [OstrovskiiRandrianantoanina](#ref-OstrovskiiRandrianantoanina)]. We include the precise version and its finite-representability proof in Appendix <a href="#app:norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="app:norm">6</a>.

For each time $n$, the central task is a finite reversible walk with unit edge labels, changing sign under reversal, whose sum has norm at least $n/24$ with probability at least $3/20$. Before normalization, the labels are sums of signed differences of standard basis vectors in finite coefficient sequences. Their prefix sums are signed indicators of unions of intervals. A reduced word acting on the line makes selected prefix sums of the total label alternate between two levels separated by its reduced length. Consecutive summation converts these oscillations into a lower bound proportional to the reduced length, measured in units of the common one-step label norm. Finite Ramsey averaging supplies increasing charts from a finite set of real points to coefficient indices, with matching marginal laws, so this ordered motion can occur along reversible transitions. The transition letters are independent and uniform; their reduced length is proportional to $n$ with a fixed positive probability.

An edge label need not be the difference of values assigned to its two chart states; a marked transition may carry a nonzero label even when the chart state does not change. A finite lattice lift records traversed oriented edges as integer coordinates. Its potential has exactly the required increments away from the boundary, and rejected boundary moves keep the lifted chain reversible. Section <a href="#sec:reduction" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:reduction">2</a> proves this transfer and reduces Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a> to the finite-walk target. Section <a href="#sec:finite-lemmas" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:finite-lemmas">3</a> constructs chart transport, Section <a href="#sec:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:action">4</a> constructs the two prefix levels, and Section <a href="#sec:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:walks">5</a> assembles the walk and proves its displacement.

<a id="sec:reduction"></a>
### Reduction to a finite walk

The reduction has three inputs. Lemma <a href="#lem:ordered-norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:ordered-norm">2</a> supplies an ordered norm from nonsuperreflexivity, Proposition <a href="#prop:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:walks">3</a> constructs a walk in that norm, and Lemma <a href="#lem:edge-labels" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:edge-labels">4</a> transfers the Markov type inequality to its odd edge labels.

<a id="pm-h-6"></a>
#### The ordered norm

<a id="eq:spreading"></a>
Write $c_{00}$ for the real sequences with finite support, and $b_i$ for the $i$th coordinate vector. Finite coefficient arrays are identified with elements of $c_{00}$ by appending zeros. A norm $N$ on $c_{00}$ is *spreading invariant* if $$\label{eq:spreading}
 N\left(\sum_{i=1}^m a_i b_i\right)
 =N\left(\sum_{i=1}^m a_i b_{k_i}\right)
 \qquad(k_1<\cdots<k_m).$$ Thus inserting or removing zero entries does not change the norm.

<a id="stmt-lem-ordered-norm"></a>
<div id="lem:ordered-norm" class="lemma">

**Lemma 2**. *If a real Banach space $X$ is not superreflexive, there is a norm $N$ on $c_{00}$ such that:*

1.  *$N$ is spreading invariant;*

<a id="eq:merge"></a>
2.  *merging two adjacent entries is contractive: $$\label{eq:merge}
      N(a_1,\ldots,a_{i-1},a_i+a_{i+1},a_{i+2},\ldots,a_m)
      \leq N(a_1,\ldots,a_m);$$*

3.  *every initial coordinate span, equipped with $N$, admits linear embeddings into $X$ with distortion arbitrarily close to $1$.*

</div>

The construction is proved in Appendix <a href="#app:norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="app:norm">6</a>. Repeated adjacent merging shows that replacing every block of a partition into consecutive intervals by its sum is contractive. Spreading invariance will allow us to place coordinates by increasing maps, while consecutive summation will convert differences of prefix sums into a norm lower bound.

<a id="pm-h-7"></a>
#### The finite-walk target

<a id="eq:edge-balance"></a>
A *finite marked chain* has a finite state set $S$ and a finite set of oriented edges $\mathcal E$. An edge $e$ has source $e^-$, target $e^+$, and reverse $e^*$, where star is an involution and $(e^*)^-=e^+$, $(e^*)^+=e^-$. The probability of choosing $e$ from its source is $q(e)$, with $\sum_{e:e^-=z}q(e)=1$ for every state $z$. We require a probability distribution $\pi$ satisfying $$\label{eq:edge-balance}
 \pi(e^-)q(e)=\pi(e^+)q(e^*).$$ The state chain is then stationary and reversible when started in $\pi$.

Marks allow several distinct edges between the same states. A labeling $g$ is *odd* if $g(e^*)=-g(e)$ for every edge.

<a id="stmt-prop-walks"></a>
<div id="prop:walks" class="proposition">

**Proposition 3** (A finite walk with linear displacement). *Let $N$ be a spreading invariant norm on $c_{00}$ for which merging adjacent entries is contractive. For every integer $n\geq1$, there are a finite stationary reversible marked chain and an odd labeling $g$ in a finite coordinate span such that $$N(g(e))=1\qquad\text{for every edge }e,$$ and, for the first $n$ edges $E_1,\ldots,E_n$ of the stationary chain, <a id="eq:unit-walk"></a>$$\label{eq:unit-walk}
 \mathbb P\left\{N\left(\sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right)
                 \geq\frac n{24}\right\}\geq\frac3{20}.$$*

</div>

The construction occupies Sections <a href="#sec:finite-lemmas" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:finite-lemmas">3</a>–<a href="#sec:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:walks">5</a>. The chain and coordinate dimension may depend on $n$; the numerical constants do not.

<a id="pm-h-8"></a>
#### Applying Markov type to edge labels

The labels in Proposition <a href="#prop:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:walks">3</a> need not be differences of a function on the chart states. A finite lattice lift will turn them into such differences and allow Markov type to control their sums.

The Markov type inequality passes to normed spaces finitely representable in its target, with the same constant. More precisely, only the span of the finitely many values of $f$ needs to admit embeddings with distortion arbitrarily close to $1$. After scaling such an embedding $T$, we have $$\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert Tx\right\rVert_X\leq(1+\eta)\left\lVert x\right\rVert,$$ so applying <a href="#eq:markov-type" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:markov-type">[eq:markov-type]</a> in $X$ gives its counterpart in the original target with constant $(1+\eta)K$. Let $\eta\downarrow0$.

<a id="stmt-lem-edge-labels"></a>
<div id="lem:edge-labels" class="lemma">

**Lemma 4** (Odd edge labels). *Suppose $V$ is a normed space of Markov type $p$ with constant $K$. For a finite marked chain satisfying <a href="#eq:edge-balance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:edge-balance">[eq:edge-balance]</a>, let $g:\mathcal E\to V$ satisfy $g(e^*)=-g(e)$. If $E_1,\ldots,E_n$ are its first $n$ edges, with initial state distributed as $\pi$, then <a id="eq:odd-markov"></a>$$\label{eq:odd-markov}
 \mathbb E\left\lVert \sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right\rVert^p
 \leq K^p n\,\mathbb E\left\lVert g(E_1)\right\rVert^p.$$ The same conclusion holds if the finite span of the labels is finitely representable in a space of Markov type $p$ with constant $K$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-edge-labels"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-edge-labels</summary>

*Proof.* Choose one edge $e_j$ from each two-element star orbit, $1\leq j\leq r$, and let $u_j$ be the coordinate vectors of $\mathbb Z^r$. Give $e_j$ displacement $u_j$ and $e_j^*$ displacement $-u_j$. A fixed point of star has label zero and is given displacement zero. If $r=0$, there is nothing to prove.

For an integer $R>n$, put $Q_R=\{-R,\ldots,R\}^r$. Lift the chain to $S\times Q_R$ by choosing an edge as before and making the corresponding lattice move. If the move would leave $Q_R$, remain at the same full state. Every accepted transition satisfies detailed balance under $\pi\otimes\operatorname{Unif}(Q_R)$ by <a href="#eq:edge-balance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:edge-balance">[eq:edge-balance]</a>; rejections add only self-loop probabilities. This is a finite stationary reversible chain.

Define $$\Phi(z,a)=\sum_{j=1}^r a_jg(e_j).$$ On an accepted edge its increment is $g(e)$, and on a rejection its increment is zero. The expected one-step cost is therefore at most $\mathbb E\left\lVert g(E_1)\right\rVert^p$. If the initial lattice point lies in $Q_{R-n}$, every possible sequence of $n$ edges is accepted. Couple the lifted walk to the original marked walk by using the same proposed edges until the first rejection. On this interior event the first $n$ edges agree, and the event is independent of the original marked trajectory. Its probability is $$\alpha_R=\left(\frac{2(R-n)+1}{2R+1}\right)^r\longrightarrow1.$$ Consequently the expected $n$-step cost of the lifted potential is at least $\alpha_R\mathbb E\left\lVert \sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right\rVert^p$. Applying Markov type to $\Phi$ gives $$\alpha_R\mathbb E\left\lVert \sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right\rVert^p
 \leq K^p n\,\mathbb E\left\lVert g(E_1)\right\rVert^p.$$ Let $R\to\infty$. The last assertion follows from finite-dimensional transfer; enlarging the cube does not enlarge the span of the labels. ◻

</details>

<a id="pm-h-9"></a>
#### Proof of the main theorem

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-edge-labels-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-edge-labels</summary>

*Proof of Theorem <a href="#thm:main" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:main">1</a>.* Suppose that $X$ has Markov type $p>1$ with constant $K$ and is not superreflexive. Lemma <a href="#lem:ordered-norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:ordered-norm">2</a> supplies a spreading invariant norm $N$ with contractive adjacent merging, whose finite coordinate spans embed in $X$ with arbitrarily small distortion. For each $n$, apply Proposition <a href="#prop:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:walks">3</a> to $N$. Lemma <a href="#lem:edge-labels" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:edge-labels">4</a>, applied through the finite span of these labels, gives <a id="eq:contradiction"></a>$$\label{eq:contradiction}
 \frac3{20}\left(\frac n{24}\right)^p
 \leq\mathbb EN\left(\sum_{i=1}^n g(E_i)\right)^p
 \leq K^p n.$$ Since $n$ is arbitrary and $K$ is fixed, this contradicts $p>1$. ◻

</details>

All chains used in this deduction are finite and start in their reversible stationary distributions. Their sizes and the embeddings into $X$ may depend on $n$; only $K$ must be uniform. It remains to construct the walks in Proposition <a href="#prop:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:walks">3</a>. The proof of Lemma <a href="#lem:ordered-norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:ordered-norm">2</a>, deferred until Appendix <a href="#app:norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="app:norm">6</a>, completes the other construction used here.

<a id="sec:finite-lemmas"></a>
### Finite charts for ordered motion

To place ordered motion in a finite stationary chain, we seek random increasing maps with the following property: an increasing partial bijection $T$ can be matched by two maps with the same marginal law, so that $C'(a)=C(T(a))$ throughout its domain with high probability. Thus a landmark moving from $T(a)$ to $a$ retains its coordinate index as the chart changes from $C$ to $C'$.

<a id="pm-h-11"></a>
#### Averaging on a finite order

For a finite ordered set $\Lambda$, call a strictly increasing map $C:\Lambda\to\{1,\ldots,B\}$ a *chart*. If $A$ is an increasing subtuple of $\Lambda$, then $C(A)$ denotes its ordered tuple of values. For probability distributions on a finite set we use $\operatorname{TV}(\mu,\mu')=\frac12\sum_x|\mu(x)-\mu'(x)|$.

<a id="stmt-lem-order-averaging"></a>
<div id="lem:order-averaging" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:subtuple-TV"></a>
**Lemma 5** (Finite order averaging). *For every finite ordered set $\Lambda$ and every $\epsilon>0$, there are an integer $B$ and a probability distribution $\nu$ on charts such that, whenever $A,A'$ are increasing subtuples of $\Lambda$ of the same length, $$\label{eq:subtuple-TV}
 \operatorname{TV}\bigl(\operatorname{Law}_\nu(C(A)),
          \operatorname{Law}_\nu(C(A'))\bigr)<\epsilon.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-order-averaging"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-order-averaging</summary>

*Proof.* List all nontrivial ordered comparisons $(A_\rho,A'_\rho)$ of equal-length subtuples, $1\leq\rho\leq R$. The assertion is immediate if $R=0$. Let $m_\rho$ be their common length, and let $T_{B,m}$ denote the increasing $m$-tuples in $\{1,\ldots,B\}$. For each chart $C$, form the vector $$v_C=\bigl(\delta_{C(A_\rho)}-\delta_{C(A'_\rho)}\bigr)_{\rho=1}^R
 \quad\text{in}\quad
 W_B=\bigoplus_{\rho=1}^R\ell_1(T_{B,m_\rho}),$$ where $W_B$ carries the sum norm. It suffices to show that $\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{v_C\}$ contains a vector of norm less than $\epsilon$: its convex coefficients give $\nu$, and each individual total-variation distance is at most half the resulting norm.

Fix $\delta>0$ with $R\delta<\epsilon$. A dual functional of norm at most $1$ is a collection $f_\rho:T_{B,m_\rho}\to[-1,1]$. For each $m$, color the $m$-tuples by the list of bins of width at most $\delta$ containing $f_\rho$ for all $\rho$ with $m_\rho=m$. The number of colors is bounded in terms of $R$ and $\delta$, independently of the functions and of $B$. The finite Ramsey theorem therefore supplies a single sufficiently large $B$ such that, for *every* such collection of functions, there is a set $H\subseteq\{1,\ldots,B\}$ of size $|\Lambda|$ homogeneous for all these colorings. Map $\Lambda$ increasingly onto $H$. Then $$\left\lvert \langle f,v_C\rangle\right\rvert
 \leq\sum_{\rho=1}^R
 \left\lvert f_\rho(C(A_\rho))-f_\rho(C(A'_\rho))\right\rvert
 \leq R\delta<\epsilon.$$ If the compact convex set $\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{v_C\}$ had distance at least $\epsilon$ from zero, finite-dimensional separation would give a dual functional of norm $1$ whose value on every $v_C$ is at least $\epsilon$, contradicting the preceding bound. Thus the required convex combination exists. ◻

</details>

<a id="sec:chart-chain"></a>
#### Reversible chart transport

We now turn the averaging lemma into a finite reversible chain.

<a id="stmt-prop-chart-transport"></a>
<div id="prop:chart-transport" class="proposition">

**Proposition 6** (Reversible chart transport). *Let $\Lambda$ be a finite ordered set, and let $T_s:D_s\to R_s$ be order-preserving bijections between its subsets, indexed by a nonempty finite set $\mathcal S$ with a fixed-point-free involution $s\mapsto s^{-1}$ and $T_{s^{-1}}=T_s^{-1}$. For every $\epsilon>0$, there are an integer $B$ and a finite stationary reversible marked chain on charts $C:\Lambda\to\{1,\ldots,B\}$ whose successive edge letters are independent and uniform in $\mathcal S$. At every stationary step $(C,s,C')$, $$C'(a)=C(T_s(a))\quad(a\in D_s)$$ holds simultaneously with probability at least $1-\epsilon$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-prop-chart-transport"><summary>Proof of stmt-prop-chart-transport</summary>

<a id="eq:chart-match"></a>
*Proof.* Apply Lemma <a href="#lem:order-averaging" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:order-averaging">5</a> to obtain $B$ and a chart law $\nu$. The matching condition is $$\label{eq:chart-match}
 C'(a)=C(T_s(a))\quad(a\in D_s).$$ Figure <a href="#fig:chart-transport" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="fig:chart-transport">1</a> depicts the rank equality needed along one transition.

For one representative of each inverse pair, couple two charts with law $\nu$ so that <a href="#eq:chart-match" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:chart-match">[eq:chart-match]</a> holds with probability at least $1-\epsilon$. Such a coupling exists by <a href="#eq:subtuple-TV" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subtuple-TV">[eq:subtuple-TV]</a>. For two laws $\mu,\mu'$ on the same finite set, place mass $\min\{\mu(a),\mu'(a)\}$ at each diagonal pair $(a,a)$ and couple the remaining masses arbitrarily. The matching probability is $1-\operatorname{TV}(\mu,\mu')$. Apply this to the laws of $C(T_s(D_s))$ and $C'(D_s)$, then sample the full charts from their conditional distributions. Denote the resulting coupling by $\lambda_s$. For the inverse index set $$\lambda_{s^{-1}}(C,C')=\lambda_s(C',C).$$ This gives the inverse version of <a href="#eq:chart-match" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:chart-match">[eq:chart-match]</a>, with the same success probability.

Discard charts of zero $\nu$-mass and start $C_0$ with law $\nu$. At each step choose a fresh uniform letter $s\in\mathcal S$, independently of the past. From the current chart $C$, then choose $C'$ with probability $\lambda_s(C,C')/\nu(C)$ and record $(C,s,C')$. The first marginal of $\lambda_s$ is $\nu$, so these conditional probabilities sum to one. Its stationary edge mass is <a id="eq:chart-edge-mass"></a>$$\label{eq:chart-edge-mass}
 \frac{1}{|\mathcal S|}\lambda_s(C,C'),$$ which is invariant under $(C,s,C')^*=(C',s^{-1},C)$. We have therefore constructed a finite stationary reversible marked chain with independent uniform letters. At stationarity, each step fails <a href="#eq:chart-match" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:chart-match">[eq:chart-match]</a> with probability at most $\epsilon$. ◻

<a id="sec:action"></a>
### Two levels from reduced words

We construct a sum of periodic functions along a word of increasing homeomorphisms. For a word with nonempty reduced form, this sum has one value at integer points and another at the integer translates of the image of $0$; the two values differ by the reduced length of the word. These two interlaced families will supply the alternating prefix sums in the walk construction.

Let $\mathcal S=\{u,v,u^{-1},v^{-1}\}$, with positive letters $u,v$ and negative letters $u^{-1},v^{-1}$. A word is *reduced* if it has no adjacent inverse letters. Its reduced form is obtained by scanning from left to right: delete the last retained letter when its inverse arrives, and append the new letter otherwise. Choose intervals $[\alpha_s,\beta_s]\subset(0,1)$, $s\in\mathcal S$, with positive lengths and pairwise disjoint closures, and put $$\mathcal A_s=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb Z}[\alpha_s+j,\beta_s+j).$$

<a id="stmt-lem-action"></a>
<div id="lem:action" class="lemma">

<a id="eq:periodic-action"></a>
<a id="eq:interior"></a>
**Lemma 7**. *There are increasing homeomorphisms $h_s:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ such that <a id="eq:pingpong"></a>$$\begin{aligned}
 h_s(y+1)&=h_s(y)+1, & h_{s^{-1}}&=h_s^{-1},\label{eq:periodic-action}\\
 h_s(\mathbb R\setminus\mathcal A_{s^{-1}})&=\mathcal A_s,
 & |h_s(y)-y|&<2.\label{eq:pingpong}
\end{aligned}$$ For a word $w=s_1\cdots s_k$, write $h_w=h_{s_1}\circ\cdots\circ h_{s_k}$, with $h_\varnothing$ the identity. If $w$ is nonempty and reduced, then $$\label{eq:interior}
 h_w(0)\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal A_{s_1});$$ in particular, $h_w(0)\notin\mathbb Z$.*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-action"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-action</summary>

*Proof.* For positive $s$, define $h_s$ to be affine on the intervals between the following three points, with the indicated values: $$\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 y&\alpha_{s^{-1}}&\beta_{s^{-1}}&\alpha_{s^{-1}}+1\\ \hline
 h_s(y)&\beta_s-1&\alpha_s&\beta_s.
 \end{array}$$ Both slopes are positive. Extend by $h_s(y+1)=h_s(y)+1$, and define $h_{s^{-1}}=h_s^{-1}$. The complement intervals $[\beta_{s^{-1}}+j,\alpha_{s^{-1}}+j+1)$ map exactly onto $[\alpha_s+j,\beta_s+j)$, proving the set identity for positive letters. Taking complements and inverses proves it for negative letters. At the breakpoints $|h_s(y)-y|<2$; the difference is affine between breakpoints and periodic. The same displacement bound holds for inverses.

The point $0$ lies outside all the arc closures. Reading a reduced word from right to left, the first image lies in the interior of the arc of the rightmost letter. At every subsequent step this arc is disjoint from the inverse arc of the next letter, so the image enters the interior of that letter’s arc. This proves <a href="#eq:interior" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:interior">[eq:interior]</a>. ◻

</details>

<a id="eq:chi-cancellation"></a>
<a id="eq:c-word"></a>
Subtracting one for negative letters makes the interval indicator change sign under reversal. Define the periodic integer-valued functions <a id="eq:chi"></a>$$\label{eq:chi}
 \chi_s(y)=\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_s}(y)-\mathbf 1_{\{s\text{ is negative}\}}.$$ The complement identity gives $$\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_{s^{-1}}}(h_s^{-1}(y))=1-\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_s}(y),$$ and hence $$\label{eq:chi-cancellation}
 \chi_{s^{-1}}(h_s^{-1}(y))=-\chi_s(y).$$ For a word $w=s_1\cdots s_k$ and an initial point $y_0=y$, set $$\label{eq:c-word}
 y_i=h_{s_i}^{-1}(y_{i-1}),\qquad
 c_w(y)=\sum_{i=1}^k\chi_{s_i}(y_{i-1}).$$ The final point is $y_k=h_w^{-1}(y)$. Periodicity of the $\chi_s$ and <a href="#eq:periodic-action" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:periodic-action">[eq:periodic-action]</a> give $c_w(y+1)=c_w(y)$. Deleting adjacent inverse letters leaves both the final point and $c_w(y)$ unchanged, by <a href="#eq:chi-cancellation" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:chi-cancellation">[eq:chi-cancellation]</a>.

<a id="stmt-lem-two-levels"></a>
<div id="lem:two-levels" class="lemma">

**Lemma 8** (Two levels). *Let the reduced form of $w$ have length $\ell>0$, with $n_+$ positive letters and $n_-$ negative letters. For every $j\in\mathbb Z$, <a id="eq:two-levels"></a>$$\label{eq:two-levels}
 c_w(j)=-n_-,\qquad c_w(h_w(0)+j)=n_+.$$*

</div>

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-two-levels"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-two-levels</summary>

*Proof.* We may replace $w$ by its reduced form $t_1\cdots t_\ell$. Start first at $j$. Before the first letter the point is outside every arc. If the point before $t_i$ is outside $\mathcal A_{t_i}$, its inverse image under $h_{t_i}$ belongs to $\mathcal A_{t_i^{-1}}$; this is disjoint from $\mathcal A_{t_{i+1}}$ because the word is reduced. Thus every indicator $\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_{t_i}}$ in <a href="#eq:c-word" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:c-word">[eq:c-word]</a> is zero, giving the first equality.

Starting instead at $h_w(0)+j$, the point before $t_i$ is $h_{t_i\cdots t_\ell}(0)+j$. By <a href="#eq:interior" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:interior">[eq:interior]</a> it lies in $\mathcal A_{t_i}$, so every indicator is one. The sum is $\ell-n_-=n_+$. ◻

</details>

For a word with nonempty reduced form, let $z\in(0,1)$ be the fractional part of $h_w(0)$. By periodicity, the two levels occur at the interlaced points $$j<z+j<j+1,\qquad
 c_w(j)=-n_-,\quad c_w(z+j)=n_+\qquad(j\in\mathbb Z).$$ The next construction places these points at fixed coordinate ranks throughout a chart trajectory.

<a id="sec:walks"></a>
### Construction of the finite walks

We now combine chart transport with the two-level cocycle. On matching transitions, moving landmarks keep their chart ranks; their prefix values will then yield a norm lower bound. We finish by checking how often matching transitions and a long reduced word occur together.

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-two-levels-2"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-two-levels</summary>

*Proof of Proposition <a href="#prop:walks" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:walks">3</a>.* Fix a norm $N$ as in the proposition. We first construct labels with a common nonzero norm and normalize them at the end. For $k\geq1$, put $$D_k=N(\underbrace{1,-1,\ldots,1,-1}_{k\text{ pairs}}).$$ These numbers are positive, and spreading invariance and the triangle inequality give $D_{k+l}\leq D_k+D_l$.

<a id="eq:landmarks"></a>
*Step 1: the finite chart chain.* Fix an integer $n\geq1$, and let $$L=8(n+1),\qquad \epsilon=\frac1{10n}.$$ Use the homeomorphisms of Lemma <a href="#lem:action" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:action">7</a>. The landmarks below record the interval endpoints used by labels and all orbit points needed to follow $n$ steps. The integer shifts provide a margin around the label window. Let $\Lambda$ be the following finite subset of $\mathbb R$, in its usual order: $$\label{eq:landmarks}
 \begin{split}
 \Lambda={}&\{\alpha_s+j,\beta_s+j:s\in\mathcal S,\ 0\leq j<L\}\\
 &\quad\cup
 \{h_w(0)+j: |w|\leq n,\ -2n\leq j\leq L+2n\}.
 \end{split}$$ All displayed shifts $j$ are integers, $|w|$ denotes the length before reduction, the empty word is included, and coincident points are counted only once. For each $s$ use the partial order-preserving bijection $$T_s=h_s|_{D_s},\qquad
 D_s=\{a\in\Lambda:h_s(a)\in\Lambda\}.$$ Its inverse is $T_{s^{-1}}$. Proposition <a href="#prop:chart-transport" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:chart-transport">6</a> gives a finite stationary reversible marked chain on charts; denote its stationary chart law by $\nu$. Call a transition $(C,s,C')$ *good* when <a id="eq:good"></a>$$\label{eq:good}
 C'(a)=C(h_s(a))\qquad(a\in D_s).$$ Every step is good with probability at least $1-\epsilon$.

<a id="eq:labels"></a>
<a id="eq:one-step-norm"></a>
For positive $s$, define $$\label{eq:labels}
 g(C,s,C')=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}
     \bigl(b_{C(\alpha_s+j)}-b_{C(\beta_s+j)}\bigr),$$ and define negative labels by oddness: $g(C,s,C')=-g(C',s^{-1},C)$. Strict increase of the charts and spreading invariance imply $$\label{eq:one-step-norm}
 N(g(C,s,C'))=D_L$$ for every transition, good or otherwise.

<a id="eq:rank-pairs"></a>
*Step 2: transport along a good trajectory.* Consider a trajectory $(C_{i-1},s_i,C_i)_{i=1}^n$ all of whose steps are good, and let $w=s_1\cdots s_n$ have reduced length $\ell>0$. Put $$m=\lfloor h_w(0)\rfloor,\qquad z=h_w(0)-m.$$ Since every letter moves every point by less than $2$, $|h_w(0)|<2n$, so $-2n\leq m\leq2n-1$; and <a href="#eq:interior" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:interior">[eq:interior]</a> gives $0<z<1$. For the integers $L/4\leq j\leq3L/4$, define $$\label{eq:rank-pairs}
 r_j=C_0(j),\qquad t_j=C_0(z+j).$$ These points belong to $\Lambda$ by <a href="#eq:landmarks" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:landmarks">[eq:landmarks]</a>, and $r_j<t_j<r_{j+1}$ whenever the indices occur.

<a id="eq:transport-rank"></a>
For either starting point $y_0=j$ or $y_0=z+j$, follow $y_i=h_{s_i}^{-1}(y_{i-1})$. For the first family, $$y_i=h_{s_i^{-1}\cdots s_1^{-1}}(0)+j;$$ for the second, $$y_i=h_{s_{i+1}\cdots s_n}(0)+j-m.$$ The words have length at most $n$, and the shifts lie in $[-2n,L+2n]$. Thus all these points belong to $\Lambda$. Moreover, for every $0\leq i\leq n$, <a id="eq:transport-bounds"></a>$$\label{eq:transport-bounds}
 j-2n<y_i<j+1+2n,$$ and the whole interval lies in $(0,L)$ for the chosen range of $j$. Because $h_{s_i}(y_i)=y_{i-1}$, goodness gives $$\label{eq:transport-rank}
 C_i(y_i)=C_{i-1}(y_{i-1})=C_0(y_0).$$

*Step 3: cumulative coefficient sums.* Let $$G=\sum_{i=1}^n g(C_{i-1},s_i,C_i),\qquad
 F(k)=\sum_{a=1}^k G_a\quad(0\leq k\leq B).$$ Every label has total coefficient sum zero, so $F(0)=F(B)=0$. For landmarks $\alpha<\beta$ and $y$, strict increase of a chart gives $$\sum_{a\leq C(y)}(b_{C(\alpha)}-b_{C(\beta)})_a
 =\mathbf 1_{[\alpha,\beta)}(y).$$ This explains the half-open interval convention. Evaluate $F$ at a transported rank $C_0(y_0)$. For a positive step, <a href="#eq:labels" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:labels">[eq:labels]</a>, <a href="#eq:transport-rank" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:transport-rank">[eq:transport-rank]</a>, and $y_{i-1}\in(0,L)$ therefore give the contribution $\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_{s_i}}(y_{i-1})$. For a negative step the label is read in chart $C_i$. Since $y_i\in(0,L)$ as well, its contribution is $$-\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_{s_i^{-1}}}(y_i)
 =\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A_{s_i}}(y_{i-1})-1.$$ Thus in both cases the contribution is $\chi_{s_i}(y_{i-1})$, and <a id="eq:F-cocycle"></a>$$\label{eq:F-cocycle}
 F(C_0(y_0))=c_w(y_0).$$ Reduction is applied to this scalar expression; $G$ retains all $n$ original edge labels. Let $n_+,n_-$ be the numbers of positive and negative letters in the reduced word. Lemma <a href="#lem:two-levels" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:two-levels">8</a>, applied with the integer shifts $j$ and $j-m$, now gives <a id="eq:F-levels"></a>$$\label{eq:F-levels}
 F(r_j)=V:=-n_-,\qquad F(t_j)=U:=n_+,
 \qquad U-V=\ell.$$

<a id="eq:linear-displacement"></a>
*Step 4: converting oscillations to norm.* Put $a=L/4$ and $b=3L/4$. The selected cuts and their prefix sums determine every consecutive block sum: $$\begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
 \text{cut}&0&r_a&t_a&r_{a+1}&\cdots&t_{b-1}&r_b&B\\
 F&0&V&U&V&\cdots&U&V&0\\
 \text{block sum}& &V&\ell&-\ell&\cdots&\ell&-\ell&-V
\end{array}$$ The last row gives the sum of the block ending at that cut. There are $b-a=L/2$ inner pairs. Replacing every block by its sum is contractive and yields <a id="eq:compressed"></a>$$\label{eq:compressed}
 (V,\underbrace{\ell,-\ell,\ldots,\ell,-\ell}_{L/2\text{ pairs}},-V).$$ The last block is nonempty, since the rank $t_b$ from <a href="#eq:rank-pairs" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:rank-pairs">[eq:rank-pairs]</a> satisfies $r_b<t_b\leq B$. We control the endpoint contribution by combining the estimate from all oscillations with the estimate from a single level. Subtracting the endpoint array by the triangle inequality and using $|V|\leq\ell$ gives $$N(G)\geq\ell D_{L/2}-|V|D_1
       \geq\ell(D_{L/2}-D_1).$$ Independently, partitioning just at an $r_j$ or a $t_j$ gives the arrays $(V,-V)$ and $(U,-U)$. Hence $$N(G)\geq\max\{|V|,U\}D_1\geq\frac\ell2D_1.$$ For nonnegative $A,B$, $\max\{A-B,B/2\}\geq A/3$. Together with $D_L\leq2D_{L/2}$, this proves $$\label{eq:linear-displacement}
 N(G)\geq\frac\ell3D_{L/2}\geq\frac\ell6D_L.$$

*Step 5: probability and normalization.* The probability that at least one transition fails to be good is at most $n\epsilon=1/10$, by stationarity and the union bound. Without conditioning on goodness, the letters are independent and uniform in $\mathcal S$. As letters are appended, the reduced length has conditional expected increment $1/2$ when positive, since exactly one of the four letters cancels its last letter; at zero the increment is $1$. Thus $\mathbb E\ell\geq n/2$. Since $0\leq\ell\leq n$, $$\frac n2\leq\mathbb E\ell
 \leq\frac n4+n\mathbb P\{\ell\geq n/4\},
 \qquad\text{so}\qquad
 \mathbb P\{\ell\geq n/4\}\geq\frac14.$$ With probability at least $1/4-1/10=3/20$, all steps are good and $\ell\geq n/4$. On this event <a href="#eq:linear-displacement" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:linear-displacement">[eq:linear-displacement]</a> gives $N(G)\geq nD_L/24$. Every edge label has norm $D_L>0$ by <a href="#eq:one-step-norm" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:one-step-norm">[eq:one-step-norm]</a>, so dividing all labels by $D_L$ proves the proposition. ◻

</details>

<a id="app:norm"></a>
### Construction of the spreading norm

We prove the ordered-norm lemma used in the reduction. Its spreading-model and subdivision steps follow the approach of Brunel and Sucheston <a id="pm-cite-14"></a>[[BrunelSucheston1974](#ref-BrunelSucheston1974), [BrunelSucheston1975](#ref-BrunelSucheston1975)]; their later work develops the resulting equal-sign-additive basis <a id="pm-cite-15"></a>[[BrunelSucheston](#ref-BrunelSucheston)]. The classical conclusion, recorded in <a id="pm-cite-16"></a>[[Ostrovskii](#ref-Ostrovskii), Theorem 2.2], is that every nonreflexive Banach space finitely represents a Banach space with an equal-sign-additive basis. We give the finite-array construction of the norm properties needed here.

<details id="proof-stmt-lem-two-levels-3"><summary>Proof of stmt-lem-two-levels</summary>

*Proof of Lemma <a href="#lem:ordered-norm" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="lem:ordered-norm">2</a>.* By <a href="#eq:superreflexive-characterization" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:superreflexive-characterization">[eq:superreflexive-characterization]</a>, there is a nonreflexive Banach space $Y$ finitely representable in $X$. Identify $Y$ with its canonical image in $Y^{**}$, and choose $v\in B_{Y^{**}}$ and $d>0$ with $\operatorname{dist}(v,Y)>d$.

The first limit will produce a spreading norm $N_0$, finitely representable in $Y$, that controls coefficient prefix sums. A second limit averages increasingly fine subdivisions. It preserves these properties and makes the resulting norm $N$ invariant under subdivision. Same-sign merging then preserves $N$, and convexity gives contractive merging for arbitrary adjacent coefficients.

<a id="eq:triangle"></a>
*Step 1: finite triangular arrays.* For every integer $M\geq1$, we construct $y_1,\ldots,y_M\in B_Y$ and $f_1,\ldots,f_M\in B_{Y^*}$ such that $$\label{eq:triangle}
 f_i(y_j)=0\quad(j<i),\qquad
 |f_i(y_j)-d|\leq M^{-1}\quad(j\geq i).$$ Suppose $y_1,\ldots,y_{i-1}$ have been chosen and let $E=\operatorname{span}\{y_1,\ldots,y_{i-1}\}$. The restriction map $Y^{**}\to(E^\perp)^*$ is a quotient map with kernel the canonical copy of $E$: Hahn–Banach extends every bounded functional on $E^\perp\subseteq Y^*$ to all of $Y^*$ with the same norm, while finite dimensionality gives $(E^\perp)^\perp=E$. Thus the quotient norm of $v+E$ equals the norm of its restriction to $E^\perp$. Hence $$\left\lVert v|_{E^\perp}\right\rVert=\operatorname{dist}(v,E)\geq\operatorname{dist}(v,Y)>d.$$ We may therefore choose $f_i\in B_{Y^*}\cap E^\perp$ with $v(f_i)=d$, by changing a sign and rescaling if necessary. The finite-dimensional form of Goldstine’s theorem gives $y_i\in B_Y$ with $|f_h(y_i)-v(f_h)|\leq M^{-1}$ for $1\leq h\leq i$. Indeed, $(v(f_1),\ldots,v(f_i))$ belongs to the closure of $\{(f_1(y),\ldots,f_i(y)):y\in B_Y\}$: otherwise a separating linear functional would give $v(f)>\sup_{y\in B_Y}f(y)=\|f\|$ for some $f\in\operatorname{span}\{f_1,\ldots,f_i\}$, contradicting $\|v\|\leq1$. This proves <a href="#eq:triangle" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:triangle">[eq:triangle]</a> inductively.

<a id="eq:finite-prefix"></a>
Read the vectors in the reverse order. For any subtuple $y_{i_1},\ldots,y_{i_m}$ with $i_1>\cdots>i_m$, the functional $f_{i_j}$ annihilates the terms after the $j$th and approximates $d$ on the first $j$ terms. Consequently, $$\label{eq:finite-prefix}
 d\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\left\lvert \sum_{h=1}^j a_h\right\rvert
 -\frac1M\sum_{h=1}^m|a_h|
 \leq\left\lVert \sum_{h=1}^m a_h y_{i_h}\right\rVert
 \leq\sum_{h=1}^m|a_h|.$$

*Step 2: a spreading limit.* Fix $k$. For each $m\leq k$, choose a finite grid in $[-1,1]^m$ with mesh at most $k^{-2}$ in the maximum norm. Color every increasing $m$-subtuple of the reversed array by the list of the norms of its grid combinations, rounded into intervals of length $k^{-1}$. The number of colors is finite and independent of $M$. The finite Ramsey theorem, applied successively for $m=1,\ldots,k$, shows that, for sufficiently large $M=M_k\geq k^3$, there is a tuple $(x_1^{(k)},\ldots,x_k^{(k)})$ on which all these colorings are homogeneous. At each arity, thin a previously homogeneous set; homogeneity from earlier arities persists under this thinning. For fixed $m\leq k$ and $a\in[-1,1]^m$, the norms of the combinations with coefficients $a$ on any two increasing $m$-subtuples differ by at most $$k^{-1}+2mk^{-2}\leq3k^{-1}.$$ The additional error follows from the upper bound in <a href="#eq:finite-prefix" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:finite-prefix">[eq:finite-prefix]</a>.

<a id="eq:N0-bounds"></a>
<a id="eq:nondegenerate"></a>
Taking a diagonal subsequence over all finite rational coefficient tuples, and then using the common $\ell_1$ Lipschitz bound, gives limits $$N_0(a_1,\ldots,a_m)
 =\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\lVert \sum_{i=1}^m a_i x_i^{(k)}\right\rVert.$$ The limits are compatible with appending zeros. The vanishing subtuple oscillations give <a href="#eq:spreading" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:spreading">[eq:spreading]</a> for $N_0$. Passing to the limit in <a href="#eq:finite-prefix" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:finite-prefix">[eq:finite-prefix]</a> yields $$\label{eq:N0-bounds}
 d\max_j\left\lvert \sum_{i=1}^j a_i\right\rvert\leq N_0(a)\leq\sum_i|a_i|.$$ The norm axioms pass to the limit, and the lower bound gives positive definiteness. On each fixed coefficient cube the convergence is uniform, by the common Lipschitz bound and compactness. Moreover, $$\label{eq:nondegenerate}
 N_0(a)\geq\frac d2\max_i|a_i|,$$ since every coefficient is the difference of two successive prefix sums. Thus the maps $a\mapsto\sum_i a_i x_i^{(k)}$ give linear embeddings of every fixed coordinate span into $Y$ with distortions tending to $1$.

<a id="eq:dilation-monotone"></a>
*Step 3: averaging subdivisions.* For positive integers $J_1,\ldots,J_m$, let $F_{\boldsymbol J}a$ be the array obtained by replacing $a_i$ by $J_i$ copies of $a_i/J_i$. Write $F_J$ when all multiplicities equal $J$. If $J_i\leq M_i$ for every $i$, then $$\label{eq:dilation-monotone}
 N_0(F_{\boldsymbol M}a)\leq N_0(F_{\boldsymbol J}a).$$ Indeed, in the $i$th block of length $M_i$, select a uniformly random $J_i$-element subset and place $a_i/J_i$ in those positions, with zeros elsewhere. Every resulting array is obtained from $F_{\boldsymbol J}a$ by inserting zeros, so has the same $N_0$ norm. Its mean is $F_{\boldsymbol M}a$, and convexity proves the claim.

It follows that <a id="eq:N-definition"></a>$$\label{eq:N-definition}
 N(a)=\lim_{J\to\infty}N_0(F_Ja)$$ exists. The bounds in <a href="#eq:N0-bounds" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:N0-bounds">[eq:N0-bounds]</a> survive subdivision, because the original prefix sums still occur at the ends of the blocks. Consequently, for every $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)$, <a id="eq:prefix-bounds"></a>$$\label{eq:prefix-bounds}
 d\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\left\lvert \sum_{i=1}^j a_i\right\rvert
 \leq N(a)\leq\sum_{i=1}^m|a_i|.$$ Spreading invariance and the norm axioms also pass to the limit. The functions $a\mapsto N_0(F_Ja)$ again have the common $\ell_1$ Lipschitz bound, so convergence is uniform on every fixed coefficient cube. The lower bound <a href="#eq:nondegenerate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:nondegenerate">[eq:nondegenerate]</a> also holds for $N$. Fix a source dimension $m$ and $\eta>0$. First choose $J$ so that $F_J$ embeds the $m$-dimensional $N$ span into the $mJ$-dimensional $N_0$ span with distortion less than $1+\eta$. Indeed, uniform additive convergence on the compact $N$-unit sphere, together with <a href="#eq:nondegenerate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:nondegenerate">[eq:nondegenerate]</a>, gives uniform relative error. Approximate this now fixed $N_0$ span in $Y$ with distortion less than $1+\eta$, and then embed its finite-dimensional image into $X$ with distortion less than $1+\eta$. The composite distortion is less than $(1+\eta)^3$; letting $\eta\downarrow0$ proves <span class="upright">(iii)</span>.

<a id="eq:subdivision-invariance"></a>
*Step 4: consecutive summation.* First, $N$ is unchanged by every fixed varying-multiplicity subdivision: $$\label{eq:subdivision-invariance}
 N(F_{\boldsymbol J}a)=N(a).$$ To see this, put $J_- =\min_iJ_i$ and $J_+=\max_iJ_i$. The composite $F_rF_{\boldsymbol J}$ replaces each $a_i$ by $rJ_i$ equal pieces. Thus, for every $r$, $$N_0(F_{rJ_+}a)
 \leq N_0(F_rF_{\boldsymbol J}a)
 \leq N_0(F_{rJ_-}a)$$ by <a href="#eq:dilation-monotone" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:dilation-monotone">[eq:dilation-monotone]</a>; let $r\to\infty$.

If adjacent nonzero coefficients have the same sign and rational ratio, subdivide them into equal pieces. Subdividing their sum into the combined number of pieces gives the identical array. Equation <a href="#eq:subdivision-invariance" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:subdivision-invariance">[eq:subdivision-invariance]</a> shows that merging these coefficients preserves $N$. Continuity extends this statement to every same-sign pair, and spreading invariance handles zeros.

Fix all other coefficients and fix $s\ne0$. The function $$t\longmapsto N(\ldots,t,s-t,\ldots)$$ is convex and is constant, at the merged norm $N(\ldots,s,\ldots)$, on the nondegenerate interval with endpoints $0$ and $s$. A convex function constant on a nondegenerate interval is at least that constant everywhere: a smaller exterior value, together with the farther endpoint, would force the nearer endpoint below that constant. This proves <a href="#eq:merge" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:merge">[eq:merge]</a> when $s\ne0$. Letting $s\to0$ proves it for $s=0$ as well. ◻

</details>
